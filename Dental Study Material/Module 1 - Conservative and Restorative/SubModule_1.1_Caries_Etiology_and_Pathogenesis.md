## Sub-Module 1.1: Caries Etiology and Pathogenesis

### 1. Definition and the Nature of Dental Caries

**DEFINITION — Dental caries (Essentials of Dental Caries):** a localized, chemical dissolution of a tooth surface brought about by metabolic events taking place in the microbial biofilm (plaque) covering the affected area. [1, p.6]

**DEFINITION — Dental caries (Textbook of Preclinical Conservative Dentistry):** a microbiological disease of the hard structure of teeth, which results in localized demineralization of the inorganic portion and destruction of the organic substances of the tooth. [2, p.4]

Both definitions converge on the same core idea — caries is a *biofilm-driven process* , not damage from a single external agent — but frame it slightly differently: source [1] emphasizes the *chemical* dissolution event itself, while source [2] emphasizes the disease as *microbiological* in origin. *Cariology* is defined as the science studying the etiology, histopathology, epidemiology, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of dental caries [2, p.4].

### 2. The Dental Biofilm: Formation and Structure

The oral cavity is an open, constantly colonized system: mucosal and dental surfaces are permanently covered by a salivary protein film to which oral microorganisms — collectively the endogenous flora, or metagenome — adhere according to the surface properties of their cell walls [1, p.14]. Squamous epithelial surfaces (cheek, lip, palate) desquamate continuously and so cannot support a stable biofilm; only the teeth (stable, non-shedding) allow biofilm build-up, concentrated at stagnation areas — approximal spaces, fissures, the gingival crevice, restoration margins [1, p.15].

| Time since clean surface | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–12 hours | Single cocci attach to the salivary pellicle |
| 12–24 hours | Cocci multiply into microcolonies; surface may feel matt/rough to the tongue |
| ~1 week (undisturbed) | Microbial succession + rising species diversity → mature, “climax” biofilm (dental plaque) |
| 48 hours (TEM) | Loose structure, mainly cocci |
| 2 weeks (TEM) | Dense mass of microcolonies of many species, separated by intermicrobial matrix channels |

*Source: [1, p.15-17].*

Clinically important: a mature biofilm behaves very differently from planktonic (free-floating) laboratory bacteria. Chlorhexidine, for example, is highly effective against planktonic organisms in a test tube, but cannot diffuse effectively into the deeper layers of an established biofilm — which is why chlorhexidine mouthwash has only limited clinical effect on caries incidence despite strong *in-vitro* antimicrobial activity [1, p.18]. Biofilm close to the major salivary duct openings (buccal to upper 1st/2nd molars, lingual to lower incisors) can mineralize into dental calculus within weeks, because saliva there is supersaturated with calcium and phosphate [1, p.18].

### 3. Biofilm Metabolism and the Stephan Curve

Biofilm organisms continuously metabolize salivary proteins/glycoproteins, producing minute, continuous pH fluctuations even without dietary sugar [1, p.18-19]; dietary fermentable carbohydrates (glucose, fructose, sucrose) dramatically amplify this.

**DEFINITION — Stephan curve:** plaque pH measured at intervals for 30 minutes after a rinse with 2% sucrose for 1–2 minutes. Curves from sites independently classified as sound, inactively-lesioned, or actively-lesioned show markedly different metabolic responses, reflecting different underlying biofilm ecology at each site. [1, p.19]

The acid is a survival by-product, not a deliberate attack on the tooth: excess unmetabolized sugar can itself kill bacteria (“sugar kill”), so biofilm organisms must ferment available carbohydrate rapidly [1, p.19]. A lesion becomes clinically visible (white spot) only after *many* such pH fluctuations, sustained over months to years, produce a net calcium/phosphate loss sufficient to make enamel visibly porous [1, p.20] — most individual metabolic fluctuations physiologically cancel out; a lesion represents a persistent *drift* , so management should target the biofilm, not just the visible lesion [1, p.20].

### 4. Chemistry of Demineralization — the Critical-pH Concept

- At physiological pH (~7.4), saliva/plaque fluid is supersaturated with respect to hydroxyapatite and fluorapatite — the necessary precondition for tooth mineral to be maintained at all [1, p.20]
- As biofilm pH falls, apatite solubility rises sharply — roughly ten-fold for every 1-unit pH drop [1, p.21]
- At the *critical pH* , fluid becomes undersaturated w.r.t. hydroxyapatite while still supersaturated w.r.t. (less-soluble) fluorapatite → subsurface hydroxyapatite dissolves while fluorhydroxyapatite deposits in the surface enamel layer — the chemical basis of the **subsurface lesion** [1, p.21-22]

Because acid diffuses preferentially along enamel rod (prism) boundaries, a smooth-surface lesion is triangular in cross-section (base at the surface), while a fissure lesion is cone-shaped with its base toward the dentine — which is why occlusal cavities, once opened, are consistently larger at the base than the surface opening suggested, and why a bitewing shadow is wider under the surface than the visible entry point [1, p.25-27]. Once ~30–40% of the enamel lesion's mineral is lost, the tissue is porous/fragile enough to fracture into a cavity — accelerated by mechanical stress such as aggressive probing [1, p.29].

### 5. Remineralization: Mechanism and Real-World Limits

Remineralization requires calcium/phosphate ions to diffuse into the porous subsurface lesion (usually through the relatively intact surface zone) and redeposit onto partially dissolved crystal surfaces; this is described as an *extremely slow* process, and the source explicitly cautions that many marketed “remineralizing” agents are validated only under artificial laboratory conditions that do not reflect the oral cavity [1, p.29-30]. What is observed clinically as “remineralization” is predominantly surface abrasion + slow mineral redeposition onto exposed crystal remnants — *not* restoration of the lesion body — and a truly arrested lesion generally cannot be reversed further except by mechanical polishing/acid microabrasion [1, p.31]. This is exactly why lesion *activity* assessment (Sub-Module 1.2) is clinically central: only an *active* lesion can still respond to caries-control measures [1, p.29].

Erosion is mechanistically distinct: it results from direct dissolution by extrinsic/intrinsic acids unrelated to biofilm stagnation, etches away successive whole crystal layers, and — unlike an active caries lesion, which retains partially demineralized crystals able to accept redeposited mineral — cannot remineralize even under prolonged exposure to supersaturated saliva [1, p.30-31].

**DEFINITION — White Spot Lesion:** the first sign of a carious lesion visible to the naked eye — a white, opaque change in enamel appearance caused by subsurface demineralization. The source explicitly warns against a common terminology confusion: white spot lesions are also called “early,” “initial,” or “incipient” lesions, but a white spot lesion can persist for years in an arrested (non-progressing) state, so labelling it “early” is not necessarily accurate — “any white spot lesion is not [necessarily] incipient.” Multiple active white spot/carious lesions occurring simultaneously in one patient, often on surfaces not usually affected by caries, is termed rampant caries. [1, p.12]

### 6. Progression into Dentine and the Pulp Response

Dentine demineralization is detectable histologically well before enamel cavitation, spreading in the direction dictated by dentinal tubule orientation [1, p.32]. It is typically preceded by *translucent dentine* (tubular sclerosis) — the vital odontoblast/pulp complex attempting to occlude tubules with mineral and reduce permeability — with deeper *tertiary dentine* laid down by corresponding odontoblasts as a further protective response, attempting to wall off the pulp [1, p.32].

Once cavitated, the cavity fills with retained microbial mass that, beyond continued acid production, exerts proteolytic activity degrading partially demineralized dentine collagen — the soft, sticky, yellow-brown carious dentine removed during excavation [1, p.32-33]. Although microorganisms do penetrate individual tubules, it is the *bulk* microbial mass in the cavity (the “biomass”), not the advancing tubular bacteria, that is the principal acid-producing fabric — clinically relevant to how much carious dentine truly needs removing at the advancing front [1, p.33].

| Pain type | Clinical pattern | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Reversible pulpitis | Provoked by stimulus (hot/cold/sweet), resolves promptly on removal of stimulus | Pulp inflammation still reversible |
| Irreversible pulpitis | Spontaneous, persistent, characteristically worse at night (supine → increased cranial blood flow) | “Crucial” differential — without endodontic treatment, may progress to necrosis + periapical infection/abscess |

*Source: [1, p.34-35]. Mechanism: ordinary connective-tissue inflammation, made distinctively painful in the pulp because the tissue is encased in rigid dentine and cannot swell to accommodate the vascular response.*

### 7. The Role of Mutans Streptococci — the Ecological Plaque Hypothesis

**Historical view:** 1960s hamster/rat studies (animals infected with certain streptococci + fed a frequent-sucrose diet developed extensive fissure caries, apparently transmissible to litter-mates) drove decades of “caries is an infectious, transmissible disease” thinking — later shown to be confounded by rodent coprophagy rather than true infection [1, p.35].

**Evidence against a single-organism model** [1, p.35-36]:

- Mutans streptococci are part of the normal resident flora, present in LOW numbers even in most caries-free individuals
- No consistent association between *S. mutans* presence/counts and either approximal caries experience or plaque pH response in individual children
- Subgrouping by salivary mutans counts does not reliably distinguish high- vs. low-caries-experience groups; high counts are often found at sites with NO demineralization
- Populations have shown dramatic caries declines over short periods with NO corresponding change in population salivary mutans levels
- A thorough literature review concludes no single microbial species can be identified as the primary cause of enamel OR root caries

**DEFINITION — Ecological plaque hypothesis:** biofilm conditions at a given site select for a locally-adapted mix of organisms, rather than a single pathogen “causing” disease. In a neutral, balanced ecosystem, acidogenic/aciduric organisms (mutans streptococci, lactobacilli) form only a small, weakly competitive fraction of the biomass; introducing them experimentally into a balanced ecosystem does not, by itself, produce caries. Rising sugar-intake frequency shifts biofilm ecology toward these organisms at protected sites, producing a self-reinforcing cycle of falling pH → further selection for acid-tolerant flora → net mineral loss. [1, p.36-37]

Conclusion of the source: caries should be understood as *“a biofilm-induced disease—the biofilm being composed of the resident microflora which is in a dynamic equilibrium with the oral environment”* , not an infectious/transmissible disease of one organism — so control should target the biofilm as a whole plus sugar-frequency reduction, not elimination of a single species [1, p.37].

### 8. Genetic Predisposition and Caries Risk — Two Textbook Perspectives

**This is a genuinely contested area across the two standardised sources used, and both positions are given in full below rather than resolved artificially.**

#### 8.1 Host Genetic Susceptibility: “No Evidence” (Essentials of Dental Caries)

Discussing race, ethnicity, and heredity directly, the source states there is **“no evidence to support inheritance as a factor explaining the disparity in caries prevalence or incidence between different racial or ethnic groups”** [1, p.177]. Familial clustering of caries (“my mother/father also had bad teeth”) is instead attributed to *shared environment* : families share dietary practices and oral hygiene behaviours, and these two factors alone are considered sufficient to explain the pattern [1, p.177-178]. The clearer, better-supported relationship in this source is socioeconomic: poverty status and educational attainment correlate strongly with caries experience, consistent with the general pattern that poorer, less-educated populations experience worse health outcomes broadly [1, p.177]. Within-population variation can be very large — an 8-fold difference in mean DMFS was recorded across 267 Danish municipalities in 15-year-olds in 2003 — illustrating that local socioeconomic/behavioural variation dwarfs any population-level genetic signal [1, p.178].

#### 8.2 Genetic/Biotechnological Approaches to Caries Prevention (Textbook of Preclinical Conservative Dentistry)

A different textbook, covering caries prevention research rather than epidemiology, presents genetics as a live *intervention* strategy rather than a settled non-factor:

- Genetically modified *S. mutans* strains have been created lacking the *lactodehydrogenase* gene, rendering them unable to produce lactic acid [2, p.71]
- Genetically modified *Lactobacillus zeae* has been engineered to produce antibodies that attach to the *S. mutans* surface, causing its death [2, p.71]
- Genetically modified foods are being researched for use as “probiotics” in high-caries-risk patients [2, p.71]
- A caries vaccine strategy is proposed: immunization with *S. mutans* antigens to induce an immune response preventing colonization, ideally administered before eruption of the deciduous dentition for maximum benefit [2, p.71]

#### 8.3 Reconciling the Two: What AAPD Adds

A 2025 AAPD best-practice document (used more fully in Sub-Module 1.3) lists “genome-level risk factors” explicitly among the limitations of current caries-risk-factor models, stating they “may account for substantial variations in caries risk” that existing risk-assessment tools do not capture [3, p.327] — i.e., the most recent authoritative position sits *between* the two textbook views: host genetics is acknowledged as a plausible, currently under-measured contributor to individual variation, while population-level racial/ethnic disparities remain best explained by socioeconomic and behavioural factors rather than heredity. **Practical takeaway:** don't present genetics as either a non-factor or a primary cause — it is an area of genuine, acknowledged scientific uncertainty, while shared-environment and socioeconomic explanations for *population-level* disparities are comparatively well-supported.

### 9. Root Surface Caries

Can only develop once cementum/root dentine is exposed to the oral environment via gingival recession; follows the same demineralization sequence as enamel caries [1, p.37-38]. Provided the covering biofilm is mechanically removed (toothbrushing + fluoride toothpaste), even an accessible cavitated root lesion can be arrested — the softened yellow-brown surface gradually hardens, darkens, and over months becomes hard, shiny, almost black — explicit evidence against restoring such lesions operatively while they remain hygiene-controllable [1, p.38].

### 10. The Role of Saliva

| Salivary function | Mechanism / data point |
|---|---|
| Buffering | Bicarbonate rises with stimulated flow; salivary pH varies 6.0–7.5, most alkaline when stimulated [1, p.39] |
| Clearance | Salivary film only ~100 µm thick; slowest movement in the upper labial sulcus; limited mixing between left/right streams either side of the tongue — relevant when applying fluoride unilaterally [1, p.40] |
| Mineral supersaturation | Precondition for enamel stability at physiological pH [1, p.20, p.39-40] |
| Flow near-zero overnight | Explains dry-mouth-on-waking and why night-time is peak caries vulnerability [1, p.39] |

A simple water rinse after a sugary meal does *not* clear fermentable sugar from within the biofilm (sucrose diffuses rapidly from the thin salivary film into the much thicker adjacent biofilm along a steep concentration gradient) — explicitly called “a useless way to attempt to control caries”; mechanical toothbrushing is required to disturb the biofilm itself [1, p.40]. Chewing (e.g., sugar-cane chewing studied in Kenyan children) substantially increases both flow and buffering, blunting the expected Stephan-curve pH drop despite high sugar exposure [1, p.40]. Hyposalivation removes clearance, buffering, and mineral-supply functions simultaneously, allowing cariogenic organisms to overgrow — patients with reduced salivary flow are described as among the most challenging to manage for caries control [1, p.41].

### 11. The Role of Sugars

**DEFINITION — Free sugars:** mono- and disaccharides added to food/drink by the consumer, cook, or manufacturer, plus sugars naturally released from fruit during juicing/blending — as distinct from intrinsic sugars (naturally within the cellular structure of whole foods), which are not considered harmful. [1, p.41-42]

A controlled experiment (dental students refraining from oral hygiene, rinsing 9x daily for 2 min with 2% sucrose) produced early active white-spot lesions within weeks, regressing once hygiene/fluoride resumed — but the *rate* of lesion development varied considerably between individual students on an identical sugar challenge, attributed to inter-individual variation in saliva composition and biofilm formation rate, explicitly rejecting the old idea of intrinsically “stronger” enamel [1, p.43-44]. Archaeological evidence (a 2000-year-old Roman tooth with caries; a stone-drilled tooth from a 5000 BC skull) shows caries has accompanied human diets throughout history; what changed is that modern, soft, industrially processed diets outpace the natural occlusal/approximal attrition that once kept lesion progression in check [1, p.44-45].

WHO guideline: restrict free-sugar intake to 5% of total daily energy — roughly 6 teaspoons (25 g)/day for an average adult, an amount exceeded by a single 330 mL can of regular soft drink (~26 g) [1, p.101].

### 12. Contemporary Microbiome Perspective: Biofilm Ecology and Dysbiosis (DCNA Supplementary)

A 2019 DCNA review extends the ecological framework with modern genomic evidence: the human mouth hosts over 800 bacterial species across multiple niches, roughly one-third never isolated in pure culture [4, p.583]. Oral commensals thrive at a resting biofilm pH of ~6.0–7.0; caries arises from **dysbiosis** of this normally symbiotic community, driven by sugar-frequent, acidic-pH environmental change, favouring an acidogenic/aciduric microflora enriched for *S. mutans* , other acid-tolerant streptococci, and *Lactobacillus* — directly consistent with the ecological plaque hypothesis in Section 7 [4, p.584].

Spatial imaging (CLASI-FISH) + metagenomic sequencing shows defined biofilm micro-architecture: *Corynebacterium* filaments act as structural anchor points, anaerobic taxa localize toward the interior while aerobes stay near the periphery, and producers/consumers of key metabolites (e.g., lactate) are positioned adjacent to each other [4, p.584]. Interspecies interaction is mediated in part through mechanisms such as **coaggregation** (direct adhesion between different bacterial species/strains), which underlies probiotic strategies aiming to physically displace cariogenic organisms [4, p.587]. *Note:* the specific term “quorum sensing” (chemical signalling molecules coordinating bacterial gene expression at a population level) does not appear in the standardised textbook or DCNA sources used for this document; coaggregation and biofilm micro-architecture, as documented above, are the closest sourced equivalents for interspecies communication in this material.

### 13. Caries Epidemiology and the “Polarization” Phenomenon (Sturdevant's)

Global caries prevalence rose sharply through the modern era, strongly associated with dietary change, but began declining in many developed countries from the late 1970s/early 1980s — a decline attributed mainly to fluoridation of public water supplies (from the 1950s-60s) and fluoride toothpaste, which by 1984 had reached 94% of toothpaste products in the US market. [5, p.67]

This decline was NOT evenly distributed: it was most prominent among upper/middle socioeconomic classes, while lower-socioeconomic and rural populations retained higher disease prevalence — a phenomenon termed “polarization” of caries, where a shrinking segment of the population bears a growing share of total disease burden. The US NHANES III survey quantified this precisely: 80% of caries occurred in just 20% of children, disproportionately from lower socioeconomic groups or minorities. A parallel global pattern is occurring: caries prevalence is declining in developed countries, increasing in less-developed countries, and reaching epidemic proportions in countries with emerging economies undergoing rapid dietary transition. [5, p.67]

This polarization finding directly reinforces why population-level prevention alone is insufficient and why individual caries-risk stratification (already covered in Sub-Module 1.3) matters clinically — a small, identifiable high-risk subgroup, not the general population, is where most disease and most preventable harm is concentrated.

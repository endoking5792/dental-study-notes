## Q9. Discuss the differential diagnosis of pain originating from and around the teeth. [10 marks]

### A. STUDY MODE

The weakest point in tooth-pain diagnosis is assuming that the tooth the patient points to is necessarily the source of pain. Referred pain and overlapping clinical features can make localization unreliable, so diagnosis must integrate history, examination, pulp testing, periodontal assessment, occlusal examination and imaging. [Certain]

#### 1. Basic concept

Differential diagnosis is the systematic comparison of plausible causes of the patient's complaint until the diagnosis that best explains the complete clinical picture is identified. [Certain]

Pain around a tooth may originate from the pulp, periodontal ligament, periodontium, restorative structures, cracked or fractured tooth structure, adjacent teeth, maxillary sinus, muscles of mastication, temporomandibular structures, neural tissues or other non-odontogenic sources. [Certain]

#### 2. Major odontogenic causes

| Source | Typical clues | Useful tests |
|---|---|---|
| Dentin/pulp | Cold or sweet sensitivity; lingering thermal pain; spontaneous pain in advanced pulpal inflammation. | Cold, heat, EPT, selective anesthesia, radiographs. |
| Periradicular tissues | Pain on biting or percussion; better localization when acute periradicular inflammation is present. | Percussion, palpation, periodontal probing, radiographs. |
| Periodontal | Localized periodontal pocket, gingival inflammation, bleeding or periodontal abscess features. | Probing, mobility, percussion, radiographs. |
| Cracked/fractured tooth | Pain on chewing or release of biting pressure, often with cold sensitivity and vague localization. | Tooth Slooth/bite test, transillumination, dye, magnification, probing, radiographs/CBCT when indicated. |
| Occlusal/restorative | Pain related to heavy contacts, restoration fracture or recurrent caries. | Occlusal analysis, restoration examination, radiographs. |

#### 3. Important non-odontogenic possibilities

Myofascial pain from trigger points in muscles of mastication can refer pain to teeth. [Certain]

Sinus or nasal mucosal inflammation can cause pain perceived in several maxillary posterior teeth, often with sinus pressure or positional aggravation. [Certain]

Neurovascular pain, neuropathic pain and other neural disorders may mimic toothache. [Certain]

Cardiac pain and systemic disorders can occasionally be perceived as dental pain. [Certain]

Temporomandibular disorders, otologic disease and other regional disorders can create pain perceived near the dentition. [Certain]

#### 4. Diagnostic sequence

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW] Differential diagnosis of tooth pain

What it should show: Patient complaint -> detailed pain history -> clinical examination -> pulp and periradicular tests -> periodontal and occlusal assessment -> radiographic examination -> selective adjunctive tests -> diagnosis and treatment plan.

Essential labels: Onset, duration, provocation, spontaneous pain, thermal response, biting, percussion, palpation, probing, pulp tests, radiographic findings.

Purpose/relevance: Shows the examiner that diagnosis is an integrated process rather than a single-test decision.

### B. EXAM MODE

Introduction: Toothache may arise from the dental tissues or may be referred from structures outside the tooth. Accurate diagnosis therefore depends on correlating the patient's history with objective clinical and radiographic findings rather than treating the tooth indicated by the patient alone. [Certain]

#### 1. Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Pain pattern / findings | Diagnostic pointers |
|---|---|---|
| Reversible pulpal inflammation | Brief, stimulus-dependent thermal pain that resolves after stimulus removal. | Cold/thermal testing with control teeth; identify and remove irritant. |
| Irreversible pulpal inflammation | Lingering thermal pain, spontaneous pain or poorly localized pain may occur. | Thermal tests, EPT and correlation with history. |
| Acute apical inflammation | Pain on biting/percussion and usually better localization than pulpal pain. | Percussion, palpation and radiographic assessment. |
| Acute apical abscess | Pain with swelling and tenderness; drainage may be present. | Palpation, percussion, pulp testing, sinus-tract tracing and radiography. |
| Periodontal abscess | Localized periodontal pain and swelling with a periodontal defect. | Probing and periodontal examination are critical. |
| Cracked tooth | Pain on chewing or release, often with cold sensitivity; localization may be difficult. | Bite test, transillumination, dye, magnification and probing. |
| Sinus-related pain | Several maxillary teeth may be tender; pain may vary with head position or sinus pressure. | Sinus history/examination and appropriate imaging. |
| Myofascial pain | Pain may be referred to several teeth and reproduced from muscle trigger points. | Palpate muscles of mastication and assess TMD features. |
| Neuropathic pain | Burning, electric, shooting or persistent pain; dental findings may be inadequate. | Neurologic history and examination; exclude odontogenic disease before referral. |

#### 2. Principles that prevent misdiagnosis

Test the suspected tooth and appropriate control teeth because individual responses vary. [Certain]

Use more than one diagnostic test and require concordance between history, examination and imaging. [Certain]

Do not initiate irreversible treatment when the diagnosis remains uncertain after appropriate noninvasive testing. [Certain]

Consider referred pain when several teeth are involved, the dental cause is inadequate, or treatment fails to alter the pain. [Certain]

#### Conclusion

The differential diagnosis of tooth pain is an exercise in localization and exclusion. The clinician should identify the pain pattern, establish whether the source is odontogenic, and then determine the pulpal, periradicular, periodontal, fracture-related or non-odontogenic diagnosis before treatment. [Certain]

#### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

Do not equate the site of perceived pain with the site of pathology. [Certain]

Pulpal pain is often poorly localized, whereas acute periradicular pain is generally easier to localize. [Certain]

Use control teeth and multiple complementary tests. [Certain]

A bite test and transillumination are particularly useful when fracture is suspected. [Certain]

Sinus, myofascial and neural pain can mimic odontogenic pain. [Certain]

Never perform irreversible treatment on an unresolved diagnosis. [Certain]

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW] One-line diagnostic algorithm

What it should show: History -> clinical tests -> pulp/periradicular/periodontal assessment -> imaging -> adjunctive tests -> diagnosis.

Essential labels: Do not treat before diagnosis.

Purpose/relevance: Fast exam flowchart.

## Q10. Discuss non-odontogenic pain and its differentiation from odontogenic pain. [10 marks]

### A. STUDY MODE

Non-odontogenic toothache is pain perceived in a tooth or dentoalveolar region when the primary pathologic source is outside the dental structures. Cohen's Pathways emphasizes that the perceived site of pain may differ from the source because of referred or heterotopic pain mechanisms. [Certain]

#### 1. Sources of non-odontogenic toothache

| Category | Examples / characteristic clues |
|---|---|
| Somatic and musculoskeletal | Myofascial pain and TMD; pain may be reproduced by palpating trigger points. |
| Sinus/nasal | Maxillary posterior tooth pain with sinus pressure, nasal symptoms or positional aggravation. |
| Neurovascular | Headache disorders with craniofacial pain that may be perceived in teeth. |
| Neuropathic | Burning, shooting, electric or persistent pain; sensory abnormalities may coexist. |
| Distant referred | Cardiac and other distant visceral sources may occasionally be perceived in teeth. |
| Psychogenic/somatoform | Consider only after reasonable exclusion of organic odontogenic and non-odontogenic disorders. |
| Systemic | Systemic disorders may produce referred pain or alter dental tissues and thereby create true odontogenic disease. |

#### 2. Odontogenic versus non-odontogenic pain

| Feature | Odontogenic pain | Non-odontogenic pain |
|---|---|---|
| Local cause | Usually identifiable caries, leakage, trauma, fracture or pulpal/periradicular disease. | Often no adequate dental etiologic factor. |
| Distribution | Often unilateral; source may become localized with periradicular involvement. | May involve multiple teeth, bilateral regions or diffuse areas. |
| Quality | Often dull, aching or throbbing; diagnosis-specific thermal/mechanical provocation may occur. | May be burning, electric, shooting, stabbing or persistent dull pain. |
| Provocation | Often reproducible by dental stimulus such as cold, heat, biting or percussion. | May relate to headache, muscle palpation, exercise, emotional stress or head position. |
| Local anesthesia | Appropriate pulpal/periradicular pain generally decreases when the responsible region is adequately anesthetized. | Pain may not be consistently abolished by anesthetizing the suspected tooth. |
| Dental treatment | Appropriate treatment should improve the complaint when the diagnosis is correct. | Persistent pain despite reasonable dental treatment raises suspicion of another source. |

#### 3. Practical differentiation

Take a complete pain history and identify onset, duration, triggers, spontaneous episodes, intensity, location and relieving factors. [Certain]

Establish whether there is a credible local dental cause. [Certain]

Perform pulp, periradicular, periodontal and fracture testing with comparison to control teeth. [Certain]

Assess muscles, TMJ and relevant extraoral structures when dental findings do not explain the complaint. [Certain]

Use local anesthesia selectively as a diagnostic aid, recognizing its limitations. [Certain]

If findings remain inconsistent, stop irreversible treatment and refer to an appropriate orofacial pain, neurologic, ENT or medical service. [Certain]

### B. EXAM MODE

Definition: Non-odontogenic toothache is pain perceived in the tooth or dentoalveolar region that originates from a non-dental source or is referred from another anatomic structure. [Certain]

#### Clinical features suggesting non-odontogenic pain

No convincing local dental cause such as caries, defective restoration, trauma or fracture. [Certain]

Multiple or bilateral painful teeth. [Certain]

Persistent or recurrent pain despite reasonable dental treatment. [Certain]

Burning, electric, shooting, stabbing or otherwise atypical pain quality. [Certain]

Pain associated with headache, muscle trigger points, exercise, emotional stress or head position. [Certain]

No consistent relief after appropriate local anesthesia. [Certain]

#### Diagnostic approach

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW] Odontogenic versus non-odontogenic decision tree

What it should show: Adequate dental cause? -> Yes: localize and classify odontogenic disease. -> No: reassess history, muscles/TMJ, sinus, neurologic and systemic sources -> targeted referral.

Essential labels: Dental cause, pulp tests, percussion, probing, fracture tests, imaging, local anesthesia, extraoral examination.

Purpose/relevance: Demonstrates safe diagnostic reasoning and prevents unnecessary endodontic treatment.

Conclusion: The key distinction is not a single symptom but the coherence of the entire diagnostic dataset. Non-odontogenic pain should be suspected when the dental findings are inadequate, the pain pattern is atypical, or appropriate dental treatment fails to alter the complaint. [Certain]

#### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

The tooth may be the site of perceived pain without being the source. [Certain]

Multiple teeth, bilateral pain and absence of a local cause are warning signs. [Certain]

Burning or electric pain should broaden the differential. [Certain]

Myofascial and sinus pain are common dental mimics. [Certain]

Do not repeat endodontic treatment merely because pain persists. [Certain]

Primary source trail: Nixdorf, Law and Mattscheck (2021), as chapter authors, Cohen's Pathways of the Pulp, 12th ed., South Asia Edition, Chapter 4, “Diagnosis of the Nonodontogenic Toothache,” pp. 115-138. [Certain]

Primary source trail: Okeson (2012), as chapter author, Seltzer and Bender's Dental Pulp, 2nd ed., Chapter 36, “Odontogenic Toothache (Pulpal and Periradicular Pain),” and Chapter 19, “Differential Diagnosis of Toothache: Odontogenic Versus Nonodontogenic Pain,” pp. 798-834. [Certain]

## Q11. Write a note on the test cavity technique in endodontic diagnosis. [10 marks]

### A. STUDY MODE

The test cavity is an invasive sensibility test in which a cavity is cut without local anesthesia to determine whether the patient perceives dentinal stimulation. [Certain]

#### 1. Rationale

Historically, the test cavity was used when routine pulp sensibility tests failed to provide a clear answer, particularly when restorations or other factors complicated testing. [Certain]

#### 2. Technique

Select the suspected tooth only after a complete history and noninvasive diagnostic assessment. [Certain]

Isolate and dry the tooth sufficiently to avoid false responses. [Likely]

Begin cavity preparation without local anesthesia in an area where preparation is otherwise indicated or where minimal damage is anticipated. [Certain]

Observe whether dentin stimulation produces a characteristic patient response. [Certain]

If a vital response is obtained, administer anesthesia and complete the planned restorative procedure. [Certain]

#### 3. Limitations and modern interpretation

Ingle's 7th edition takes a strongly negative position. Abbott explains that the test cavity only establishes whether the pulp can respond to a stimulus and does not distinguish normal pulp, reversible pulpitis or irreversible pulpitis. [Certain]

A negative response is also non-specific because it cannot reliably distinguish necrotic pulp, a pulpless infected tooth or a root-filled tooth. [Certain]

Anxiety can produce false-positive pain responses, and a calcified canal with a viable pulp can produce a false-negative response. [Certain]

The procedure unnecessarily damages tooth structure or an existing restoration and may create a new restoration requirement. [Certain]

#### 4. Important textbook disagreement

| Source | Position | Exam interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Ingle's Endodontics 7th ed., Abbott | Test cavity is invasive, non-specific and has no place in modern dentistry when better noninvasive tests are available. | Mention that it is obsolete as a routine diagnostic test. |
| Summitt's Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry 4th ed. | Describes test preparation as an additional test when thermal and electric tests are inconclusive and restorative treatment is otherwise indicated. | Acknowledge historical/operative use, but emphasize selective use only. |

### B. EXAM MODE

Definition: A test cavity is an invasive pulp sensibility test in which dentin is cut without local anesthesia and the patient's response is assessed. [Certain]

#### Indications

Historically used when cold and electric pulp tests are inconclusive. [Certain]

More defensible when the tooth is already indicated for restorative preparation, rather than creating an otherwise unnecessary cavity. [Likely]

#### Procedure

Complete history, examination and routine pulp tests first. [Certain]

Use control teeth and interpret the result in the context of the entire diagnostic picture. [Certain]

Prepare through enamel into dentin without anesthesia. [Certain]

Pain/sensitivity indicates that the tooth can respond to the stimulus, but does not by itself establish the exact pulpal diagnosis. [Certain]

If needed, anesthetize and proceed with the indicated treatment. [Certain]

#### Disadvantages

Invasive and destructive. [Certain]

May damage an existing restoration. [Certain]

May cause apprehension and false-positive responses. [Certain]

False-negative responses may occur in calcified canals. [Certain]

Does not provide a specific pulpal diagnosis. [Certain]

Better noninvasive tests are generally available. [Certain]

#### Conclusion

The test cavity has historical diagnostic value but should not be used as a reflex test. In the contemporary diagnostic sequence, it is inferior to a careful history, comparative sensibility tests, clinical examination and appropriate imaging, and irreversible treatment should be avoided when the diagnosis remains uncertain. [Certain]

#### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

Test cavity = cutting dentin without local anesthesia. [Certain]

It is a sensibility test, not a true vitality test. [Certain]

Ingle 7th edition strongly rejects routine use. [Certain]

Pain response does not distinguish normal pulp from different forms of pulpitis. [Certain]

No response is also non-specific. [Certain]

Essential labels: No anesthesia; dentin stimulus; control tooth; non-specific result.

Purpose/relevance: Simple exam diagram.

## Q12. Write a note on Tooth Slooth and its role in the diagnosis of cracked/fractured teeth. [10 marks]

### A. STUDY MODE

Tooth Slooth is a commercially available biting instrument used to reproduce pain associated with cracked or fractured tooth structure and to localize the affected cusp. [Certain]

#### 1. Why it is useful

Cracked teeth are diagnostically difficult because pain may be intermittent, poorly localized and absent from routine radiographs. [Certain]

The bite test can reproduce the patient's characteristic pain and can help identify both the tooth and the specific cusp or segment involved. [Certain]

#### 2. Principle

The broad base of the Tooth Slooth rests on multiple occlusal contacts while the small pyramidal tip is placed over the suspected cusp. [Certain]

The patient bites on the tip, and the clinician assesses pain during biting and especially during release of pressure. [Certain]

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW] Tooth Slooth bite test

What it should show: Tooth Slooth with broad base supported by adjacent occlusal surfaces and pyramidal tip contacting the suspected cusp.

Essential labels: Broad base, pyramidal tip, suspected cusp, direction of biting force.

Purpose/relevance: Shows the diagnostic principle and is easy to reproduce in an examination.

#### 3. Interpretation

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Pain reproduced on one cusp | Strongly raises suspicion that the cusp or associated crack is responsible. |
| Pain on release of pressure | Characteristic of cracked tooth symptoms and should prompt fracture-focused examination. |
| No pain | Does not completely exclude a crack; symptoms may be intermittent. |
| Multiple positive teeth | Requires broader differential diagnosis and control testing. |

#### 4. Complete fracture diagnosis

Use the bite test with visual inspection, transillumination, dye, magnification and periodontal probing. [Certain]

Existing restorations may need to be removed if they obscure fracture lines. [Certain]

Radiographs may fail to demonstrate coronal cracks because the fracture plane is often not aligned with the x-ray beam. [Certain]

CBCT may assist in selected cases, particularly when a vertical root fracture or associated bone defect is suspected, but its diagnostic accuracy for VRF is not uniformly reliable. [Certain]

### B. EXAM MODE

Tooth Slooth is a diagnostic biting instrument used to localize pain arising from cracked or fractured teeth. [Certain]

#### Uses

Reproduces pain by controlled biting on individual cusps. [Certain]

Helps localize the symptomatic tooth. [Certain]

Helps identify the affected cusp. [Certain]

Complements transillumination, dye, magnification, probing and radiographic assessment. [Certain]

#### Technique

Dry and inspect the tooth under good illumination. [Certain]

Place the Tooth Slooth tip on one cusp at a time. [Certain]

Ask the patient to bite firmly but within comfort limits. [Likely]

Release pressure and ask whether the characteristic pain is reproduced. [Certain]

Repeat on adjacent cusps and control teeth for comparison. [Certain]

#### Advantages

Simple, chairside and inexpensive relative to advanced imaging. [Likely]

Can localize a symptomatic cusp when radiographs are non-diagnostic. [Certain]

Can reproduce the patient's chief complaint, which strengthens diagnostic confidence. [Certain]

#### Limitations

A negative test does not exclude an asymptomatic or intermittently symptomatic crack. [Certain]

Pain can have other causes, so a positive bite test should not be interpreted in isolation. [Certain]

A complete diagnostic work-up is required before irreversible treatment. [Certain]

#### Conclusion

Tooth Slooth is particularly valuable because it converts an otherwise vague complaint of pain on chewing into a controlled, tooth- and cusp-specific stimulus. It should be used as one component of a structured fracture diagnosis rather than as a stand-alone test. [Certain]

#### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

Tooth Slooth is a bite test instrument. [Certain]

Test one cusp at a time. [Certain]

Pain on biting or release supports a crack-related diagnosis. [Certain]

Transillumination and dye should be used as complementary tests. [Certain]

Routine radiographs may miss coronal cracks. [Certain]

Primary source trail: Bakland (2019), Ingle's Endodontics, 7th ed., Chapter 13, “Crown-Originating Dental Fractures,” pp. 391-401. ToothSlooth is specifically listed among recommended biting applicators. [Certain]

Primary source trail: Nixdorf, Law and Mattscheck (2021), Cohen's Pathways of the Pulp, 12th ed., South Asia Edition, Chapter 22, “Chronic Fractures and Cracks,” figure and text around the Tooth Slooth bite test and transillumination. [Certain]

Primary source trail: Hilton and Summitt et al. (2013), Summitt's Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, 4th ed., fracture diagnosis section. The source identifies transillumination and the biting test as the two most useful tests for cracked-tooth diagnosis. [Certain]

## Q13. Discuss diagnostic aids in endodontics, with special reference to the diagnosis of fractured teeth. [100 marks]

### A. STUDY MODE

A fracture diagnosis is not made by one test. The strongest diagnostic approach integrates the patient's symptom pattern, visual examination, pulp and periradicular testing, periodontal probing, bite testing, transillumination, dyes, magnification, radiography and selective CBCT. [Certain]

#### 1. Objectives of endodontic diagnosis

Identify the tooth responsible for the complaint. [Certain]

Determine the pulpal and root canal status. [Certain]

Determine the periapical/periradicular status. [Certain]

Identify the cause of disease or pain. [Certain]

Assess restorability and prognosis before irreversible treatment. [Certain]

#### 2. Diagnostic aids: complete framework

| Diagnostic aid | Main information obtained | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| History and pain analysis | Onset, duration, triggers, spontaneous pain, thermal pattern, biting, localization and relieving factors. | Patient description is subjective. |
| Visual examination | Caries, discoloration, swelling, cracks, fracture lines and restoration defects. | Small cracks can be hidden by restorations or difficult to see. |
| Magnification and illumination | Improved visualization of fracture lines, canal anatomy and subtle defects. | Magnification alone is not a guaranteed fracture detector. |
| Percussion | Periodontal ligament tenderness and periradicular inflammation. | Positive response may occur with sinus disease or occlusal trauma. |
| Palpation | Apical tenderness or swelling. | Usually indicates inflammation but does not establish cause alone. |
| Periodontal probing | Localized deep pocket, attachment pattern and possible fracture communication. | A normal probing pattern does not exclude all fractures. |
| Mobility | Loss of periodontal support or fracture-related mobility. | Non-specific. |
| Cold test | Sensibility response to cold. | Tests neural response, not direct blood flow. |
| Heat test | Heat-provoked response when clinically indicated. | Less commonly required and less standardized. |
| Electric pulp test | Ability of sensory fibers to respond to electrical stimulus. | Cannot by itself establish pulp histologic status. |
| Selective anesthesia | Localization of pain to a region. | Anesthetic spread can involve adjacent teeth. |
| Test cavity | Response to dentinal stimulation. | Invasive and non-specific. |
| Bite test / Tooth Slooth | Reproduction and localization of crack-related pain. | Negative test does not exclude an intermittent crack. |
| Transillumination | Interruption of light transmission by fracture line. | Requires appropriate access for light penetration. |
| Dyes | Highlight fracture lines and infractions. | May stain other defects and requires careful interpretation. |
| Periapical radiography | Root, periapical and gross fracture-related changes. | Fracture visibility depends on beam orientation and anatomy. |
| Angulated radiographs | Additional information about fracture/resorption orientation. | Still two-dimensional. |
| CBCT | Three-dimensional anatomy, bone defects and selected fracture findings. | Artifacts, especially in root-filled teeth, can reduce diagnostic accuracy. |
| Pulp blood-flow tests | Directer assessment of vascular status using methods such as LDF or pulse oximetry. | Limited availability, technique sensitivity and validation issues. |
| Exploratory surgery | Direct visualization when noninvasive findings remain inadequate and surgery is otherwise justified. | Invasive and should not be indiscriminate. |

#### 3. Pulp and periradicular diagnostic tests

Ingle emphasizes that pulpal and periapical diagnosis should not rely on a single diagnostic test or one isolated piece of information. [Certain]

Cold and electric pulp tests are sensibility tests and should be interpreted against the patient's history and control teeth. [Certain]

Percussion assesses periodontal ligament tenderness rather than pulp vitality. [Certain]

Palpation helps identify apical inflammation or swelling. [Certain]

Periodontal probing is particularly important when a fracture or combined endodontic-periodontal lesion is suspected. [Certain]

#### 4. Selective anesthesia

Selective anesthesia can help localize chronic or poorly localized pain, but anesthetic spread through bone can affect adjacent teeth, particularly with maxillary infiltration. [Certain]

Ingle therefore considers local anesthetic testing imperfect and recommends exhausting noninvasive diagnostic information first. [Certain]

#### 5. Test cavity

The test cavity is an invasive sensibility test and is now strongly questioned by Abbott because its results are non-specific and it creates unnecessary structural damage. [Certain]

This is an important source disagreement because Summitt's operative text describes test preparation as an additional option when conventional tests are inconclusive and restorative preparation is otherwise indicated. [Certain]

#### 6. Fracture classification

| Fracture category | Origin / direction | Typical clinical significance |
|---|---|---|
| Craze line / infraction | Superficial crown enamel crack. | Often asymptomatic; may represent a potential cleavage plane. |
| Fractured cusp | Cusp segment separates from a crown-originating fracture. | Often pain on biting; restorability depends on depth and pulpal status. |
| Cracked tooth | Incomplete crown-originating fracture extending apically to variable depth. | Pain on chewing and thermal sensitivity are common. |
| Split tooth | Complete fracture with separated segments. | Usually poor restorability. |
| Vertical root fracture | Root-originating longitudinal fracture, often in root-filled teeth. | Frequently associated with localized periodontal breakdown and poor prognosis. |
| Trauma-related fracture | Acute injury affecting crown, root or both. | Management depends on fracture level, pulpal status and restorability. |

Ingle groups dental fractures under three broad categories: crown-originating fractures, vertical root fractures and trauma-related fractures. [Certain]

#### 7. Diagnosis of crown-originating fractures

Symptomatic crown-originating fractures commonly present with pain on chewing, sometimes accompanied by cold or sweet sensitivity. [Certain]

A fracture should be suspected when symptoms are not adequately explained by caries, periodontal disease or recent trauma. [Certain]

#### 8. Visual examination

Inspect all surfaces under good illumination and magnification. [Certain]

Look for craze lines, fracture lines crossing marginal ridges or extending gingivally. [Certain]

Remove a restoration when it is obscuring the suspected fracture and when removal is justified diagnostically. [Certain]

Dye such as methylene blue or red dye can enhance visualization of fracture lines. [Certain]

#### 9. Transillumination

Transillumination is particularly useful for crown-originating cracks because a fracture line interrupts the passage of light through tooth structure. [Certain]

When light encounters a fracture plane, the structure beyond the crack appears relatively dark because the beam is redirected or blocked. [Certain]

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW] Transillumination of a cracked tooth

What it should show: Light entering from facial or lingual side, passing through intact tooth structure and being interrupted at the crack.

Essential labels: Light source, intact enamel/dentin, fracture line, dark segment beyond fracture.

Purpose/relevance: High-yield and easy to reproduce.

#### 10. Biting test and Tooth Slooth

The biting test is one of the most useful clinical procedures for localizing symptomatic crown fractures. [Certain]

Use Tooth Slooth, rubber wheel, cotton applicator, cotton roll or another controlled biting aid. [Certain]

Test individual cusps sequentially. [Certain]

Pain during biting or release of pressure supports a crack-related diagnosis. [Certain]

#### 11. Periodontal probing

A localized deep probing defect may indicate that a fracture has extended to the root surface or that a vertical root fracture is present. [Certain]

Probing findings must be interpreted with the clinical history, pulp status and radiographic findings. [Certain]

#### 12. Radiography

Conventional radiographs may fail to show a crown-originating crack because the fracture is thin and its orientation may not intersect the x-ray beam in a favorable direction. [Certain]

Vertical root fractures are also difficult to visualize unless the x-ray beam is appropriately aligned with the fracture plane. [Certain]

Multiple horizontal angulations can improve diagnostic information. [Certain]

#### 13. CBCT in fracture diagnosis

CBCT provides three-dimensional imaging and may demonstrate fracture-associated bone loss, root morphology and selected fracture patterns. [Certain]

However, Ingle reports that CBCT accuracy for vertical root fracture is controversial, particularly in root-filled teeth because beam-hardening and streak artifacts can obscure or mimic fracture lines. [Certain]

Therefore, CBCT is an adjunct and should not replace clinical examination. [Certain]

#### 14. Magnification and microscope

Enhanced illumination and magnification improve visualization of subtle cracks, although magnification alone is not sufficiently predictable for all fractures. [Certain]

The combination of magnification, illumination and dye can markedly improve identification of infractions. [Certain]

#### 15. Pulp blood-flow methods

Pulse oximetry estimates hemoglobin oxygen saturation and can provide information related to pulpal blood flow. [Certain]

Laser Doppler flowmetry measures changes in red blood cell flux and has been investigated as a noninvasive vitality method, particularly in immature or traumatized teeth. [Certain]

Routine use remains limited by equipment, probe design, technical difficulty and cost. [Certain]

#### 16. Exploratory surgery

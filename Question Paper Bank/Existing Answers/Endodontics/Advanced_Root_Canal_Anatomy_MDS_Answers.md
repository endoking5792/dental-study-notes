## 1. Discuss radix entomolaris and its endodontic significance. [25 marks]

### STUDY MODE

#### 1. Definition and terminology

Radix entomolaris (RE) is an additional root located distolingually in a mandibular molar, most characteristically the mandibular first molar. The corresponding additional root on the mesiobuccal side is termed radix paramolaris. The clinical problem is not simply the presence of an extra root, but the presence of an additional canal with an independent or partially joined pathway that may be missed, inadequately shaped, or instrumented in an unsafe direction.[1,2]

Ingle’s Endodontics identifies a third root, usually distolingual, in mandibular first molars and also records much rarer complex combinations involving radix entomolaris and radix paramolaris.[1]

#### 2. Developmental basis and morphology

The additional root is considered a developmental anatomical variation associated with abnormal development of the root sheath, with genetic and population-related influences proposed in the literature.[1,2] The root may range from a relatively short, conical root with a simple canal to a long, narrow and markedly curved root with a complex canal configuration.

Three-dimensional anatomy is clinically important because a distolingual root may be superimposed on the distal root on a conventional periapical radiograph. The apparent number of roots therefore may underestimate the true anatomy.[1,2]

#### 3. Prevalence and population considerations

The prevalence of RE varies substantially among populations and according to methodology. In mandibular first molars, a contemporary multinational CBCT study found an overall RE prevalence of approximately 3%, with substantial variation between countries.[3] In mandibular second molars, a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 21,383 teeth reported a pooled global RE prevalence of 0.72%, showing that the anomaly is less common in second than first molars.[4]

These values should not be memorized as a universal fixed prevalence because prevalence depends on tooth group, ancestry, sampling method and imaging technique.[3,4]

#### 4. Classification of radix entomolaris

A practical description considers the root morphology and curvature rather than treating every RE as anatomically identical. The root may be small and separate, fused coronally with the distal root, or distinctly independent. Curvature may be predominantly coronal, apical, or occur in more than one plane.

| Feature | Clinical description | Endodontic implication |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Additional distolingual root | Always search for an additional canal when anatomy suggests RE |
| Origin | May arise as a distinct root or from a fused root complex | Orifice position may be displaced lingually |
| Curvature | Straight to severe; may involve buccolingual and mesiodistal planes | Increases risk of ledge, transportation and file separation |
| Canal size | Often smaller than principal distal canals | Requires fine negotiation and conservative enlargement |
| Apical anatomy | May have separate foramen or merge with another distal canal | Working length and apical preparation must be determined individually |

#### 5. Identification

Identification begins before access preparation. The clinician should inspect the external crown and root contour, assess the number and outline of roots on the preoperative radiograph, and look for an unusual distal or distolingual root outline.[1,2]

Radiographic methods include periapical radiographs with different horizontal angulations. In mandibular molars, a change in projection can separate superimposed roots and reveal an additional distolingual root.[1] A sudden disappearance or narrowing of the distal canal, an eccentric canal trajectory, or an unexplained radiolucent root outline should increase suspicion.

CBCT can reveal three-dimensional root number, root curvature and canal configuration when conventional radiographs are inconclusive and when the additional information is expected to influence treatment. CBCT should be selected case by case rather than as a routine substitute for conventional radiography because radiation exposure and artifacts remain relevant.[1,5]

#### 6. Access cavity and orifice location

Access must provide adequate visualization without unnecessary removal of tooth structure. The distolingual orifice should be actively sought when the external root outline or chamber anatomy suggests a third root. Troughing, if required, should be controlled and performed under magnification with ultrasonic tips rather than aggressive bur removal.

The key anatomical warning is that the RE canal may begin from an orifice positioned distolingually and then curve sharply. The initial file should therefore be small and precurved, with tactile exploration before rotary instrumentation.[1]

#### 7. Negotiation and biomechanical preparation

Establish straight-line access to the orifice while preserving pericervical dentin.

Use a small precurved stainless-steel hand file, commonly a #6, #8 or #10 K-file, to negotiate the canal.

Use copious irrigation and a chelating agent where necessary to facilitate negotiation.

Establish a reproducible glide path before introducing rotary or reciprocating NiTi instruments.

Determine working length with an electronic apex locator and confirm radiographically when indicated.

Use a flexible NiTi system and a conservative taper appropriate to the canal diameter and curvature.

Avoid forcing instruments apically, especially where a sharp coronal or apical curvature exists.

Reconfirm patency and canal trajectory frequently and irrigate between instruments.

The objective is controlled cleaning and shaping while maintaining the original canal path. Excessive enlargement of a thin, curved RE root risks transportation, strip perforation or excessive dentin removal.[1,6,7]

#### 8. Irrigation and obturation

Mechanical instrumentation cannot contact every surface of a complex root canal. Chemical irrigation therefore remains essential. Sodium hypochlorite provides tissue dissolution and antimicrobial activity, while EDTA is used to remove the inorganic component of the smear layer after shaping. Irrigant activation may improve fluid exchange in inaccessible regions, but activation should supplement rather than replace mechanical and chemical preparation.[1,8]

Obturation should reproduce the prepared canal without forcing filling material beyond the apical terminus. Warm techniques can be useful in irregular anatomy, but the choice depends on the preparation, apical control and operator proficiency.

#### 9. Endodontic significance

| Problem | Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Missed canal | RE not recognized preoperatively | Persistent infection and treatment failure |
| Severe curvature | Narrow, curved canal | Ledge, transportation, perforation, instrument separation |
| Superimposition | RE overlaps distal root on 2-D radiograph | Underdiagnosis |
| Thin dentin | Small root and deep concavities | Strip perforation or weakening |
| Complex apical anatomy | Separate or joining foramina | Incomplete cleaning or inaccurate working length |

The most important examination principle is: an extra root is clinically important only because it represents an additional endodontic space that must be identified, negotiated, disinfected, shaped and obturated safely.

#### 10. Recent advances

CBCT has strengthened preoperative recognition of additional roots by providing three-dimensional information that conventional periapical imaging cannot provide consistently.[3-5] Contemporary NiTi systems use heat treatment and altered alloy processing to improve flexibility and resistance to cyclic fatigue, which is relevant in narrow curved canals.[6,7] A Dental Materials Journal study demonstrated that curvature angle and radius materially influence cyclic fatigue behavior and that modern heat-treated alloys can improve fatigue resistance, supporting the use of metallurgy as part of instrument selection in difficult anatomy.[6]

The evidence does not justify claiming that any particular modern file system is universally superior. Performance is dependent on alloy, geometry, cross-section, motion, speed, canal curvature and clinical technique.[6,7]

### EXAM MODE

Radix entomolaris is an additional distolingual root, usually associated with the mandibular first molar. Its importance lies in the associated additional canal, which may be missed because of root superimposition on conventional radiographs and because the canal is frequently narrow and curved.[1-3]

#### Definition and morphology

The mandibular first molar normally has two roots, mesial and distal, but an additional distolingual root may occur as radix entomolaris. Radix paramolaris refers to an additional mesiobuccal root.[1] The additional root may be short or long, separate or partially fused, and may exhibit significant curvature.

#### Identification

Study the preoperative periapical radiograph carefully for an unusual distal root outline or root contour.

Take additional radiographs with altered horizontal angulation when root superimposition is suspected.

Inspect the chamber floor for an additional distolingual canal orifice.

Use magnification and illumination during access.

Use CBCT selectively when conventional imaging is insufficient and the additional information will alter management.[3-5]

#### Management

Prepare conservative but adequate access and locate the distolingual orifice.

Negotiate initially with a small precurved hand file.

Use EDTA when calcification or narrowing limits negotiation and establish a reproducible glide path.

Confirm working length with an apex locator and appropriate radiographic assessment.

Use flexible NiTi instrumentation with controlled motion and adequate irrigation.

Avoid forcing instruments through sharp curvatures and avoid excessive apical taper.

Irrigate copiously and consider activation to improve fluid exchange in inaccessible areas.

Obturate after adequate disinfection and maintain a three-dimensional seal.

#### Clinical significance

Failure to recognize RE can produce a missed canal, persistent intraradicular infection and treatment failure. Severe curvature increases the risks of ledging, transportation, perforation and instrument separation. Therefore, diagnosis before instrumentation is the central principle of RE management.

#### High-yield examination points

RE = additional distolingual root; RP = additional mesiobuccal root.[1]

Think of RE whenever the distal root outline is unusual or three roots are suggested radiographically.[1,2]

Two or more horizontal radiographic angulations can reduce superimposition.[1]

CBCT is an adjunct, not a routine replacement for periapical radiography.[5]

Small precurved hand file + glide path precedes NiTi preparation.

Severe curvature increases cyclic fatigue risk, making instrument metallurgy and conservative preparation important.[6,7]

#### Diagrams worth drawing in the examination

Mandibular first molar showing normal two roots versus three-rooted RE anatomy.

Occlusal access diagram showing mesial, distal and distolingual orifices.

Curved RE canal with precurved file and glide-path concept.

#### Common examination mistakes

Confusing radix entomolaris with radix paramolaris.

Writing prevalence as a single universal number.

Treating the third root as if it were a straight conventional distal canal.

Using CBCT as a routine investigation without justification.

Failing to mention preoperative angled radiographs and magnification.

## 2. Discuss the anatomy, identification and management of C-shaped root canals. [25 marks]

### STUDY MODE

#### 1. Definition and developmental anatomy

A C-shaped canal is a root canal configuration in which the canal space has a continuous or near-continuous C-shaped cross-sectional form, commonly associated with fusion of roots and a longitudinal developmental groove. Cooke and Cox introduced the term C-shaped canal; Ingle’s Endodontics describes the configuration as arising from fusion of the mesial and distal roots with a deep radicular groove.[1,9]

The classic tooth is the mandibular second molar, although C-shaped anatomy can occur in other teeth, including mandibular premolars and maxillary molars.[1,9]

#### 2. Prevalence

Prevalence is strongly influenced by tooth type and population. A CBCT systematic review and meta-analysis of 25,445 teeth found a pooled C-shaped prevalence of approximately 12% in mandibular second molars, while the prevalence was much lower in mandibular first molars.[10] East Asian populations showed particularly high prevalence in mandibular second molars.[10]

Ingle’s Endodontics reports a broad historical range for mandibular second molars because different anatomical studies and diagnostic criteria were used.[1] Therefore, the examiner should emphasize variation rather than quote a single number without context.

#### 3. Anatomical characteristics

Root fusion may produce a single conical or fused-root outline with a longitudinal developmental groove.[1,9]

The canal may be continuous, semi-continuous, or divided into separate canals at different levels.

Thin fins and isthmuses connect larger canal spaces.

The cross-sectional configuration changes from coronal to apical levels.

Deep external grooves may correspond to thin internal dentin, creating a perforation risk.

Canal bifurcation may occur at almost any level between chamber and apex.[1,9]

| Level | Possible appearance | Clinical concern |
|---|---|---|
| Coronal | C-shaped continuous lumen or large crescentic space | Difficult orientation; large irregular recesses |
| Middle | Semicolon, separate canals with isthmus | Debris retention and incomplete mechanical contact |
| Apical | Separate canals, narrow C-shaped lumen or merged exits | Difficult cleaning and obturation |

#### 4. Classification

Melton et al. described three major categories that remain useful for examination writing.[1,9]

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| C1 | Continuous C-shaped canal from chamber toward apex |
| C2 | Semicolon-shaped canal; a portion is separated by dentin |
| C3 | Two or more distinct canals separated by dentin with a C-shaped orifice pattern |

Fan and colleagues later proposed a five-category cross-sectional classification based on the amount and distribution of dentin and the shape of the canal space. The five-category system is more descriptive of axial morphology, while the Melton system is simpler and more commonly reproduced in examination answers.[9]

#### 5. Identification

#### A. Preoperative identification

Inspect the radiograph for a fused or conical root with a longitudinal outline suggesting absence of a normal furcation.

Look for a long pulp chamber and indistinct chamber floor.

Observe whether canals can be followed separately in the apical third.

Take radiographs at different horizontal angulations.

Use CBCT selectively where the three-dimensional anatomy is uncertain or treatment planning is likely to change.[1,10]

Ingle’s Endodontics highlights four radiographic clues: a conical root with an apparent furcation, an unusually long pulp chamber, an indistinct apical canal pattern, and poorly defined apical foramina.[1]

#### B. Clinical identification

After access, the chamber floor may show a continuous groove connecting the canal orifices. A DG-16 explorer, small precurved K-files and magnification can identify the actual canal pathway. Dye staining and ultrasonic troughing can improve visualization of developmental grooves and hidden orifices.[1,9]

#### 6. Management

#### Access

Access should provide sufficient visualization of the C-shaped system while avoiding unnecessary dentin removal. The access design must permit exploration of the entire groove and allow instruments to enter separate components of the canal.

#### Negotiation

Explore the entire groove with small precurved hand files.

Establish individual pathways where separate canals are present.

Determine working length for each identifiable pathway.

Avoid assuming that a single canal trajectory continues to the apex.

#### Biomechanical preparation

The central management problem is that the thin fins and isthmuses are not reliably contacted by rotary instruments. Simple enlargement of the main lumen can remove excessive dentin from already thin walls and may cause stripping or perforation.[1,9]

Use conservative mechanical preparation.

Use flexible NiTi instruments only after safe manual negotiation and glide-path establishment.

Irrigate frequently with sodium hypochlorite.

Use EDTA for smear-layer control after mechanical shaping.

Use sonic or ultrasonic activation as an adjunct for irrigant penetration and debris disruption, while recognizing that activation does not guarantee complete removal from every fin or isthmus.[8]

#### Obturation

Irregular fins and connecting spaces may make adaptation difficult with conventional cold lateral compaction. Thermoplasticized gutta-percha can improve adaptation to irregular canal walls, although the final choice depends on the prepared anatomy and operator skill.[1,9]

#### Restoration

Thin dentin between the canal system and external root surface must be considered during post space preparation and restorative planning. Aggressive post preparation may perforate or weaken the root.[1]

### EXAM MODE

C-shaped root canal anatomy is an important anatomical variation, most commonly encountered in mandibular second molars. It results from root fusion and formation of a longitudinal developmental groove, producing a canal system with fins, isthmuses and changing cross-sectional morphology.[1,9]

#### Anatomy and classification

The canal may be continuous from chamber to apex, partly separated by dentin, or divided into two or more distinct canals. Melton’s classification is commonly written as C1, C2 and C3.[1,9]

| Type | Key feature |
|---|---|
| C1 | Continuous C-shaped lumen |
| C2 | Semicolon-shaped lumen |
| C3 | Two or more separate canals |

#### Identification

Preoperative radiograph: fused/conical root, long chamber, indistinct floor and unclear apical canal anatomy.

Take angled radiographs when required.

Inspect the chamber floor carefully after access.

Use a small precurved K-file to trace the groove and identify separate pathways.

Use magnification, illumination and ultrasonic troughing where necessary.

Use CBCT selectively for complex anatomy when the information is expected to alter treatment.[1,10]

#### Management

Create conservative access with adequate visualization.

Negotiate all identifiable canal components separately.

Establish working length and glide paths.

Prepare conservatively because fins and isthmuses may have thin dentin.

Use copious NaOCl irrigation and EDTA for smear-layer control.

Use sonic/ultrasonic activation as an adjunct.

Avoid aggressive enlargement of the groove because stripping and perforation are major risks.[1,8,9]

Use a thermoplasticized obturation approach when the irregular anatomy makes adaptation difficult.

#### High-yield examination points

C-shaped anatomy is most characteristic of mandibular second molars.[1,9]

Think in three dimensions because the canal configuration changes from coronal to apical levels.

Melton: C1 continuous, C2 semicolon, C3 separate canals.[9]

Thin fins and isthmuses are the main biological and mechanical challenge.

Simple enlargement is dangerous because external grooves may correspond to thin dentin.[1]

CBCT can clarify complex anatomy but should be justified case by case.[5,10]

#### Diagrams worth drawing in the examination

Mandibular second molar with fused roots and longitudinal external groove.

Axial cross-sections showing C1, C2 and C3 patterns.

Three-dimensional C-shaped canal showing fins and isthmuses.

Conservative preparation with irrigant activation and thermoplastic obturation.

#### Common examination mistakes

Treating C-shaped canals as a single round canal.

Failing to describe the changing morphology along the root.

Using aggressive troughing or enlargement.

Confusing C-shaped canal classification with Vertucci classification.

Claiming that ultrasonic activation completely cleans all fins and isthmuses.

Martins et al. International Endodontic Journal CBCT systematic review/meta-analysis.[10]

Cohen’s Pathways of the Pulp for contemporary cleaning, shaping and ultrasonic principles.[2,8]

## 3. Describe the methods used to identify and estimate root canal curvature. [50 marks]

### STUDY MODE

#### 1. Introduction

Root canal curvature is a three-dimensional anatomical property that influences access, working length, instrument selection, shaping strategy, fatigue risk and the probability of procedural errors. The weakest part of any curvature assessment based on a single radiograph is that a three-dimensional curve is being represented in two dimensions.[11]

A systematic and critical review identified numerous methods for measuring curvature and found no universal consensus on a single method. Two-dimensional methods commonly measure curvature angle, while three-dimensional approaches can additionally characterize radius, height, length and shape.[11]

#### 2. Why curvature must be assessed

Predicts difficulty of negotiation and shaping.

Influences the risk of ledging, transportation, perforation and instrument separation.

Helps determine instrument flexibility and taper requirements.

Assists working-length interpretation and access modification.

Allows standardized selection of canals in laboratory studies.[11]

#### 3. Clinical identification

#### A. Preoperative radiography

Take a high-quality periapical radiograph with minimal distortion.

Assess curvature in the mesiodistal plane.

Take a second radiograph with altered horizontal angulation when a buccolingual component is suspected.

Observe abrupt changes in canal trajectory, narrowing, disappearance, or superimposition.

A radiograph should be treated as a projection, not a complete three-dimensional map. Maxillary molars and roots with superimposition particularly benefit from altered horizontal angulation.[1]

#### B. Tactile identification

A small precurved stainless-steel K-file can provide information about the direction and abruptness of curvature. The file may encounter resistance in the direction of the curve, and tactile control can supplement radiographic information during negotiation. Tactile sensation cannot quantify curvature reliably and should not replace imaging.[1,2]

#### C. CBCT

CBCT can reveal curvature in planes that are hidden on conventional periapical images. It can demonstrate the level of curvature, direction, double curvature and root thickness. Its use should be justified by expected diagnostic or treatment-planning benefit rather than routine application.[5,11]

CBCT itself is not free of limitations. Beam-hardening and volumetric distortion can influence assessment of root canal anatomy and filling quality, particularly in complex roots.[12]

#### D. Micro-CT

Micro-CT provides high-resolution three-dimensional characterization and is particularly valuable in research. It can quantify curvature and complex anatomy without destructive sectioning, but it is not a routine clinical diagnostic modality.[11]

#### 4. Quantitative methods

#### A. Schneider method

The Schneider method is one of the classic two-dimensional methods. A line is drawn along the long axis of the coronal portion of the canal, and a second line follows the apical canal segment to the apical foramen. The acute angle between these lines represents the curvature angle.[13]

| Schneider angle | Traditional description | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| <5° | Straight | Low curvature |
| 5° to 10° | Moderately curved | Intermediate difficulty |
| >10° | Severely curved | Greater instrumentation difficulty |

The categories are useful for examination purposes but should not be interpreted as absolute biological thresholds because curvature severity is also determined by radius, length and three-dimensional direction.[11,13]

#### B. Weine method

The Weine approach uses the canal path and a reference line through the coronal and apical portions to estimate curvature. It differs geometrically from Schneider and may yield a different angle for the same canal. Comparative studies demonstrate that different methods can produce statistically different curvature values.[13]

#### C. Long-axis technique

The long-axis technique uses the tooth or root long axis as a reference. It can be useful where the canal curvature is assessed relative to the external root morphology.[13]

#### D. Pruett method

Pruett et al. emphasized that curvature angle alone does not fully characterize difficulty and introduced a method incorporating both angle and radius of curvature. The radius is measured around the most abrupt curved segment. A smaller radius represents a sharper curve and is generally more demanding for NiTi instruments than a broad curve with the same angle.[14]

#### E. Curvature height and distance

Curvature height describes the maximum perpendicular deviation from a reference line, while curvature distance describes the longitudinal distance over which the curve develops. These parameters help distinguish a long gentle curve from a short abrupt curve.[11,13]

#### F. Canal access angle

Günday et al. evaluated the canal access angle together with canal length, curvature height and curvature distance. Their results demonstrated that different curvature measurement techniques yield different angles and suggested that access-angle characteristics may provide additional information about instrumentation difficulty.[13]

#### 5. Three-dimensional curvature assessment

Modern imaging permits analysis in multiple planes. A 3-D assessment may include:

Curvature angle in more than one plane.

Radius of curvature.

Level of maximum curvature.

Curvature height.

Curvature length.

Direction of curvature.

Double or S-shaped curvature.

Cross-sectional dimensions and remaining dentin thickness.

Hartmann et al. found that published studies used both 2-D and 3-D approaches but no consensus exists on a single clinically universal measurement system.[11]

#### 6. Practical clinical algorithm

Start with a preoperative periapical radiograph.

Assess the canal in the apparent mesiodistal plane.

Take a second horizontal projection if the canal path or root outline is unclear.

Assess root thickness and external root concavities.

Use CBCT selectively when complex three-dimensional curvature will change treatment planning.

During negotiation, use a small precurved file to determine tactile direction and canal trajectory.

Classify the curve qualitatively as straight, moderate, severe, abrupt, double or S-shaped.

Where quantitative comparison is required, state the measurement method because Schneider, Weine and other methods are not interchangeable.[11,13]

#### 7. Relationship between curvature and instrument behavior

Curved canals impose tensile and compressive stresses on rotating NiTi instruments, concentrating stress around the point of curvature.[15] Cyclic fatigue is influenced not only by angle but also by radius. A small radius produces a sharper curvature and can substantially increase fatigue stress.[6,15]

A Dental Materials Journal study comparing NiTi instruments in artificial canals demonstrated that curvature angle and radius affected cyclic fatigue performance and that heat-treated alloy technologies improved fatigue resistance in several experimental conditions.[6]

### EXAM MODE

Root canal curvature should be assessed before and during treatment because it determines the difficulty of negotiation, shaping and instrument control. No single measurement method completely represents the three-dimensional curvature of a root canal.[11]

#### Methods of identification

| Method | Main use | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Periapical radiograph | Initial assessment | 2-D projection |
| Angled radiographs | Separates superimposed roots/canals | Still 2-D |
| Tactile file assessment | Direction and negotiability | Subjective; nonquantitative |
| CBCT | 3-D clinical assessment | Radiation, artifacts, cost |
| Micro-CT | Research 3-D characterization | Not routine clinical imaging |

#### Methods of measurement

Schneider method: angle between the coronal canal axis and apical canal segment.[13]

Weine method: alternative geometric measurement of canal curvature.[13]

Long-axis method: curvature related to the long axis of the tooth/root.[13]

Pruett method: incorporates angle and radius of curvature.[14]

Curvature height and distance: characterize the amplitude and longitudinal extent of the curve.[11,13]

3-D CBCT/micro-CT methods: assess curvature in multiple planes and can identify double curvatures.[11]

#### Clinical interpretation

A high angle is important, but a small radius may be even more clinically significant because it creates a sharper bend. Therefore, curvature should be described as a combination of angle, radius, length, level and direction rather than by angle alone.[11,14]

#### Recent evidence

A 2019 International Endodontic Journal systematic review identified 31 studies and concluded that multiple curvature measurement methods exist without consensus on a single standard.[11] Contemporary research increasingly uses CBCT and micro-CT to characterize three-dimensional curvature, while NiTi research incorporates radius and double-curvature models when assessing cyclic fatigue.[6,11]

#### High-yield examination points

Schneider measures angle; Pruett adds radius as a critical parameter.[11,14]

Same canal can yield different angles with different measurement methods.[13]

Small radius = sharper curvature and greater instrument stress.[6,15]

2-D radiography cannot fully represent 3-D curvature.

CBCT is selective; micro-CT is mainly research based.

Always report the method when giving a numerical curvature angle.

#### Diagrams worth drawing in the examination

Schneider angle construction.

Pruett angle and radius diagram.

Comparison of large-radius gentle curve versus small-radius abrupt curve.

Double/S-shaped canal with two curvature points.

Clinical algorithm for preoperative curvature assessment.

#### Common examination mistakes

Calling a canal severe solely because the angle is high without considering radius.

Comparing curvature values from different methods as if they were identical.

Calling CBCT mandatory for every curved canal.

Using micro-CT as a clinical investigation.

Failing to mention double curvature and three-dimensional direction.

Chi et al., Dental Materials Journal study on curvature/radius and NiTi cyclic fatigue.[6]

## 4. Discuss the management of curved root canals. [50 marks]

### STUDY MODE

#### 1. Introduction

Curved root canals are challenging because instruments tend to straighten within the canal. The resulting forces can cause canal transportation, ledging, zipping, perforation, loss of working length and instrument separation. The aim is not to eliminate the curvature but to clean and shape the canal while maintaining its original trajectory.[1,2,15]

#### 2. Preoperative assessment

Assess root number and morphology on periapical radiographs.

Use altered horizontal angulation where superimposition is present.

Assess angle, radius, length, level and direction of curvature.

Identify double or S-shaped curves.

Consider CBCT selectively in complex anatomy.

#### 3. Access cavity

Adequate access reduces instrument deflection and binding. Straight-line access should be obtained to the extent compatible with preservation of sound tooth structure. Excessive coronal removal can weaken the tooth, whereas inadequate access increases instrument stress and procedural error.[1,2]

#### 4. Negotiation and glide path

The most important mechanical principle is establishing a reproducible glide path before rotary enlargement. Small precurved stainless-steel hand files are useful because they can negotiate the original canal trajectory and bypass minor irregularities.[1,2]

Use #6, #8 and #10 K-files as required.

Precurve the apical few millimeters.

Use watch-winding and balanced tactile movements rather than forcing apically.

Use EDTA to reduce binding in narrow canals.

Confirm reproducible working length before larger instruments.

Mechanical glide-path instruments can be useful after the canal has been safely negotiated. Contemporary studies support their ability to create glide paths in curved canals, but they do not eliminate the need for manual negotiation and a reproducible initial pathway.[16]

#### 5. Working length

Working length should be established with an electronic apex locator supplemented by appropriate radiographic assessment. Curvature can cause file deviation and projection errors, making an apex locator particularly useful when combined with a good radiographic understanding of the canal.[1,2]

#### 6. Biomechanical preparation

#### Principles

Preserve the original canal curvature.

Avoid excessive apical force.

Use flexible instruments.

Maintain a glide path.

Use controlled pecking or short amplitude movements.

Clean flutes frequently.

Irrigate repeatedly.

Use smaller tapers where dentin thickness and curvature demand it.

#### Hand instrumentation

Stainless-steel hand files are useful for initial negotiation because they can be precurved. Step-back preparation can preserve curvature if performed carefully, but aggressive enlargement may create transportation in severely curved canals.

#### Crown-down preparation

Crown-down preparation reduces coronal interferences before apical negotiation and may decrease the tendency of larger instruments to bind coronally. It is useful in curved canals when combined with adequate glide-path establishment.

#### NiTi rotary and reciprocating systems

NiTi instruments provide greater flexibility than stainless steel and are therefore central to modern management of curved canals. However, NiTi does not eliminate procedural risk. Cyclic fatigue and torsional stress remain major causes of fracture.[6,15]

Heat-treated NiTi alloys, controlled-memory behavior and optimized cross-sectional designs can improve flexibility and fatigue resistance. Evidence from experimental studies shows that file performance varies with alloy, geometry, movement and curvature, so system selection should be anatomy driven rather than brand driven.[6,7,17]

#### 7. Irrigation

Curvature increases the importance of chemical debridement because instruments cannot contact every wall. Sodium hypochlorite should be used copiously with safe needle placement. EDTA is used for smear-layer removal. Activation can improve irrigant exchange in irregular areas, but it does not make inaccessible anatomy fully instrumentable.[8]

#### 8. Prevention of procedural errors

| Error | Mechanism | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Ledge | File straightening and forced apical progression | Precurve small files; glide path; flexible NiTi |
| Transportation | Unequal dentin removal on outer wall | Conservative taper; flexible instruments |
| Zipping | Apical transportation in curved apical segment | Avoid force; maintain original path |
| Strip perforation | Excessive preparation of danger zone | Anticurvature filing; conservative shaping |
| Instrument separation | Cyclic fatigue/torsional stress | Glide path, proper motion, avoid binding |
| Loss of working length | Debris blockage/ledge formation | Frequent irrigation, recapitulation, patency where appropriate |

#### 9. Obturation

The prepared canal should be obturated three dimensionally without overextension. Warm techniques may improve adaptation in irregular curved spaces, but the technique should match apical control and the shape created during preparation.

#### 10. Contemporary developments

Modern heat-treated NiTi systems and reciprocating motion have increased the fatigue resistance of instruments compared with older alloy technologies in many laboratory models.[6,7,17] The most defensible contemporary conclusion is that metallurgy and kinematics reduce risk but do not replace anatomical knowledge, glide-path control and conservative shaping.

## 5. Classify root canals according to Weine and discuss the management of curved and calcified root canals. [50 marks]

### STUDY MODE

#### PART A. WEINE CLASSIFICATION

Weine classification describes canal configurations according to the number of canals present in the root and how they divide or merge before reaching the apex. It is particularly useful as a simple system for communicating canal patterns, although modern micro-CT and CBCT studies have demonstrated that many complex configurations cannot be adequately represented by older two-dimensional systems.[18,19]

| Weine type | Configuration | Schematic |
|---|---|---|
| Type I | One canal from orifice to one apical foramen | 1 → 1 |
| Type II | Two separate orifices joining into one canal with one apical foramen | 2 → 1 |
| Type III | Two separate canals from two orifices to two separate apical foramina | 2 → 2 |
| Type IV | One canal leaving the orifice and dividing into two canals with two apical foramina | 1 → 2 |

Weine’s system is useful for rapid examination description but is not exhaustive. Ahmed and Dummer proposed newer coding systems because modern imaging reveals additional roots, multiple canals, accessory configurations and complex anomalies that older classifications may not describe adequately.[18,19]

#### PART B. CURVED ROOT CANALS

Curved canals are managed by maintaining the original canal trajectory while achieving adequate cleaning and shaping. The principles are covered in detail in Question 4 and are summarized here in an examination-oriented format.

#### Management sequence

Preoperative radiographic assessment and curvature estimation.

Modify access to reduce coronal interferences.

Negotiate with small precurved K-files.

Establish a reproducible glide path.

Determine working length.

Use flexible NiTi with conservative taper.

Irrigate frequently and recapitulate.

Avoid instrument binding and excessive apical force.

Monitor for ledge, transportation and separation.

Obturate according to the final canal shape.

#### PART C. CALCIFIED ROOT CANALS

#### 1. Definition and clinical significance

Canal calcification refers to narrowing or partial/near-complete radiographic obliteration of the pulp space caused by secondary or tertiary dentin deposition. In older patients, calcification commonly progresses from the coronal region toward the apical region.[1]

A radiographically invisible canal should not automatically be assumed to be anatomically absent. Ingle’s Endodontics emphasizes that a histological root canal space may persist despite apparent radiographic obliteration.[1]

#### 2. Diagnosis

Review the preoperative radiograph for canal narrowing, chamber obliteration and root landmarks.

Assess the tooth axis and root morphology.

Take a second radiographic angulation when orientation is uncertain.

Use bitewing or other orientation imaging when access depth is difficult.

Consider CBCT selectively for three-dimensional localization of calcified canals.[1,5]

CBCT can assist triangulation and orientation in selected calcified cases, but artifacts and radiation exposure mean that it should be justified rather than automatically used.[5,12]

#### 3. Access principles

The goal is to locate the original canal while preserving tooth structure. In calcified chambers, the tactile sensation of 'dropping' into the pulp chamber may be absent. Orientation should therefore be verified using external tooth landmarks, radiographs and careful measurement of access depth.[1]

Establish a conservative but adequate access.

Use magnification and illumination.

Use ultrasonic tips for controlled removal of calcification and troughing.

Use small round burs cautiously for initial penetration.

Use a radiopaque marker when depth/orientation is uncertain.[1]

#### 4. Negotiation

Use #6, #8 and #10 K-files.

Precurve the file tip.

Use EDTA to assist negotiation.

Use watch-winding and gentle balanced movements.

Do not force the file when resistance is encountered.

Recapitulate and irrigate frequently.

C-files and other stiff small hand files can be useful for initial penetration of calcified segments. Rotary glide-path instruments should only be introduced after a safe manual pathway has been established.[1]

#### 5. Ultrasonics

Ultrasonic instruments are useful for controlled removal of calcified dentin, troughing and localization of hidden orifices. Small ultrasonic tips provide visualization and controlled cutting, but excessive power or prolonged contact can generate heat or remove excessive dentin.[1,2]

#### 6. Guided and digital approaches

Static guided endodontic techniques have emerged for selected severely calcified canals. A CBCT dataset can be combined with an intraoral scan to plan a virtual drill trajectory and create a guide that directs the bur toward the anticipated canal path. This is particularly relevant when conventional freehand negotiation is judged to have a high risk of perforation. The technique is case dependent and requires sufficient access for guide placement.

The contemporary principle is therefore escalation rather than immediate escalation: conventional radiography and careful clinical orientation first, CBCT when justified, then guided or specialized techniques for selected high-risk anatomy.

| Complication | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Perforation | Wrong axis/depth | Orientation, CBCT when justified, controlled ultrasonic troughing |
| Ledge | Forced file | Precurved small file, chelation |

## 7. Discuss the identification, location and biomechanical preparation of the MB2 canal in maxillary molars. [100 marks]

### STUDY MODE

#### 1. Introduction

The mesiobuccal root of the maxillary first molar is one of the most important examples of clinically significant canal variation. Ingle’s Endodontics reports that the mesiobuccal root contains two canals more often than one and summarizes data from 14,346 teeth in which two canals were identified in 59.4% of mesiobuccal roots overall, with higher rates in laboratory studies than clinical studies.[1]

The second mesiobuccal canal, or MB2, is clinically important because its orifice is often concealed beneath dentin, its course may be narrow and curved, and it may merge with MB1 or exit through a separate apical foramen.[1,21] A contemporary Indian systematic review and meta-analysis found that detection depended strongly on the method used, with pooled detection/prevalence estimates higher when magnification and ultrasonic instrumentation were added to direct vision.[21]

#### 2. Anatomy of the maxillary first molar

The maxillary first molar normally has three roots: mesiobuccal, distobuccal and palatal. The mesiobuccal root is broad buccolingually and may contain one or two canals. The distobuccal and palatal roots are much more consistently single-canal roots.[1]

| Root | Expected canal anatomy | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Mesiobuccal | MB1 commonly; MB2 frequent | Highest canal-variation risk |
| Distobuccal | Usually one canal | Generally easier to locate |
| Palatal | Usually one large canal | Large landmark canal for orientation |

The MB2 canal is often positioned palatal and slightly mesial to MB1, although the exact relationship varies. Therefore, a rigid geometric rule should never replace direct anatomical inspection.[1,2]

#### 3. Why MB2 is missed

Dentin bridge covering the orifice.

Orifice located in a developmental groove rather than an obvious round opening.

MB2 may be very small or calcified.

Root and canal superimposition on 2-D radiographs.

Coronal anatomy may cause the clinician to stop searching after locating MB1.

MB2 may merge with MB1 and therefore not be obvious radiographically.

Operator experience and access design affect detection.[1,21]

#### 4. Preoperative identification

#### A. Radiographic clues

Assess the number and shape of roots.

Look for an eccentric radiolucent canal outline within the mesiobuccal root.

Look for sudden disappearance or narrowing of the MB1 canal.

Use altered horizontal angulation to separate superimposed anatomy.

Ingle’s Endodontics specifically notes that changing horizontal angulation can better open the mesiobuccal root and demonstrate the two canals.[1]

#### B. CBCT

CBCT can identify the number and course of MB canals in three dimensions and can be useful when conventional radiography and clinical inspection remain inconclusive. Its use should be selective and justified by the expected diagnostic benefit.[5,21]

The clinician should recognize that CBCT is not infallible. Small canals may be below spatial resolution, artifacts can degrade images, and volumetric distortion can affect assessment.[5,12]

#### 5. Access cavity design

Access should permit direct visualization of the chamber floor and the developmental groove between MB1 and the palatal canal. Ingle’s Endodontics describes a quadrilateral access approach for maxillary molars and recommends controlled troughing approximately 2 to 3 mm lingual to MB1 when searching for MB2.[1]

The access should not be enlarged indiscriminately. The objective is to expose the search zone while preserving pericervical dentin and avoiding furcal gouging.[1,2]

#### 6. Location of MB2

#### Anatomical search zone

The MB2 orifice is usually sought along the developmental groove extending from MB1 toward the palatal canal. It is commonly located palatal and mesial relative to MB1, but the position can vary.[1,2]

The safest principle is to search the groove rather than to memorize a single millimeter distance. Orifice location should be interpreted in relation to the chamber floor, MB1, palatal orifice and root anatomy.

#### Clinical localization sequence

Remove the chamber roof completely and establish adequate visualization.

Identify the palatal orifice and MB1.

Inspect the groove between MB1 and palatal orifice.

Dry the chamber and use magnification and coaxial illumination.

Use a DG-16 explorer to detect a catch or discontinuity in the groove.

Use ultrasonic troughing conservatively along the groove.

Explore the trough with a precurved #6, #8 or #10 K-file.

Confirm that the file follows a reproducible canal path.

Use an apex locator and radiograph to establish working length once negotiation is possible.

Cohen’s Pathways of the Pulp emphasizes that small ultrasonic tips can improve visibility and controlled dentin removal during canal-orifice location. The main risk is excessive troughing and loss of dentin.[2]

#### 7. Magnification and illumination

Direct vision alone underestimates MB2 detection. Dental operating microscopes improve visualization of chamber-floor anatomy, dentin color changes, grooves and small orifices. The Indian systematic review found substantially higher MB2 detection when magnification was combined with direct vision, and the highest pooled detection estimate among the evaluated clinical approaches occurred when magnification was combined with ultrasonic instrumentation.[21]

This is not evidence that a microscope guarantees MB2 location. It means that detection is strongly technique dependent.

#### 8. Ultrasonic troughing

Ultrasonic troughing is performed along the developmental groove from MB1 toward the palatal canal. The tip should be small and controlled, with short strokes and continuous visual reassessment.[1,2]

Use a small non-cutting or minimally aggressive ultrasonic tip where appropriate.

Trough incrementally rather than making a deep continuous cut.

Maintain awareness of the furcal wall and remaining dentin.

Explore the trough repeatedly with a small hand file.

Stop when the canal is identified rather than creating a large trough.

The purpose of ultrasonics is controlled removal of the dentin bridge hiding the orifice, not enlargement of the entire mesiobuccal root.

#### 9. Troughing depth and safety

Ingle’s Endodontics specifically describes troughing 2 to 3 mm lingual to MB1 as a search strategy.[1] This should be interpreted as a clinical search region, not a mandatory drilling depth in every tooth. Root size, dentin thickness and individual anatomy must control the actual extent.

The principal hazards are furcal perforation, excessive dentin removal, gouging and weakening of the tooth.

#### 10. Negotiation of MB2

Use a small precurved stainless-steel K-file.

Orient the file according to the expected canal direction.

Use gentle watch-winding and short amplitude movements.

Use EDTA when calcification or dentin debris impedes negotiation.

Do not force the file apically.

Confirm that the file is within the true canal by tactile feel, radiography and apex locator data.

Once a reproducible glide path exists, enlarge gradually.

#### 11. Working-length determination

MB2 working length can be difficult because the canal may be narrow, curved and merging with MB1. An electronic apex locator should be used with appropriate radiographic confirmation and clinical correlation.[1,2]

When MB2 merges with MB1, the point of communication and final apical anatomy should be understood before final shaping. A canal that appears to terminate separately coronally may join another canal in the middle or apical third.

#### 12. Canal configuration of MB2

| Configuration | Description | Management |
|---|---|---|
| 2 → 2 | MB1 and MB2 remain separate to separate foramina | Shape and obturate independently |
| 2 → 1 | MB1 and MB2 merge before the apex | Locate both; determine shared apical path |
| 1 → 1 after apparent duplication | Two coronal spaces converge early | Avoid unnecessary enlargement of the common pathway |
| Complex/isthmus | Connecting anatomy between canals | Emphasize irrigation and conservative preparation |

Vertucci-type categories can describe some MB root patterns, but modern classification systems are better suited to complex anatomy.[18,19]

#### 13. Biomechanical preparation: principles

The MB2 canal is commonly narrower and more curved than MB1. Therefore, biomechanical preparation should be conservative and pathway preserving.[1,2]

#### A. Glide path

Negotiate manually with small precurved files.

Establish a reproducible glide path before rotary instrumentation.

Confirm that the file can reach working length without excessive binding.

A glide path reduces the tendency of rotary instruments to bind and improves predictability in narrow curved canals.[2,16]

#### B. Coronal enlargement

Limited coronal enlargement can remove dentin interferences and allow better instrument access. However, aggressive coronal flaring of the mesiobuccal root can thin the furcal wall and increase structural risk.[1,2]

#### C. NiTi instrumentation

Flexible NiTi systems are advantageous in the curved MB2 canal. Heat-treated instruments can offer increased flexibility and cyclic-fatigue resistance compared with conventional NiTi in many experimental conditions.[6,7,17]

The clinician should choose an instrument based on canal diameter, curvature, taper and remaining dentin rather than attempting to impose a fixed sequence on every MB2.

#### D. Taper selection

The goal is sufficient space for irrigation and obturation while preserving dentin. A small MB2 does not require the same taper as a large palatal canal. Excessive taper can remove dentin from the thin furcal wall and increase risk of strip perforation.

#### E. Irrigation

Irrigation is critical because mechanical instruments do not contact all surfaces of a narrow canal or any connecting isthmus. Sodium hypochlorite should be used throughout shaping, followed by appropriate chelation for smear-layer control. Irrigant activation can improve fluid movement in complex anatomy.[2,8]

#### 14. Patency and recapitulation

Patency should be approached judiciously. A small file may be used to maintain a negotiable pathway when appropriate, but forcing a file beyond the apical constriction is not a substitute for accurate working-length control.

#### 15. Obturation of MB2

If MB2 remains separate, it should be shaped and obturated as an independent canal. If it merges with MB1, the final obturation should reflect the common apical pathway. Thermoplasticized gutta-percha may be advantageous where irregularities or joining anatomy make adaptation difficult, although no obturation technique compensates for inadequate disinfection.

#### 16. Complications related to MB2

| Complication | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Missed MB2 | Failure to search groove | Systematic search, magnification, ultrasonics |
| Perforation | Deep blind troughing | Incremental ultrasonic troughing, anatomical orientation |
| Ledge | Forced file in curved canal | Precurved small files and glide path |
| Transportation | Excessive shaping | Flexible NiTi, conservative taper |
| Instrument separation | Cyclic/torsional fatigue | Glide path, proper instrument use |
| Strip perforation | Overpreparation of furcal wall | Conservative shaping and dentin awareness |

#### 17. Evidence on MB2 detection

A systematic review and meta-analysis of Indian studies found a pooled MB2 prevalence/detection estimate of 64.76% using CBCT, 26.5% using direct vision alone, 60.4% using magnification with direct vision and 71.9% using direct vision, magnification and ultrasonic instrumentation.[21]

These values illustrate a central clinical principle: detection is method dependent. A low detection rate using direct vision alone should not be interpreted as absence of MB2 in the population.[21]

Stropko’s clinical observations similarly showed that the ability to locate MB2 improved with operator experience, the dental operating microscope and microendodontic instruments.[22]

#### 18. Contemporary classification and digital imaging

Ahmed et al. developed a new root and root canal classification system because conventional systems were insufficient for the complexity revealed by modern imaging. Their work is particularly relevant to MB2 because the mesiobuccal root may contain multiple pathways, joining patterns and apical exits that cannot be reduced to a single simple category.[18]

CBCT and micro-CT permit three-dimensional visualization, while newer studies are beginning to evaluate machine learning and deep-learning approaches for recognition of complex root canal morphology.[23] These technologies are emerging adjuncts, not replacements for clinical anatomical knowledge.

#### 19. 100-mark master synthesis

The MB2 problem can be understood as a chain: prediction → visualization → localization → negotiation → confirmation → glide path → conservative shaping → irrigation → obturation. Failure at any link may leave an infected canal untreated.

## 8. Cross-question contemporary concepts and evidence synthesis

The modern management of difficult root canal anatomy has shifted from a purely mechanical view of instrumentation toward an anatomy-first strategy. The clinician first seeks to understand three-dimensional morphology, then chooses access, instruments, irrigation and obturation around that anatomy.[1,2,11,18]

### 1. Three-dimensional imaging

CBCT can reveal additional roots, canals, curvature and complex configurations that may be hidden on periapical radiographs. Its value is greatest when the additional information changes diagnosis or treatment planning.[5,10,21]

### 2. Magnification and ultrasonics

Operating microscopes improve visualization of small chamber-floor landmarks, while ultrasonic instruments allow controlled removal of dentin during troughing. Their strongest clinical role is in localization of hidden anatomy such as MB2 and calcified orifices.[1,2,21,22]

### 3. Modern NiTi metallurgy

Heat treatment, alloy modification, cross-sectional optimization and altered kinematics have improved resistance to cyclic fatigue in many experimental settings. However, fracture risk remains dependent on canal curvature, radius, instrument dimensions, motion and technique.[6,7,15,17]

### 4. Contemporary classification

Older systems such as Weine and Vertucci remain valuable for teaching and communication, but modern imaging demonstrates configurations that exceed their original categories. Ahmed and Dummer proposed coding systems intended to describe complex root, canal and anomaly patterns more completely.[18,19]

### 5. AI and digital morphology

Recent Journal of Endodontics research has begun evaluating deep-learning methods for recognition of complex root canal morphology from radiographic data. These approaches are promising for decision support but should currently be considered emerging rather than established replacements for clinician interpretation.[23]

### 6. Dental Materials perspective

The relevance of Dental Materials literature in these anatomy questions is strongest in the behavior of endodontic NiTi instruments under curvature-induced cyclic fatigue. Experimental evidence demonstrates that curvature angle and radius influence fracture behavior and that modern heat-treated alloy technologies can alter fatigue resistance.[6]

### 7. Caries Research relevance

The direct anatomical questions in this document are primarily supported by endodontic morphology literature because that is where the evidence base is concentrated. Caries Research literature is therefore not artificially inserted into the anatomy sections. Its strongest relevance is at the pulp-preservation interface, where contemporary deep-caries guidance emphasizes selective tissue removal and biologically based vital pulp preservation when appropriate.[24]

This distinction is deliberate: adding a journal merely because it was requested would weaken source quality if it did not address the anatomical claim being made.

### Final examination synthesis

For difficult root canal anatomy, the highest-yield conceptual sequence is: KNOW THE EXPECTED ANATOMY → SUSPECT THE VARIATION → IMAGE IT → VISUALIZE IT → NEGOTIATE IT → ESTABLISH A GLIDE PATH → SHAPE CONSERVATIVELY → DISINFECT CHEMICALLY → OBTURATE ACCORDING TO THE ACTUAL CONFIGURATION. This sequence integrates the principles common to RE, C-shaped canals, curved canals, calcified canals, extra canals and MB2 management.

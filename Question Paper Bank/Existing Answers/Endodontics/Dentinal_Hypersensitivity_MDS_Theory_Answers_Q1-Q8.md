## 1. Discuss dentin/dentinal hypersensitivity in detail, including its etiology, pathogenesis, clinical features, diagnosis and management. [100 marks]

Suggested answer structure: introduction → definition → etiologic factors → pathogenesis → theories → clinical features → diagnosis and differential diagnosis → management → prevention → prognosis → conclusion.

### Introduction

Dentinal hypersensitivity is a common form of odontogenic pain associated with exposed dentin. The condition becomes clinically important when dentinal tubules are patent and external stimuli produce a brief, sharp pain. The cervical region is particularly vulnerable because gingival recession, periodontal treatment, abrasion and erosion can remove the protective root covering.

### Definition

Ingle et al. describe dentin sensitivity as a short, sharp pain from exposed dentin in response to thermal, tactile, osmotic, evaporative or chemical stimuli that cannot be attributed to another pathosis or dental defect. (Ingle et al., 2019)

### Etiology and predisposing factors

| Factor | Mechanism / clinical relevance |
|---|---|
| Gingival recession | Exposes cervical/root dentin and patent tubules. |
| Periodontal therapy | Scaling, root planing and periodontal surgery may expose or instrument root surfaces. |
| Abrasion | Mechanical tooth surface loss, commonly related to improper or vigorous brushing or other abrasive habits. |
| Erosion | Acid-mediated loss of tooth structure; brushing can accelerate loss after acid softening. |
| Attrition | Tooth-to-tooth functional or parafunctional wear that may expose dentin. |
| Abfraction / occlusal stress | Proposed contribution to cervical defects through stress concentration and tooth flexure; often considered multifactorial. |
| Bleaching | May provoke transient sensitivity in exposed or susceptible dentin. |
| Anatomical defects | Exposed dentin at the CEJ and other developmental/anatomic conditions may increase risk. |
| Acidic oral environment | Increases demineralization and can remove/alter the protective smear layer. |
| Restorative procedures | Dentin exposure, adhesive manipulation and inadequate sealing may contribute to postoperative sensitivity. |

### Pathogenesis

The central biological event is increased permeability of exposed dentin. Hypersensitive teeth have been reported to show a greater number and larger diameter of patent tubules than control dentin. Consequently, stimuli capable of producing rapid dentinal fluid movement can activate nociceptive elements at the pulpodentin border.

### Hydrodynamic theory

The hydrodynamic theory, classically associated with Brännström and Aström (1964), is the most widely accepted explanation. Mechanical, thermal and osmotic stimuli produce movement of fluid within patent dentinal tubules. Rapid movement produces mechanical effects near the pulpal ends of the tubules and activates predominantly Aδ nociceptive fibers, producing sharp pain.

The theory is strongly supported by experimental observations. Acid etching increases dentinal sensitivity by increasing tubular patency, while occluding tubules reduces sensitivity. Cold is a particularly common stimulus and produces rapid transient pain. Ingle also notes that cold tends to produce outward fluid movement, whereas heat produces inward movement.

### Other theories and modern concepts

| Theory / concept | Core proposal | Main limitation / interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Neural theory | Nerve endings within dentin are directly activated by external stimuli. | Insufficient nerve distribution through the full dentin thickness to explain all clinical sensitivity. |
| Odontoblast transduction theory | Odontoblasts may detect stimuli through receptors and ion channels and communicate with sensory nerve terminals. | Biologically plausible but not a replacement for the strong hydrodynamic evidence. |
| Hydrodynamic theory | Fluid movement within patent tubules mechanically activates pulpal nociceptive elements. | Best supported overall model, although the exact molecular transduction mechanism remains incompletely defined. |
| Mechanosensitive ion-channel concept | Fluid movement and shear stress may activate mechanosensitive channels such as TREK and ASIC-related channels. | Provides a modern cellular explanation of how hydraulic forces may become neural signals. |

### Clinical features

Usually affects exposed cervical/root dentin, particularly incisors and premolars.

Pain is sharp, sudden and short-lasting.

Cold is a frequent trigger; air blast, toothbrushing, sweet or salty foods and other osmotic stimuli may also provoke pain.

Pain normally ceases when the stimulus is removed.

Spontaneous pain is not typical of uncomplicated dentinal hypersensitivity.

The tooth is generally vital.

The affected surface often shows gingival recession, cervical wear, exposed root dentin or a noncarious cervical lesion.

Symptoms may decrease with age because of dentinal sclerosis and secondary/reparative dentin formation.

### Diagnosis

Diagnosis is essentially a diagnosis of exclusion. The clinician must establish that the pain is reproducible from exposed dentin and is not better explained by caries, pulpal inflammation, crack, defective restoration or another odontogenic/nonodontogenic pain condition.

#### 1. History

Site, number of teeth and duration of symptoms.

Exact provoking stimulus: cold, air, touch, sweets, acids or brushing.

Duration after removal of the stimulus.

Severity, frequency and progression.

Presence or absence of spontaneous pain or lingering pain.

Dietary acids, reflux/regurgitation history where relevant, brushing habits and abrasive products.

Recent periodontal therapy, bleaching, restorations or tooth preparation.

Parafunction, bruxism and other mechanical habits.

#### 2. Clinical examination

Inspect for gingival recession and exposed root surfaces.

Identify cervical abrasion, erosion, attrition and abfraction-like defects.

Assess plaque, periodontal condition and previous root instrumentation.

Inspect for caries, cracks, defective restorations and exposed dentin.

Palpate/percuss when indicated and compare with neighboring teeth.

#### 3. Sensibility testing

Controlled air and tactile stimulation of the exposed area can reproduce the patient's characteristic response. Cold and electrical pulp testing are used when pulpal disease is a differential diagnosis. Control teeth should be used for comparison.

#### 4. Radiographic examination

Radiographs are not diagnostic of dentinal hypersensitivity itself, but are useful for excluding caries, periapical disease, defective restorations and other pathology.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Typical distinction from dentinal hypersensitivity |
|---|---|
| Reversible pulpitis | Stimulus-related pain, but pulpal pathology must be considered if symptoms are more intense or prolonged. |
| Irreversible pulpitis | May show lingering or spontaneous pain and other inflammatory features. |
| Caries | Localized lesion with clinical/radiographic evidence of caries. |
| Cracked tooth | Pain may be associated with biting/release and may not correspond simply to exposed cervical dentin. |
| Defective restoration | Pain associated with restoration, marginal leakage or other restorative defect. |
| Root-surface caries | Caries lesion rather than a clean noncarious exposed surface. |
| Occlusal trauma | Percussion/tenderness and functional history may predominate. |
| Neuropathic/referred pain | Atypical distribution, quality or lack of reproducible local dentinal trigger. |

### Management: principles

Treatment should be etiologic and stepwise. Two principal strategies are recognized: reduce dentinal fluid flow by occluding or sealing tubules, and/or reduce neural responsiveness to dentinal stimuli.

#### A. Eliminate or control etiologic factors

Modify vigorous or traumatic toothbrushing and use an appropriate soft brush.

Reduce frequent dietary acid exposure and avoid aggressive brushing immediately after acidic intake.

Control plaque and periodontal inflammation while avoiding unnecessary root-surface trauma.

Identify and manage intrinsic acid exposure where relevant.

Evaluate parafunctional activity and occlusal factors.

Treat caries and defective restorations.

Use preventive fluoride measures.

#### B. Home-use desensitizing dentifrices

| Agent / group | Principal action | Exam point |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium nitrate | Neural modulation; proposed reduction of nerve excitability/depolarization-repolarization activity. | Major evidence-supported home-care approach in the project sources. |
| Strontium salts | Desensitizing effect associated with tubule-related changes/mineral deposition. | Clinical efficacy varies with study design. |
| Fluoride / stannous fluoride | Promotes mineral deposition and tubular occlusion. | Stannous fluoride gel has shown prolonged desensitizing effects in clinical trials. |

Seltzer and Bender summarize multiple randomized controlled trials of potassium-containing dentifrices. Their sample-weighted analysis reported approximately 60% reduction in cold sensitivity with 3.75% and 5% potassium nitrate formulations, although placebo responses were substantial.

#### C. Professional topical agents

Fluoride varnishes and gels: promote mineral deposition and reduce permeability.

Oxalate solutions: produce relatively insoluble deposits and reduce fluid flow.

Calcium phosphate systems: encourage mineral deposition and tubule occlusion.

Bioactive glass: supplies ions that can promote mineral deposition over exposed dentin.

Glutaraldehyde/HEMA systems such as GLUMA: reduce permeability through protein precipitation/denaturation and tubular occlusion.

#### D. Resin and dentin adhesive sealing

Resin impregnation and dentin adhesive systems can form resin tags and a hybrid layer, reducing dentinal fluid movement. Seltzer and Bender cite clinical and experimental evidence for substantial reductions in sensitivity after resin application. Sturdevant also emphasizes the role of resin tags and hybrid-layer formation.

Useful for localized exposed root surfaces and refractory sensitivity.

Provides rapid tubular sealing.

Technique sensitivity and longevity are important limitations.

Sclerotic cervical dentin is more resistant to acid etching and may show reduced adhesive penetration.

Recurrence can occur when the resin seal is lost.

#### E. Glass ionomer cement and composite

Restoration is considered when sensitivity is intolerable and conservative measures fail, or when the defect is carious, structurally significant, periodontally relevant, deep, or requires restoration for functional or esthetic reasons. The etiologic factor should be controlled before or together with restoration.

#### F. Periodontal root coverage

Selected patients with significant gingival recession and exposed root surfaces may benefit from mucogingival/root-coverage procedures. Case selection depends on periodontal anatomy and prognosis.

#### G. Iontophoresis

Iontophoresis has been used to enhance penetration of desensitizing ions into dentin. It is a conservative adjunct, although reported effectiveness is variable.

#### H. Lasers

Lasers can reduce sensitivity through tubular occlusion, reduction of dentinal permeability and, depending on wavelength and parameters, modification of neural responsiveness. Nd:YAG has been extensively studied, while Er:YAG and CO2 lasers have also been investigated.

Seltzer and Bender report a study using Nd:YAG at 30 mJ and 10 Hz for 2 minutes in 30 patients, with reductions in sensitivity to air and probing over 3 months. Combination with sodium fluoride varnish produced greater tubular occlusion in the cited studies. However, controlled trials have produced variable results, and laser energy must be selected carefully to avoid thermal injury.

### Treatment of associated noncarious tooth wear

The underlying lesion should be managed rather than simply covering the symptom.

| Lesion | Cause | Clinical approach |
|---|---|---|
| Abrasion | External mechanical friction, especially brushing/habits. | Remove the mechanical cause; conservative desensitization first; restore only when indicated. |
| Erosion | Chemical loss from acids; brushing may accelerate it. | Reduce acid frequency, address intrinsic acid sources, modify brushing and support remineralization. |
| Attrition | Functional/parafunctional tooth-to-tooth contact. | Assess occlusion, bruxism and functional movements; protect and restore significant sensitive wear where indicated. |
| Abfraction / stress-related cervical defect | Proposed tooth flexure/stress contribution, often interacting with abrasion and erosion. | Do not assume a single cause; assess mechanical, chemical and occlusal factors. |

### Prognosis

Symptoms may decrease spontaneously because of natural tubular occlusion, mineral deposition, secondary/reparative dentin and dentinal sclerosis. Nevertheless, recurrence is possible if the causal factor persists or a desensitizing seal is lost.

## 2. Discuss the etiology, pathogenesis/theories and management of dentinal hypersensitivity. [50 marks]

### Definition

Dentinal hypersensitivity is a short, sharp pain arising from exposed dentin in response to thermal, tactile, osmotic, evaporative or chemical stimuli, when another dental defect or pathosis does not account for the symptoms. (Ingle et al., 2019)

### Etiology

Gingival recession and exposed root surfaces.

Periodontal scaling, root planing and periodontal surgery.

Noncarious tooth surface loss: abrasion, erosion and attrition.

Cervical defects associated with occlusal stress/abfraction concepts.

Tooth bleaching.

Anatomical/developmental defects and exposed dentin at the CEJ.

Acidic dietary habits and intrinsic acid exposure.

Aggressive toothbrushing and abrasive oral hygiene products.

### Pathogenesis

Exposure of dentin permits external stimuli to act on patent dentinal tubules. Hypersensitive dentin has greater tubular patency and larger/more numerous open tubules than normal dentin. A stimulus causes rapid fluid displacement within the tubule. This produces mechanical deformation/shear effects at the pulpodentin border and activates nociceptive nerve fibers, producing a sharp pain.

### Theories

| Theory | Explanation | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Neural theory | Direct stimulation of nerve endings within dentin. | Historically important but inadequate to explain the distribution of intradentinal nerves. |
| Odontoblast transduction theory | Odontoblasts act as sensory/transducing cells and communicate with nerves. | Supported by receptor/channel findings but remains complementary. |
| Hydrodynamic theory | Stimuli cause fluid movement in dentinal tubules that activates pulpal sensory elements. | Most widely accepted and best supported. |

Brännström and Aström (1964) provided the classic hydrodynamic formulation. Modern concepts add that fluid movement may generate shear stress and activate mechanosensitive ion channels. Ingle also notes that cold tends to cause outward fluid movement and a rapid transient pain response.

### Management

Management follows two major strategies: reduce dentinal fluid flow or reduce neuronal responsiveness.

#### 1. Etiologic and preventive control

Correct toothbrushing technique and reduce excessive force.

Use an appropriate low-trauma brush and avoid abrasive habits.

Reduce frequency of acidic foods/drinks and avoid brushing immediately after acid exposure.

Control plaque and periodontal disease while minimizing root-surface trauma.

Address intrinsic acid exposure where present.

Evaluate parafunction and occlusal factors.

Treat caries and defective restorations.

#### 2. Tubule-occluding agents

Sodium fluoride and stannous fluoride.

Strontium chloride.

Potassium oxalate and other oxalate preparations.

Calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate.

Bioactive glasses.

Fluoride varnishes, resins and other dentin sealers.

Glass ionomer cement and composite when restorative coverage is indicated.

#### 3. Neural-modulating agents

Potassium nitrate is the principal example in the project sources. It is proposed to reduce nerve excitability by depolarization and interference with repolarization. Seltzer and Bender report that potassium-containing dentifrices were effective in the majority of randomized controlled trials, although placebo effects were appreciable.

#### 4. Protein precipitation / chemical desensitization

Glutaraldehyde-containing systems such as GLUMA reduce dentinal permeability through protein denaturation/precipitation and occlusion of tubules. Sturdevant describes confocal evidence of occluding structures following GLUMA application, while also noting that not all experimental studies demonstrate identical permeability effects.

#### 5. Resin sealing

Dentin adhesives form resin tags and a hybrid layer that reduce dentinal fluid movement. Resin impregnation has demonstrated substantial reductions in sensitivity. The limitation is that longevity depends on retention of the seal and sclerotic cervical dentin is less receptive to adhesive penetration.

#### 6. Lasers and other adjuncts

CO2, Nd:YAG and Er:YAG lasers have been investigated. Nd:YAG is particularly well studied and may occlude tubules by thermal modification of superficial dentin. Iontophoresis and selected periodontal root-coverage procedures are additional options.

## 3. Discuss the management of dentinal hypersensitivity, including various treatment modalities and preventive measures. [100 marks]

### Introduction and treatment philosophy

The weakest management strategy is to prescribe a desensitizing agent without identifying why the dentin is exposed. Successful management requires diagnosis, control of the causal factor, reduction of dentinal permeability and/or reduction of neural responsiveness, followed by escalation only when necessary.

### Objectives

Establish a correct diagnosis and exclude other causes of pain.

Identify and eliminate the etiologic factor.

Reduce exposure of dentinal tubules where possible.

Reduce dentinal fluid flow by tubular occlusion.

Reduce neuronal responsiveness where appropriate.

Restore or surgically cover exposed dentin in selected cases.

Prevent recurrence.

### Management algorithm

Essential labels: History, examination, differential diagnosis, prevention, home care, professional agents, restoration, surgery, laser.

### I. Diagnosis before treatment

Confirm the pain is short, sharp and stimulus-dependent.

Locate the exposed dentin.

Assess gingival recession, periodontal history and noncarious tooth wear.

Exclude caries, cracked tooth, defective restoration and pulpal pathology.

Use controlled air, tactile and thermal tests and compare with control teeth.

Use radiography where a competing diagnosis requires it.

### II. Preventive and etiologic measures

#### A. Toothbrushing modification

Use a gentle technique with appropriate pressure.

Use a soft brush and avoid aggressive horizontal scrubbing of cervical surfaces.

Do not encourage complete cessation of brushing, because this can increase plaque accumulation and caries risk.

Reduce abrasive exposure and avoid unnecessary tartar-control products when they aggravate symptoms.

#### B. Dietary modification

Reduce frequency rather than merely the amount of dietary acid exposure.

Limit repeated sipping of acidic drinks.

Rinse with water after acid exposure.

Avoid immediate vigorous brushing after an acidic challenge.

#### C. Intrinsic acid control

Where gastric acid exposure contributes to erosion, the underlying medical/dietary issue should be recognized and appropriately managed.

#### D. Periodontal prevention

Maintain periodontal health while minimizing unnecessary trauma to exposed root surfaces.

Provide careful postoperative instructions after periodontal procedures because root exposure can cause transient hypersensitivity.

### III. Home desensitizing agents

| Agent | Mechanism | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium nitrate | Neural modulation; proposed reduction in excitability. | Simple home use; evidence from multiple clinical trials. | Requires continued use; placebo response is substantial. |
| Fluoride | Promotes mineral deposition/tubular occlusion. | Widely available and preventive for caries. | Effect may be inconsistent depending on formulation. |
| Stannous fluoride | Tubule-related mineral deposition and reduced permeability. | Clinical evidence for prolonged desensitization. | Requires repeated/appropriate use. |
| Strontium salts | Tubule-related desensitizing/mineral effects. | Historically used in dentifrices. | Efficacy varies among study designs. |

Seltzer and Bender summarize controlled trials showing that potassium-containing dentifrices reduce cold sensitivity. Their sample-weighted analysis reported approximately 60% reduction with 3.75% and 5% potassium nitrate formulations, while placebo dentifrices also produced a meaningful response.

### IV. Professional chemical agents

#### 1. Fluoride varnishes and gels

Fluoride preparations can promote mineral deposition and reduce tubular permeability. Seltzer and Bender cite a meta-analysis in which 0.717% stannous fluoride gel applied for 3 to 5 minutes produced a significant and prolonged reduction in hypersensitivity.

#### 2. Oxalates

Oxalate salts can produce insoluble deposits that reduce dentinal fluid movement.

#### 3. Calcium phosphate systems

These aim to promote mineral deposition within and over dentinal tubules.

#### 4. Bioactive glass

Bioactive glass supplies calcium and phosphate ions and can promote mineral formation over exposed dentin, thereby reducing permeability.

#### 5. Glutaraldehyde/HEMA

Glutaraldehyde-containing desensitizers can denature and precipitate proteins within dentinal fluid and form occluding structures. The project source describes GLUMA as a clinically used example.

### V. Resin-based desensitization

Resin impregnation is a direct tubular sealing method. Resin enters exposed tubules and forms tags, reducing fluid flow. Seltzer and Bender cite experimental and clinical evidence for substantial reduction in sensitivity, including approximately twofold reduction in one experimental model.

Indications: localized, persistent or refractory hypersensitivity; exposed root surfaces; selected cases before indirect restorations.

Advantages: rapid effect and direct tubular sealing.

Limitations: technique sensitivity, sclerotic dentin, possible wear or loss of seal and recurrence.

### VI. Restorative treatment

Restorative treatment should be conservative and selective. Sturdevant recommends considering restoration when the area is carious, structurally compromising, intolerably sensitive despite conservative measures, periodontally relevant, very deep, or requires restoration for functional/esthetic reasons.

Glass ionomer cement: useful for cervical/root-surface coverage and chemical adhesion.

Resin composite: useful where esthetics and adhesive retention are favorable.

Adhesive dentin sealing may be used with or without restorative contouring depending on the defect.

### VII. Periodontal surgical management

In selected recession cases, root-coverage procedures can eliminate the exposed surface. The procedure is not a routine desensitizing treatment and should be chosen on periodontal indications and predictable anatomy.

### VIII. Iontophoresis

Iontophoresis has been used to enhance the delivery of desensitizing ions through dentin. It may be considered when topical measures are insufficient, but effectiveness is variable.

### IX. Laser therapy

| Laser | Proposed effect | Evidence / caution |
|---|---|---|
| Nd:YAG | Tubular occlusion by thermal alteration of superficial dentin; possible neural effects at lower settings. | Most extensively studied in the project source; parameter dependent. |
| Er:YAG | Reduction of dentinal permeability and desensitization. | Clinical and in vitro evidence exists; results vary. |
| CO2 | Thermal modification and reduction of dentinal permeability. | Investigated for cervical dentinal hypersensitivity. |

Seltzer and Bender report Nd:YAG studies with clinically meaningful reductions in air and probing sensitivity and greater tubular occlusion when combined with sodium fluoride varnish. They also note variability in controlled clinical outcomes and the need for further research.

### X. Management of noncarious lesions

| Etiology | Corrective measure |
|---|---|
| Abrasion | Modify brushing/habit; reduce abrasive exposure; restore only if indicated. |
| Erosion | Reduce acid frequency; manage intrinsic acid exposure; modify brushing timing; support remineralization. |
| Attrition | Assess function and parafunction; manage bruxism; consider occlusal protection and restoration when significant sensitive wear exists. |
| Abfraction/stress-related defect | Evaluate occlusal stress together with abrasion and erosion rather than assuming a single mechanism. |

### XI. Prevention of recurrence

Continue individualized oral hygiene instruction.

Continue desensitizing toothpaste where appropriate.

Maintain fluoride exposure.

Review diet and acid exposure.

Review the lesion and restoration periodically.

Reassess persistent symptoms for a missed pulpal, structural or nonodontogenic diagnosis.

### Evidence-based interpretation

No single desensitizing modality can be declared universally superior from the project sources. Seltzer and Bender specifically note that many studies are before-and-after comparisons and that direct comparisons among active treatments are limited. Treatment should therefore be individualized and escalated according to severity, etiology, localization and response.

Potassium nitrate is primarily a neural-modulating approach.

Fluoride, oxalates, calcium phosphate, bioactive glass and sealers act mainly through tubular/mineral mechanisms.

Resin adhesives provide direct tubular sealing through resin tags/hybrid-layer formation.

Restoration is not automatically indicated for every noncarious cervical lesion.

Lasers are adjunctive and parameter dependent.

Prevention is central: brushing, acid control, periodontal care and parafunction management.

## 4. Define dentinal hypersensitivity. Discuss its causes, theories and management. [50 marks]

### Definition

Dentinal hypersensitivity is a short, sharp pain from exposed dentin produced by thermal, tactile, osmotic, evaporative or chemical stimuli and not attributable to another dental defect or pathosis. (Ingle et al., 2019)

### Causes / predisposing factors

Gingival recession and exposed root surfaces.

Periodontal scaling, root planing and surgery.

Abrasion from vigorous or improper brushing and other habits.

Erosion from dietary or intrinsic acids, often accelerated by brushing.

Attrition from functional/parafunctional tooth-to-tooth contact.

Abfraction/stress-related cervical defects as a proposed contributory mechanism.

Bleaching and other procedures causing exposure or increased sensitivity.

Anatomical defects and exposed dentin at the CEJ.

### Theories

#### 1. Neural theory

External stimuli are proposed to directly activate nerve endings in dentin. The main limitation is the relatively limited distribution of intradentinal nerve fibers.

#### 2. Odontoblast transduction theory

Odontoblasts possess several receptors and ion channels and may participate in sensory transduction. This provides a biologically plausible complementary mechanism.

#### 3. Hydrodynamic theory

Brännström and Aström (1964) proposed that external stimuli cause fluid movement in patent dentinal tubules. Rapid fluid movement produces mechanical stimulation of pulpal sensory fibers and causes pain. This is the most widely accepted theory.

When conservative measures fail or when the lesion itself requires restoration, exposed dentin may be covered with glass ionomer or composite. Selected recession cases may be managed with root-coverage procedures.

Nd:YAG, Er:YAG and CO2 lasers have been investigated for tubular occlusion and desensitization. Nd:YAG is the most extensively studied in the project source. Correct parameters and thermal control are essential.

Dentinal hypersensitivity is fundamentally a problem of exposed, patent dentin and stimulus-induced sensory activation. The hydrodynamic theory provides the best overall explanation. Management should be stepwise, beginning with diagnosis and cause control and progressing from home and professional desensitization to adhesive/restorative, periodontal or laser therapy when indicated.

## 5. Discuss the theories of dentinal hypersensitivity. [25 marks]

### Introduction

Dentinal hypersensitivity is a short, sharp pain from exposed dentin produced by appropriate thermal, tactile, osmotic, evaporative or chemical stimuli. Several mechanisms have been proposed, but the hydrodynamic theory has the strongest overall experimental support.

### 1. Neural theory

The neural theory proposes that external stimuli directly stimulate nerve endings extending into dentinal tubules. It attempts to explain dentinal pain by direct neural activation.

Major limitation: intradentinal nerve fibers are not distributed throughout dentin sufficiently to explain all areas of sensitivity.

It does not satisfactorily explain why tubular occlusion markedly reduces sensitivity.

### 2. Odontoblast transduction theory

This theory proposes that odontoblasts act as sensory/transducing cells. Odontoblasts express several ion channels and receptors, including TRP-related channels and calcium/potassium channels, providing a possible pathway by which external stimuli could influence nearby nerve terminals.

Supports a possible biological role for odontoblasts in sensory signaling.

It is best regarded as complementary rather than a replacement for the hydrodynamic mechanism.

### 3. Hydrodynamic theory

Brännström and Aström (1964) proposed that movement of fluid within dentinal tubules is responsible for dentinal pain. Thermal, tactile and osmotic stimuli produce rapid movement of fluid in patent tubules. This movement produces mechanical stimulation near the pulpal ends of the tubules and activates nociceptive fibers, especially Aδ fibers.

[DIAGRAM TO DRAW] Hydrodynamic theory

What it should show: External stimulus → dentinal fluid displacement → mechanical deformation/shear → pulpal nociceptor activation → pain.

Essential labels: Open tubule, fluid, pulp, Aδ nerve ending.

Purpose: Primary diagram for this question.

### Evidence supporting the hydrodynamic theory

Hypersensitive dentin has greater numbers and larger diameters of patent tubules.

Acid etching increases dentinal sensitivity and tubular patency.

Occlusion of tubules reduces dentinal permeability and sensitivity.

Air blast, cold and hyperosmotic solutions can induce fluid movement.

The typical pain is rapid and sharp, consistent with activation of fast Aδ fibers.

### Modern molecular interpretation

Ingle notes that fluid movement produces shear stress that may activate mechanosensitive ion channels such as TREK1, TREK2 and ASIC-related channels. Thus, the modern view does not necessarily discard the hydrodynamic theory; it attempts to explain how hydraulic forces are converted into neural activity.

### Comparison

| Feature | Neural | Odontoblast | Hydrodynamic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary event | Direct nerve stimulation | Odontoblast sensory transduction | Fluid movement in tubules |
| Major evidence | Limited | Increasing biological evidence | Strong experimental and clinical support |
| Tubular patency | Not central | Potentially relevant | Essential |
| Current status | Insufficient alone | Complementary hypothesis | Most accepted |

### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

Name all three major theories.

Hydrodynamic theory is the most widely accepted.

Brännström and Aström (1964) is the landmark citation.

Patent tubules and fluid movement are the key terms.

Modern ion-channel concepts provide a molecular extension of the hydrodynamic model.

## 6. Discuss recent concepts and advances in the management of dentinal hypersensitivity. [100 marks]

Important qualification: the project sources contain substantial modern mechanistic and material concepts, but they are not a comprehensive 2026 evidence review. The following answer therefore emphasizes advances documented in the uploaded sources and distinguishes newer concepts from established treatment principles.

### Introduction

Management has evolved from empiric desensitization toward mechanism-based treatment. The modern framework recognizes two principal targets: reduce dentinal fluid movement through tubular occlusion/sealing and reduce the excitability of the neurons involved in dentinal pain.

### 1. Mechanism-based treatment

| Therapeutic target | Examples | Conceptual basis |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce fluid flow | Fluoride, oxalates, calcium-phosphate agents, bioactive glass, resins, glass ionomer, adhesives, lasers | Reduce dentinal permeability by narrowing or occluding tubules. |
| Reduce neural responsiveness | Potassium-containing dentifrices; selected pharmacologic concepts | Alter excitability of dentinal nociceptive neurons. |
| Prevent exposure | Dietary control, brushing modification, periodontal prevention | Remove the initiating factor rather than treating symptoms alone. |

### 2. Recognition of tubular patency as a therapeutic endpoint

Seltzer and Bender report that hypersensitive teeth have significantly more patent tubules and greater mean tubular diameter. This has shifted attention toward measurable reduction of permeability and durable tubular occlusion rather than simply masking symptoms.

### 3. Bioactive and mineralizing approaches

The project sources identify calcium phosphate and bioactive glass among tubule-related desensitizing approaches. These materials aim to create mineral deposits over and within exposed dentin, thereby reducing fluid movement while potentially contributing to a more physiologic surface.

Calcium phosphate systems aim to promote mineral deposition.

Bioactive glass can release calcium and phosphate ions and promote mineral formation.

Fluoride and stannous fluoride remain important mineral/tubule approaches.

### 4. Adhesive and resin technology

Dentin adhesives provide a more direct seal of exposed tubules. Resin tags and a hybrid layer reduce permeability. Sturdevant describes the expanded indication of dentin adhesives for desensitization and notes the importance of tubular sealing.

A limitation is the sclerotic cervical lesion. Sturdevant notes that cervical sclerotic dentin is more resistant to acid etching and that adhesive penetration may be reduced. Therefore, modern adhesive management must account for substrate condition rather than assuming normal dentin behavior.

### 5. Glutaraldehyde/HEMA protein-precipitation concept

GLUMA-type systems represent a chemically driven approach. Glutaraldehyde can denature and precipitate proteins within dentinal fluid and alter permeability. Confocal observations have demonstrated occluding structures, although Sturdevant also notes conflicting in vitro findings regarding permeability.

### 6. Laser advances

Laser desensitization is one of the major technology-based developments documented in the project. Nd:YAG, Er:YAG and CO2 lasers have been studied.

| Laser concept | Proposed mechanism | Important project evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Nd:YAG | Thermal alteration/melting and resolidification of superficial dentin; possible neural threshold effects at lower energy. | Studies reported clinically meaningful sensitivity reduction and tubular occlusion. |
| Nd:YAG + sodium fluoride | Laser-assisted incorporation/occlusion of fluoride-related deposits. | Studies reported >90% tubule-orifice occlusion in the cited combination work. |
| Er:YAG | Reduction of dentinal permeability and desensitization. | Clinical and in vitro studies reported effectiveness. |
| CO2 | Thermal modification and reduction of dentinal permeability. | Investigated for cervical hypersensitivity. |

A critical modern concept is that laser effect is parameter dependent. Excessive energy may cause unwanted thermal effects. The aim is not simply to 'use a laser' but to select a wavelength, energy, pulse duration, frequency and delivery method appropriate for dentin and pulp safety.

### 7. Neural and ion-channel concepts

Ingle identifies mechanosensitive channels such as TREK1, TREK2 and ASIC-related channels as possible molecular links between dentinal fluid shear and neural activation. Odontoblasts also express several TRP and other ion channels. These observations expand the hydrodynamic model into a cellular and molecular framework.

### 8. Improved clinical trial methodology

Seltzer and Bender emphasize that variation in diagnostic methods, study duration, outcome measurement and control groups produces wide differences in reported efficacy. Modern management therefore requires standardized stimulus application and reproducible pain assessment when evaluating desensitizing therapies.

### 9. Evidence-based stepwise management

### 10. Advances in prevention

Recognition that acid exposure and brushing act synergistically in erosive tooth wear.

Greater emphasis on minimizing unnecessary removal of the protective smear layer.

Individualized dietary counseling rather than generic avoidance advice.

Prevention of postoperative sensitivity through appropriate dentin sealing/desensitization.

Recognition that treatment of the underlying noncarious lesion is part of hypersensitivity management.

### 11. What remains unresolved

The project sources caution against declaring one modality universally superior. Seltzer and Bender note limited direct comparisons and heterogeneous study designs. Thus, 'newer' does not automatically mean 'better'. Long-term durability, repeated application, substrate condition and adverse effects remain important determinants of clinical choice.

## 7. Discuss the role of lasers in the management of dentinal hypersensitivity. [10 marks]

### Introduction

Laser therapy is an adjunctive method for dentinal hypersensitivity. Its principal rationale is reduction of dentinal permeability through tubular occlusion, with some evidence also suggesting modification of neural responsiveness.

### Lasers studied

Nd:YAG: most extensively studied in the project source.

Er:YAG: demonstrated desensitizing effects in clinical/in vitro studies.

CO2: investigated for cervical dentinal hypersensitivity.

### Mechanisms

Thermal alteration of superficial dentin with melting and resolidification can occlude tubule orifices.

Laser-assisted treatment can reduce dentinal permeability.

At lower selected energy levels, laser irradiation may alter Aδ nerve thresholds without necessarily sealing all tubules.

Combination with sodium fluoride can enhance tubular occlusion.

### Evidence

Seltzer and Bender cite a Nd:YAG study using 30 mJ at 10 Hz for 2 minutes in 30 patients, with approximately 65% reduction in air sensitivity and 72% reduction in probing sensitivity at 3 months. In related work, Nd:YAG combined with sodium fluoride produced more than 90% tubule-orifice occlusion.

The same source reports that Nd:YAG was more effective than Er:YAG in one comparison, although other work found both wavelengths capable of reducing sensitivity. A controlled split-mouth trial did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between laser and placebo at several time points, illustrating that evidence is not uniform.

### Advantages

Potentially rapid reduction in sensitivity.

Tubular occlusion can be demonstrated morphologically.

May be useful for localized or refractory cases.

Can be combined with fluoride.

### Limitations and precautions

Outcome depends strongly on wavelength and laser parameters.

Excessive thermal energy may damage the pulp.

Clinical trial results are heterogeneous.

Not a substitute for identifying and eliminating the cause of exposed dentin.

## 8. Define attrition, abrasion and erosion. Discuss their relationship with dentinal hypersensitivity and techniques for its management. [100 marks]

### Introduction

Attrition, abrasion and erosion are major forms of noncarious tooth surface loss. They are clinically important in dentinal hypersensitivity because progressive loss of enamel or root covering exposes dentin and can increase tubular patency. In practice, the lesions frequently have multifactorial causes rather than a single isolated mechanism.

### Definitions

| Term | Definition | Typical pattern / cause |
|---|---|---|
| Abrasion | Abnormal tooth surface loss produced by direct friction between teeth and external objects or by friction involving an abrasive medium. | Commonly improper toothbrushing; may produce a smooth, V-shaped cervical notch. |
| Erosion | Loss of tooth surface by chemical action of acids; toothbrushing may contribute to subsequent mechanical loss. | Exogenous dietary acids produce rounded/dished defects; endogenous gastric acids can cause generalized lingual/incisal/occlusal loss. |
| Attrition | Mechanical wear of incisal/occlusal tooth structure caused by functional or parafunctional tooth-to-tooth contact. | Occlusal/incisal wear facets; may become advanced with bruxism/parafunction. |

### Relationship with dentinal hypersensitivity

[DIAGRAM TO DRAW] Relationship of noncarious wear to hypersensitivity

What it should show: Abrasion/erosion/attrition → enamel or cementum loss → dentin exposure → patent tubules → stimulus-induced fluid movement → dentinal hypersensitivity.

Essential labels: Abrasion, erosion, attrition, exposed dentin, open tubule, fluid, pulp.

Purpose: Shows the direct link between wear and hypersensitivity.

### A. Abrasion and hypersensitivity

Toothbrush abrasion is a major cause of exposed cervical dentin. Sturdevant describes a sharp wedge-shaped cervical defect with a smooth surface as a typical toothbrush-abrasion pattern. The condition may be associated with gingival recession, making the dentin even more exposed.

Vigorous brushing removes cervical tooth structure and can expose dentinal tubules.

Abrasive dentifrices and habits such as chewing hard objects can contribute.

Pain is often provoked by cold, air and toothbrushing.

#### Management of abrasion

Identify the brushing/habit-related cause.

Teach a gentle, nontraumatic brushing technique.

Reduce unnecessary abrasive products.

Manage associated gingival recession/periodontal factors.

Use desensitizing toothpaste and topical agents when required.

Restore only when sensitivity persists, the defect is structurally important, periodontally relevant, very deep, or otherwise meets restorative indications.

### B. Erosion and hypersensitivity

Erosion is chemical tooth surface loss produced by acids. Sturdevant distinguishes exogenous acids, such as lemon/citrus exposure, from endogenous gastric acid. The resulting surface is generally smooth. Toothbrushing is an important contributing factor because softened tooth structure is more susceptible to mechanical removal.

Exposed dentin becomes susceptible to thermal, tactile and osmotic stimulation.

Frequent acidic challenges can remove the smear layer and maintain tubular patency.

Intrinsic gastric acid can cause widespread tooth surface loss.

#### Management of erosion

Reduce frequency and duration of acidic exposure.

Avoid repeated sipping of acidic beverages.

Rinse with water after acid exposure.

Delay vigorous toothbrushing after an acidic challenge.

Use fluoride/remineralizing measures.

Identify intrinsic acid sources and arrange appropriate medical management when necessary.

Restore only when sensitivity, function, structural integrity or esthetics justify intervention.

### C. Attrition and hypersensitivity

Attrition results from functional or parafunctional tooth-to-tooth contact. Normal age-related wear is distinct from pathologic attrition. When enamel is worn away, softer dentin may become exposed and wear faster, creating cupped lesions and potential sensitivity.

Sturdevant notes that advanced attrition should prompt evaluation of functional movements and habits such as tooth grinding/bruxism. The treatment of significant sensitive wear should therefore address the cause as well as the defect.

#### Management of attrition

Assess occlusion and functional movements.

Ask about grinding, clenching and parafunctional habits.

Identify associated wear facets and patterns.

Consider protective measures such as an occlusal appliance where indicated.

Restore significant sensitive or functionally problematic wear when conservative measures are inadequate.

Before extensive cast or indirect restorations, perform appropriate occlusal analysis and investigate the etiology.

### D. Abfraction and multifactorial cervical lesions

Sturdevant describes the abfraction concept as a proposed mechanism in which heavy eccentric occlusal forces cause cervical tooth flexure, tension and microfracture. The text also emphasizes that these defects may subsequently be enlarged by toothbrush abrasion and acids. Therefore, cervical defects should not automatically be assigned to a single mechanism.

### Diagnosis of a noncarious cervical lesion

Assess shape, surface texture and location.

Look for gingival recession and periodontal history.

Ask about brushing pressure and technique.

Assess dietary and intrinsic acid exposure.

Examine occlusal contacts and wear facets.

Determine whether dentin is exposed and whether symptoms are reproducible.

Exclude cervical caries and pulpal disease.

### Relationship between wear, tubular patency and sensitivity

The common pathway is loss of protective tooth structure followed by dentin exposure. Seltzer and Bender identify erosion, abrasion, attrition and gingival recession among risk factors for dentinal hypersensitivity. Sturdevant also emphasizes that cervical hypersensitivity can arise from chemical erosion, mechanical abrasion and occlusal stresses.

| Factor | Effect on dentin | Hypersensitivity consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Abrasion | Mechanical removal of cervical structure and smear layer. | Exposes tubules and increases stimulus transmission. |
| Erosion | Chemical demineralization and surface softening. | Maintains a permeable surface; brushing can amplify loss. |
| Attrition | Occlusal/incisal wear and dentin exposure. | Exposed dentin can become sensitive. |
| Gingival recession | Loss of root coverage. | Exposes cervical/root dentin. |

### General management of dentinal hypersensitivity associated with tooth wear

#### 1. Preventive measures

Eliminate the initiating mechanical or chemical insult.

Modify brushing technique and reduce abrasivity.

Control dietary acid frequency.

Manage intrinsic acid exposure.

Maintain periodontal health.

Use fluoride and appropriate desensitizing dentifrices.

#### 2. Conservative desensitization

Potassium nitrate for neural modulation.

Fluoride and stannous fluoride for mineral/tubule effects.

Oxalates and calcium phosphate agents for tubular occlusion.

Bioactive glass for mineral deposition.

Glutaraldehyde/HEMA for protein precipitation and permeability reduction.

Resin/dentin adhesive sealing for direct tubular occlusion.

#### 3. Restorative management

Sturdevant recommends restoration of abraded/eroded areas only when specific indications exist, including caries, structural compromise, intolerable sensitivity unresponsive to conservative treatment, periodontal contribution, excessive depth, or functional/esthetic requirements. Sensitive significant attrition may also require restoration.

#### 4. Surgical management

Where gingival recession is a major component and periodontal anatomy is favorable, root-coverage procedures may reduce exposed dentin and sensitivity.

#### 5. Laser management

Laser therapy can be considered in selected refractory cases as a mechanism-based adjunct. Nd:YAG, Er:YAG and CO2 lasers have been studied for tubular occlusion and desensitization.

### Clinical decision algorithm

What it should show: Identify lesion → determine dominant etiologic factors → control mechanical/chemical causes → desensitize → reassess → restore/surgically cover only when indicated.

### Important principle

The presence of a cervical defect does not itself justify restoration. Sturdevant explicitly emphasizes determining and eliminating the cause. Restoration is reserved for defined biologic, structural, periodontal, functional or esthetic indications.

### Conclusion

Abrasion, erosion and attrition are distinct forms of noncarious tooth wear, but they frequently interact. Their relationship with dentinal hypersensitivity is explained by exposure and increased patency of dentinal tubules. Management is therefore based on diagnosis of the wear mechanism, elimination of causative factors, preventive and desensitizing measures, and selective restorative or periodontal intervention.

All three can expose dentin and contribute to hypersensitivity.

Treat the cause before restoring the defect.

Do not restore every noncarious cervical lesion.

### COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

Using 'abrasion' as a generic term for all cervical wear.

Calling abfraction an established single-cause diagnosis; the project source presents it as a proposed stress mechanism that may interact with abrasion and acids.

Failing to distinguish noncarious lesions from cervical caries.

Treating hypersensitivity without controlling the causative wear process.

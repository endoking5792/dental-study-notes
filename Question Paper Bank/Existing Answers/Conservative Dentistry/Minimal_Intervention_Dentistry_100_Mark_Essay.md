## 1. INTRODUCTION

Dentistry has undergone a major paradigm shift from the traditional “extension for prevention” concept towards “prevention of extension”, preservation of tooth structure and control of the disease process.

Traditional operative dentistry largely followed the sequence:

Disease → surgical removal → cavity preparation → restoration

Modern dentistry recognizes that dental caries is a dynamic, multifactorial disease involving alternating demineralization and remineralization. Therefore, detection of an early lesion should ideally result in preventive or remineralizing treatment rather than immediate tooth preparation.

The contemporary philosophy is consequently:

Risk assessment → early detection → prevention/remineralization → lesion arrest → minimal operative intervention → repair rather than replacement → lifelong maintenance

The modern concept of minimal intervention dentistry (MID) therefore aims not simply to make a smaller cavity, but to manage the patient's disease while preserving maximum healthy tooth tissue and maintaining the tooth over its lifetime.

Burke described the transition from “extension for prevention” to “prevention of extension” as a central feature of minimal intervention dentistry.

## 2. DEFINITION

### Minimal Intervention Dentistry

Minimal Intervention Dentistry is a philosophy of caries management based on early detection of disease, assessment of individual caries risk, prevention and remineralization of early lesions, arrest of active disease, and the use of the least invasive operative procedure necessary to preserve maximum sound tooth structure.

It is therefore:

Preventive

Patient-centred

Risk-based

Biologically conservative

Adhesive

Repair-oriented

Maintenance-dependent

It should not be interpreted simply as “small cavity preparation.”

## 4. RATIONALE FOR MINIMAL INTERVENTION DENTISTRY

A restoration repairs the consequences of caries; it does not eliminate the disease itself.

Restorative intervention alone does not eliminate the etiologic factors responsible for caries. Effective preventive management requires assessment of microorganisms, oral hygiene, dietary factors, antimicrobial needs and patient motivation.

Furthermore:

every preparation removes some sound tooth structure;

repeated replacement of restorations tends to produce progressively larger restorations;

extensive preparations weaken the tooth;

operative procedures may injure the pulp;

restoration margins can develop recurrent/secondary caries;

no restorative material completely reproduces natural enamel and dentin.

The best restoration is often the restoration that can be avoided.

## 6. FUNDAMENTAL CONCEPTS OF MID

### A. Caries is a disease, not merely a cavity

The visible cavity represents only one stage of a much larger biological process. Caries development: Cariogenic biofilm + fermentable carbohydrate → acid production → pH fall → mineral loss → white-spot lesion → progression / arrest / remineralization → cavitation. Caries is dynamic, with periods of demineralization and remineralization. Disease progresses when demineralization predominates.

### B. Early caries should be treated non-operatively whenever possible

A non-cavitated lesion may be monitored; subjected to plaque control; treated with fluoride; sealed; infiltrated; managed by dietary modification; or managed with salivary and behavioural interventions. The objective is to arrest or reverse the lesion before restorative intervention becomes necessary.

### C. Sound tooth structure should be preserved

Modern adhesive materials permit restoration of small defects without the mechanical retention required by traditional non-adhesive restorations. Thus, preparation follows the lesion rather than an arbitrary geometric cavity design.

### D. Selective removal rather than routine complete removal

Deep carious lesions present a special challenge because aggressive complete excavation increases the possibility of pulpal exposure. Contemporary evidence supports conservative approaches such as selective caries removal, particularly in deep lesions.

### E. Repair rather than replacement

If only a localized portion of a restoration has failed, the entire restoration need not necessarily be removed. Localized defects can often be refurbished, resealed, repaired with composite, or repaired with an appropriate adhesive material. This preserves additional tooth structure.

## 7. PRINCIPLES OF MINIMAL INTERVENTION DENTISTRY

A useful conceptual sequence is:

Detect — Identify disease early.

Diagnose — Determine lesion activity, lesion severity and cavitation.

Assess risk — Consider previous caries experience, diet, fluoride exposure, oral hygiene, salivary function, socioeconomic and behavioural factors, medications, systemic conditions and plaque control.

Prevent — Control biofilm, dietary frequency, fluoride exposure and susceptible surfaces.

Arrest/remineralize — Use appropriate non-operative and micro-invasive approaches.

Restore conservatively — If cavitation compromises cleansability or function, perform the smallest appropriate restoration.

Repair — Repair localized restoration defects whenever possible.

Recall and reassess — MID is a lifelong management philosophy, not a one-time procedure.

## 12. OPERATIVE MINIMAL INTERVENTION TECHNIQUES

### A. Preventive resin restoration

A small carious defect is conservatively prepared and restored, while adjacent susceptible but non-cavitated fissures are sealed. Sturdevant describes the principle that after removal of a cavitated lesion, adjacent less-involved or at-risk areas should be managed more conservatively using sealants or minimally invasive preparations.

### B. Conservative cavity preparation

Characteristics:

preservation of marginal ridges

preservation of cuspal dentin

avoidance of unnecessary extension

rounded internal form

adhesive retention rather than mechanical retention whenever possible

#### Modified Class I preparation

For minimally involved Class I lesions, Sturdevant describes a modified, scooped-out preparation using a small round or inverted-cone instrument rather than a conventional geometric preparation.

## 13. SELECTIVE CARIES REMOVAL

This is one of the most important contemporary concepts.

### Principle

Remove infected/soft carious tissue at the periphery, sufficiently to obtain a sound/sealable margin, while preserving affected, remineralizable dentin close to the pulp when complete removal would risk exposure.

### Deep lesion

Peripheral caries removal → Establish clean/sealable margins → Selective removal toward pulp → Preserve affected dentin → Protect pulp → Seal with adhesive restoration

The rationale is to avoid unnecessary pulpal exposure.

The project sources document the historical evidence surrounding incomplete and stepwise excavation, including studies by Bjørndal, Maltz, Leksell and others.

## 14. STEPWISE EXCAVATION

### First visit

Remove peripheral caries.

Selectively remove caries near pulp.

Place suitable sealing/restorative material.

Temporarily or definitively seal.

### Re-entry, when indicated

Reassess.

Evaluate dentin.

Complete treatment if required.

Restore definitively.

### Advantage

The objective is to allow arrest of the lesion, reduction of bacterial activity, dentinal hardening, tertiary dentin formation and preservation of pulp vitality.

However, stepwise excavation is not synonymous with routine management of every deep lesion; contemporary evidence increasingly supports selective one-stage removal in many situations.

## 29. MID AND PULPAL PRESERVATION

Maintain pulp vitality whenever biologically possible.

Aggressive excavation of deep lesions may result in pulpal exposure, inflammation, loss of vitality and need for endodontic treatment.

Therefore, selective removal, appropriate liners/bases when indicated, adhesive sealing and careful thermal/mechanical management are integral to MID.

The project sources emphasize that operative procedures themselves can irritate the pulp and that unnecessary cutting should be avoided.

## 41. CURRENT CONTROVERSIES

### A. Complete versus selective caries removal

Traditional philosophy: Remove all carious tissue.

Contemporary philosophy: Preserve affected dentin when complete removal risks pulpal exposure.

Current evidence increasingly supports selective removal for appropriately selected deep lesions, but the exact approach depends on lesion depth, pulp status, restorability and ability to obtain a good seal.

### B. “Bioactive” does not automatically mean superior

A material may demonstrate ion release, apatite formation or remineralization in vitro without necessarily demonstrating superior long-term clinical performance.

Laboratory bioactivity ≠ proven clinical superiority.

### C. New diagnostic technology versus clinical diagnosis

Advanced devices can improve detection and monitoring, but they should supplement rather than replace clinical judgment. The project sources caution that the accuracy and clinical feasibility of several newer detection systems remain incompletely established.

Minimal Intervention Dentistry represents the modern philosophy of operative dental care in which the primary objective is not simply to restore a cavity but to control the disease process while preserving the maximum quantity and quality of natural tooth tissue.

It integrates early caries detection, risk assessment, prevention, remineralization, lesion arrest, micro-invasive treatment, selective caries removal, adhesive restorative dentistry, atraumatic restorative treatment, repair of defective restorations and lifelong maintenance.

The development of adhesive composites, GIC/RMGIC, bioactive and ion-releasing materials, improved diagnostic technologies, lasers, digital dentistry and AI-assisted caries detection has progressively expanded the scope of MID.

The future is likely to progress from:

“Remove and replace” → “Detect and prevent” → “Preserve and repair” → “Remineralize and regenerate.”

Thus, the ultimate goal of MID is not the smallest possible restoration, but the longest possible survival of the natural tooth with the least biological cost.

## HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

MID is a philosophy of disease control and tooth preservation, not merely small cavity preparation.

Caries is a dynamic disease involving demineralization and remineralization.

Early non-cavitated lesions should preferably receive non-operative/micro-invasive management.

Caries-risk assessment is fundamental to MID.

Selective caries removal is particularly important for deep lesions where pulpal exposure is a concern.

Adhesive dentistry permits conservative, lesion-specific preparations.

ART is an important minimally invasive treatment using hand excavation and adhesive fluoride-releasing material.

GIC/RMGIC are especially valuable because of adhesion and fluoride release.

Repair is preferred over replacement of suitable localized restoration defects.

The future of MID lies in AI-assisted diagnosis, optical monitoring, bioactive materials, smart materials and regenerative dentistry.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES

Defining MID merely as “small cavity preparation.”

Discussing only ART and ignoring disease management.

Forgetting caries-risk assessment.

Saying that all carious dentin must always be removed.

Treating “bioactive” as synonymous with “clinically superior.”

Ignoring repair of restorations.

Listing materials without explaining why they are suitable for MID.

Discussing lasers without mentioning their limitations.

Omitting recall and maintenance.

Describing prevention and restoration separately instead of presenting MID as a continuum of care.

## QUESTION 1

1. Define dental caries and discuss its etiological factors.

Marks: 100

Mark-weighting note: 100 marks = extended long essay. ## STUDY MODE

### Introduction

Dental caries is a chronic, biofilm-mediated, diet-associated disease in which repeated ecological challenges at the tooth–biofilm interface produce net mineral loss. The contemporary understanding moves beyond the older idea of a simple tooth defect or an infection attributable to one organism. Sturdevant nevertheless retains the clinically important formulation of caries as an infectious microbiologic disease producing localized dissolution and destruction of calcified dental tissues. The key postgraduate concept is that the lesion is the clinical consequence of a disease process occurring at the patient, biofilm, tooth and environmental levels. fileciteturn15file5

### Definition

**Dental caries is an infectious microbiologic disease of the teeth resulting in localized dissolution and destruction of the calcified tissues.** Sturdevant further stresses that cavitation is a sign of the underlying bacterial disease and that restorative treatment alone does not cure the carious process. fileciteturn15file5

### Modern etiologic concept

Caries is multifactorial and dynamic. It develops when the balance between pathological factors—acidogenic/aciduric biofilm, fermentable carbohydrate exposure, reduced salivary protection and susceptible tooth sites—and protective factors such as saliva, fluoride, plaque disruption and favorable diet shifts toward net demineralization. Summitt describes caries as alternating demineralization and remineralization, with disease progression occurring when demineralization outweighs remineralization. fileciteturn14file5

### Classical factors

The classical model may be expressed as **host (susceptible tooth + saliva) + microflora/biofilm + substrate + time**, with the modern model adding behavioral, socioeconomic, medical and environmental determinants. Sturdevant specifically identifies three factors that must be present simultaneously for caries: cariogenic bacteria, a susceptible tooth surface and nutrients supporting bacterial growth. fileciteturn14file0

### 1. Microbial / biofilm factors

Cariogenicity depends on ecological selection rather than simply the presence of a named pathogen. Acidogenic organisms metabolize fermentable carbohydrate and lower plaque pH; aciduric organisms survive and remain competitive in the acidic environment. Sturdevant identifies mutans streptococci as important primary organisms in initial coronal caries because of adhesion, acid production/tolerance, sucrose utilization and bacteriocin production; lactobacilli are emphasized in further dentinal destruction after cavitation. fileciteturn14file0 The contemporary ecological view in the 2019 source links frequent sugar exposure and acidic pH with dysbiosis of supragingival biofilms. fileciteturn15file9

### 2. Dietary / substrate factors

Fermentable carbohydrates provide substrate for bacterial acid production. Sucrose is especially important because it is readily fermented and also supports synthesis of extracellular and intracellular polysaccharides. Frequency and duration of exposure are critical; repeated exposures prolong periods of low plaque pH. The 2019 dietary review describes caries as a diet-related disease and emphasizes frequent/prolonged intake of added sugars as a risk factor. fileciteturn15file11

### 3. Tooth / host factors

A susceptible tooth provides a suitable surface for plaque retention and mineral dissolution. Important factors include tooth morphology, pits and fissures, proximal contacts, exposed root surfaces, enamel maturation and mineral composition. Deep fissures are particularly vulnerable because they can retain plaque and food and are difficult to cleanse. Sturdevant notes that fissures may trap plaque unless the enamel is fluoride-rich. fileciteturn15file10

### 4. Saliva

Saliva is a major protective host factor. It clears food, sugars and unattached organisms; buffers plaque acids; provides antimicrobial components; and supplies calcium and phosphate for remineralization. Reduced salivary flow therefore markedly increases caries risk. Sturdevant identifies bacterial clearance, buffering, antimicrobial activity and remineralization as major protective mechanisms. fileciteturn14file0

### 5. Fluoride exposure

Fluoride changes the balance toward remineralization and resistance to acid dissolution. Sturdevant describes increased resistance of tooth structure, remineralization of incipient lesions and antimicrobial effects as major anticaries actions. fileciteturn15file14 Seltzer and Bender emphasize that the major clinically relevant effects are topical: inhibition of demineralization and enhancement of remineralization. fileciteturn14file7

### 6. Oral hygiene and plaque retention

Plaque-free tooth surfaces do not decay in the usual caries process. Mechanical plaque disruption limits mature biofilm accumulation and reduces acidogenic selection. Brushing and interdental cleaning are therefore disease-control measures, not merely cosmetic procedures. Pits, fissures and inaccessible proximal sites remain higher-risk areas. fileciteturn15file7

### 7. Patient-related systemic and behavioral factors

Age, previous caries experience, medical illness, medications causing hyposalivation, impaired manual dexterity, cognitive limitations, diet, socioeconomic barriers and ability to access/comply with care modify risk. Sturdevant lists prior caries, frequent sucrose intake, low fluoride exposure, young/old age, reduced salivary function, high cariogenic bacterial counts and existing lesions as important risk indicators. fileciteturn15file6

### 8. Time and frequency

Time is not an independent etiologic agent but determines the cumulative effect of repeated episodes. Every carbohydrate exposure can produce a plaque-pH fall followed by recovery. Frequent exposures shorten the periods available for remineralization. Thus, **frequency of cariogenic challenge is often more important than the absolute amount consumed at a single event**. fileciteturn14file12

### 9. Socioeconomic and environmental determinants

Caries is socially patterned. Access to fluoride, preventive dental care, healthy foods, oral-health education, caregiver support and continuity of treatment can determine whether biologic risk becomes clinically expressed disease. The 2019 person-centered approach explicitly includes barriers to care, family/institutional support and ability to adhere to treatment in the risk-based plan. fileciteturn14file8

### Etiologic interaction

No single factor is sufficient in every patient. High sugar exposure may be compensated partly by good salivary function and fluoride; conversely, modest dietary risk can produce severe disease when salivary flow is markedly reduced. The clinician therefore identifies **modifiable pathological factors and protective factors** rather than merely counting lesions.

### Clinical interpretation

Etiology should be translated into risk assessment: What disease has occurred? What lesions are active? Why did they occur at these sites? What protective factors are deficient? What is likely to happen if nothing changes? This is the basis of personalized caries management described in the 2019 source. fileciteturn17file13

### Further high-yield learning points

Caries is a disease process, not merely a cavity.

Classical core: host + microflora + substrate + time.

Sucrose is especially cariogenic; frequency matters.

Saliva is a major protective determinant.

Fluoride acts mainly through topical mechanisms.

Previous caries is one of the strongest practical risk indicators.

Risk assessment must identify why disease occurred.

Restoration repairs damage; it does not cure the disease process.

**Saliva protects by clearance, buffering, antimicrobial action and remineralization.** Hyposalivation causes prolonged acid challenge and loss of calcium/phosphate availability. fileciteturn14file4

Fluoride increases resistance to demineralization, promotes remineralization and has antimicrobial effects. Its major clinically relevant role is topical and requires repeated exposure. fileciteturn14file7

Poor plaque control increases biofilm mass and ecological maturity. Mechanical plaque removal, interdental cleaning and sealing of susceptible fissures reduce disease expression. fileciteturn15file7

• Previous caries experience

• Young or older age

• Medical illness

• Medications causing dry mouth

• Reduced salivary flow

• High cariogenic bacterial burden

• Poor fluoride exposure

• Poor diet/oral hygiene

• Limited access or compliance. fileciteturn15file6

### H. Time

Repeated acid attacks determine cumulative mineral loss. **Frequent exposure is a major determinant** because the pH is repeatedly driven below the critical range before adequate recovery/remineralization.

Caries is best understood as a **biofilm-mediated, diet-driven dysbiosis occurring in a susceptible host**, modified by saliva, fluoride, behavior, medical status and social environment. Management must therefore target the **patient-level disease process as well as individual lesions**. Sturdevant explicitly states that restorative treatment does not cure the carious process. fileciteturn14file0

## QUESTION 2

2. Discuss the pathophysiology of dental caries.

Marks: 25

## STUDY MODE

### Core concept

Caries is a dynamic process of alternating demineralization and remineralization. Disease progresses when demineralization exceeds remineralization over time. Summitt emphasizes that caries diagnosis should therefore consider lesion activity and determinants, not merely the presence of a visible defect. fileciteturn14file5

### Initiation

A susceptible tooth surface becomes colonized by an adherent biofilm. Fermentable carbohydrate, especially sucrose, is metabolized to organic acids. Plaque pH falls and mineral begins to dissolve from enamel or exposed root/dentin.

### Enamel lesion

The earliest enamel lesion is subsurface demineralization beneath a relatively intact surface. The lesion can appear as a chalky, matte white spot after drying. The intact surface can remain mechanically sound while the subsurface mineral is lost. fileciteturn14file5

### Mineral dynamics

When substrate is removed and saliva buffers the acids, calcium and phosphate can diffuse back into the lesion. Fluoride promotes remineralization and formation of a more acid-resistant, fluoride-containing mineral phase. fileciteturn14file4

### Progression

Persistent acid challenge enlarges porosity and eventually compromises surface integrity, producing cavitation. Once cavitated, plaque retention increases and dentinal destruction becomes more clinically important.

### Dentin

Dentin is less mineralized and contains an organic collagen matrix. Caries progresses through tubules; bacterial invasion and demineralization are associated with zones of affected and infected dentin. Arrested dentin may become hard, dark and sclerotic. fileciteturn15file10

### Role of saliva

Salivary clearance, buffering and calcium/phosphate supply oppose lesion progression. Hyposalivation removes these protective mechanisms and may produce rapidly progressive disease. fileciteturn14file4

### Role of fluoride

Fluoride adsorbs to crystal surfaces, inhibits dissolution and enhances remineralization. It also interferes with bacterial metabolism at sufficient local concentrations. fileciteturn14file7

### Further high-yield learning points

Caries is dynamic, not static.

Subsurface enamel lesion precedes cavitation.

Saliva and fluoride can shift the balance toward remineralization.

Cavitation is a late event.

Lesion activity is clinically important.

### Definition and principle

**Dental caries is a dynamic process in which episodes of demineralization alternate with remineralization; progression occurs when mineral loss exceeds mineral gain.** fileciteturn14file5

### Sequence

**Cariogenic biofilm → fermentable carbohydrate → acid production → plaque-pH fall → mineral dissolution → subsurface lesion → lesion enlargement → surface breakdown/cavitation → dentinal involvement.**

### Enamel

Initial lesion is subsurface with a relatively intact surface. It clinically appears as a chalky/matte white spot after drying. Before cavitation, the lesion may arrest or remineralize if the environment is corrected. fileciteturn15file8

### Dentin

After cavitation, the lesion gains a protected plaque habitat. Demineralization and bacterial invasion progress through dentin; chronic/arrested lesions may become hard and sclerotic. fileciteturn15file10

### Protective reversal

Saliva buffers acid and supplies calcium/phosphate; fluoride inhibits demineralization and enhances remineralization. Therefore, **lesion activity is determined by the balance of pathological and protective factors.** fileciteturn14file4

## QUESTION 3

3. Discuss factors contributing to rampant caries.

Marks: 25

## STUDY MODE

### Definition

Rampant/acute caries is a rapidly damaging infectious caries process involving multiple teeth and usually multiple surfaces. Sturdevant associates it with gross dietary inadequacy, absence of oral hygiene, or systemic illness and stresses comprehensive patient evaluation. fileciteturn15file7

### Dietary factors

Frequent sucrose/fermentable-carbohydrate exposure, sweetened beverages, prolonged sipping, sticky foods and nocturnal feeding can maintain an acidic environment. Interproximal-predominant rampant disease may suggest diet as a major factor; cervical/interproximal disease suggests combined diet and hygiene effects. fileciteturn15file7

### Plaque/oral hygiene

Complete or severe deficiency in plaque control permits thick, mature biofilm and prolonged acid production. Difficult-to-clean fissures, proximal surfaces and cervical areas are particularly vulnerable.

### Salivary factors

Hyposalivation due to medication, systemic disease, irradiation or dehydration causes reduced clearance, buffering and remineralization and can precipitate rapidly progressive caries. fileciteturn14file1

### Medical factors

Systemic illness may alter diet, dexterity, oral hygiene, salivary function or medication exposure. Head-and-neck irradiation is a classic cause of severe xerostomia with rapid caries destruction. fileciteturn14file1

### Age/behavior

Young children may have immature enamel, poor plaque control and frequent dietary exposure. Older adults may have exposed roots, xerostomia, polypharmacy and reduced dexterity.

### Socioeconomic/caregiver factors

Poor access to preventive care, inadequate nutrition, low fluoride exposure and inadequate caregiver support can amplify risk. The 2019 person-centered model explicitly incorporates barriers to care and family/institutional support. fileciteturn17file13

### Clinical significance

Rampant caries is a **signal to search for the cause**, not merely a reason to restore multiple teeth. Disease control must precede or accompany definitive operative treatment.

### Further high-yield learning points

Rampant caries demands comprehensive patient evaluation.

Think diet + hygiene + saliva + fluoride + systemic factors.

Xerostomia can dramatically accelerate caries.

Do not restore every lesion without disease control.

## QUESTION 4

4. Discuss contemporary/current prevention and management strategies for dental caries.

Marks: 100

## STUDY MODE

### Contemporary philosophy

The contemporary approach is a shift from the historical “drill-and-fill” model to **patient-centered, risk-based, minimally invasive caries management**. Restorative treatment repairs consequences; disease control targets causation. Sturdevant explicitly states that management should be directed at the total patient level and that restorative treatment does not cure the carious process. fileciteturn14file0

### 1. Caries risk assessment

Assess previous caries, current lesion activity, diet, plaque control, fluoride exposure, salivary flow/buffering, medical history, medications, exposed roots, restorations, access and compliance. The 2019 decision-making framework asks what happens if nothing is done, identifies low/moderate/high risk, and selects individualized alternatives according to risk and lesion stage/activity. fileciteturn14file8

### 2. Lesion detection and diagnosis

Diagnosis integrates clinical examination, risk history, radiography where indicated, and assessment of lesion activity. Summitt emphasizes that detection is not equivalent to diagnosis; diagnosis estimates the dynamics and determinants of the disease. fileciteturn14file5

### 3. Dietary control

Reduce frequency of free-sugar exposure; avoid prolonged sipping/snacking; substitute noncariogenic foods; provide individualized dietary counseling. The 2019 dietary review emphasizes frequent/prolonged added-sugar intake as a risk factor and recommends diet screening and counseling. fileciteturn15file11

### 4. Biofilm control

Twice-daily toothbrushing with fluoride toothpaste, interdental cleaning and professional plaque-control reinforcement reduce cariogenic biofilm. Mechanical plaque disruption is preferred over indiscriminate antimicrobial use as a sustainable strategy. fileciteturn15file7

### 5. Fluoride

Fluoride toothpaste is foundational. Higher-concentration prescription dentifrices and professionally applied fluoride can be used according to risk and lesion type. The 2019 source describes 5000-ppm fluoride dentifrice/gel and 5% NaF varnish as effective strategies for appropriate high-risk/root or noncavitated lesion control. fileciteturn17file1

### 6. Saliva management

Identify xerogenic medications and medical causes; stimulate residual gland function when possible; encourage hydration and appropriate sugar-free chewing gum; use saliva substitutes/moisturizers when needed. Sturdevant notes physician consultation may be required when medication contributes to xerostomia. fileciteturn17file2

### 7. Sealants and surface protection

Deep pits and fissures can be sealed to reduce plaque retention. Sturdevant considers pit-and-fissure sealants highly effective for susceptible surfaces. fileciteturn15file7

### 8. Nonrestorative lesion management

Noncavitated lesions may be arrested/remineralized using plaque control, fluoride and other evidence-supported nonrestorative measures. The 2019 source stresses combining patient-level risk reduction with lesion-level nonrestorative treatment. fileciteturn17file3

### 9. SDF

Silver diamine fluoride provides a nonrestorative option for arresting cavitated or noncavitated lesions in selected patients, particularly those with high risk, special-care needs or limited treatment tolerance. Silver provides antibacterial activity; fluoride supports remineralization. The major esthetic limitation is black staining of arrested carious tissue. fileciteturn17file12

### 10. Minimally invasive operative management

When restoration is required, preserve sound tooth structure, control the lesion biologically, and obtain a durable peripheral seal. Contemporary tissue-removal concepts favor selective removal rather than unnecessarily complete excavation of deep carious dentin. The 2019 source emphasizes tight marginal sealing and tooth-preserving management. fileciteturn16file8

### 11. ART and ITR

Atraumatic restorative treatment uses hand excavation and an adhesive glass-ionomer restoration and is valuable where conventional care is difficult. ITR can temporarily stabilize multiple lesions in high-risk or special-care patients. fileciteturn15file2

### 12. Recall and monitoring

Caries management is longitudinal. Reassess lesion activity, plaque, diet, fluoride exposure, salivary function and restoration integrity. Schwendicke emphasizes that many strategies require repetition and that long-term consequences matter. fileciteturn17file4

### 13. Contemporary diagnostic technologies

When conventional examination is insufficient, adjunctive approaches include digital radiographic enhancement/subtraction, fiber-optic transillumination, quantitative light-induced fluorescence, laser fluorescence and electrical conductance/impedance. Seltzer and Bender note that the accuracy and clinical feasibility of many technologies require careful interpretation. fileciteturn14file10

### 14. Behavioral and person-centered care

Motivational communication, patient preferences, caregiver involvement and barriers to adherence should be incorporated. A technically excellent prescription can fail if it is not feasible for the patient.

### 15. Population prevention

Community water fluoridation, fluoride toothpaste, school-based programs, oral-health education and equitable access to preventive services reduce population disease burden. The contemporary goal is not simply to treat lesions but to reduce incidence over the life course.

### Further high-yield learning points

Restoration is not a cure for the disease.

Risk-based and lesion-based management must be combined.

Fluoride remains foundational.

Noncavitated lesions should not automatically be drilled.

SDF is useful for caries arrest but stains carious tissue.

Minimally invasive tissue removal preserves tooth structure.

Recall is part of treatment, not an optional afterthought.

### Introduction

Saliva is one of the most important natural protective factors against dental caries. Sturdevant states that adequate salivary functioning is crucial and identifies four principal mechanisms: bacterial clearance, buffering, antimicrobial activity and remineralization. fileciteturn14file0

### 1. Mechanical clearance

Salivary flow dilutes and clears sugars, acids and unattached microorganisms. Normal flow is particularly effective during mastication and stimulation. Adults produce roughly 1–1.5 L/day, although secretion varies considerably and is very low during sleep. fileciteturn14file1

### 2. Buffering

Bicarbonate is the major salivary buffer. It neutralizes plaque acids and assists recovery of plaque pH. Urea and other basic compounds can also increase pH through ammonia generation. fileciteturn14file2

### 3. Antimicrobial action

Saliva contains lysozyme, lactoferrin, lactoperoxidase, agglutinins, secretory IgA and other proteins that influence microbial adhesion and survival. fileciteturn14file1

### 4. Remineralization

Saliva is supersaturated with calcium and phosphate and supplies ions for repair of early demineralized lesions. Statherin and proline-rich proteins help maintain calcium/phosphate availability. fileciteturn14file10

### 5. Pellicle and lubrication

Salivary proteins contribute to the acquired pellicle, lubrication and protection of oral tissues. Some components reduce direct acid attack and modulate mineral exchange.

### 6. Saliva and fluoride

Saliva acts as a vehicle and reservoir for topical fluoride. Low concentrations repeatedly present in saliva/plaque assist in inhibiting demineralization and enhancing remineralization. fileciteturn14file7

### 7. Flow rate

Salivary flow rate is clinically critical. Reduced flow increases clearance time, prolongs low pH and decreases mineral availability. Summitt identifies unstimulated flow as particularly relevant to clearance and xerostomia. fileciteturn17file11

### 8. Hyposalivation and caries

Causes include medications, head-and-neck irradiation, systemic disease and autoimmune disorders. Rapidly progressive caries may occur because multiple salivary protective functions are simultaneously impaired. fileciteturn14file1

### 9. Clinical assessment

Assess subjective dry mouth, mucosal dryness, pooled saliva, salivary flow and buffering where appropriate. No single salivary test predicts caries perfectly; interpretation should be integrated with diet, plaque, fluoride and lesion history. fileciteturn15file4

### 10. Management

Identify and modify xerogenic medication when medically feasible; stimulate residual salivary function with sugar-free gum; maintain hydration; use saliva substitutes where needed; intensify fluoride and preventive care in high-risk patients. fileciteturn17file2

Fluoride facilitates calcium/phosphate deposition on partially demineralized crystal surfaces and promotes formation of a more acid-resistant fluoride-containing mineral phase. fileciteturn14file7

### 3. Antibacterial effects

Fluoride can enter bacterial cells as hydrogen fluoride at low pH and interfere with bacterial metabolism. Sturdevant also describes inhibition of glucosyltransferase and intracellular carbohydrate storage. fileciteturn15file14

### 4. Delivery

Community water fluoridation, fluoride toothpaste, mouthrinses, gels, varnishes and high-fluoride dentifrices are major routes. The clinically important concept is repeated topical availability.

### 5. Varnish

Fluoride varnish creates a concentrated fluoride reservoir at the enamel surface and is useful because of safety, ease of application and prolonged local exposure. fileciteturn17file0

### 6. High-risk care

The 2019 caries-management source supports high-fluoride dentifrice and professionally applied varnish for appropriate high-risk patients and lesion arrest. fileciteturn17file1

### 7. Limitations

Fluoride does not eliminate the need for dietary control, plaque disruption and risk-based care. Excess systemic exposure during enamel development can produce fluorosis; delivery must therefore be age/risk appropriate.

**Fluoride is a major anticaries agent whose most important clinical actions are topical: inhibition of demineralization and enhancement of remineralization.** fileciteturn14file7

1. **Inhibits enamel/dentin mineral dissolution.**

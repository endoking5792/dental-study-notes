QUESTION 1

Discuss the various non-carious tooth defects/lesions and their management in detail.

Marks: 100

STUDY MODE

### Introduction and terminology

Non-carious tooth surface loss refers to pathological or excessive loss of enamel, dentin and/or cementum that is not primarily produced by bacterial caries. The principal destructive processes relevant to operative dentistry are attrition, abrasion and erosion, with abfraction/stress-related mechanisms proposed for a subset of cervical lesions. [Certain]

The clinically important distinction is that these mechanisms frequently coexist. Acid-softened tooth structure is more susceptible to subsequent mechanical wear, so assigning every lesion to a single mechanism from its shape alone is unsafe. [Certain]

### Classification

| Lesion/process | Mechanism | Typical site/appearance | Diagnostic emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attrition | Tooth-to-tooth contact | Incisal/occlusal facets | Functional or parafunctional contact |
| Abrasion | External mechanical friction | Usually smooth or wedge-shaped cervical wear | Toothbrush, toothpaste, habits, occupational agents |
| Erosion | Chemical dissolution by acids without bacterial action | Smooth, glazed, rounded or cupped defects | Dietary or gastric acids |
| Abfraction/stress-related lesion | Proposed stress-corrosion/tooth-flexure mechanism | Often angular cervical notch | Occlusal stress plus other factors may contribute |
| Combined wear | Interaction of mechanisms | Mixed morphology | Common clinically |

### Etiology

Mechanical factors include tooth-to-tooth contact, parafunctional activity, toothbrush and dentifrice abrasion, abrasive substances, and habits such as holding objects between the teeth. [Certain]

Chemical factors include extrinsic dietary acids and intrinsic gastric acid. Summitt specifically identifies soft drinks, wines, fruits and fruit juices as extrinsic sources and reflux, bulimia and other gastric disorders as intrinsic sources. [Certain]

Biological modifiers include saliva, salivary flow, buffering and remineralization. Reduced salivary flow or buffering decreases the capacity to neutralize and repair acid challenges. [Certain]

The project sources emphasize that cervical lesions are multifactorial. Toothbrush/toothpaste abrasion, low pH and occlusal forces all have evidence supporting a contributory role, but no single factor explains all lesions. [Certain]

### Pathogenesis

Attrition produces loss where opposing tooth surfaces repeatedly contact, producing characteristic facets. [Certain]

Abrasion occurs when an external object or abrasive medium mechanically removes tooth structure. Cervical toothbrush abrasion classically produces a sharp wedge-shaped defect on the facial gingival portion of the tooth. [Certain]

Erosion is progressive chemical loss in the presence of acids. Exogenous acids may produce localized rounded or dished defects, whereas endogenous gastric acid can produce generalized lingual, incisal and occlusal changes. [Certain]

Acid exposure can soften enamel and dentin and markedly increase their susceptibility to brushing and other mechanical impacts. Delaying brushing after an acid challenge permits salivary remineralization and reduces mechanical loss. [Certain]

The abfraction hypothesis proposes that cervical tooth flexure under heavy eccentric occlusal loading creates tensile stress and microfracture, after which abrasion and acids may accelerate the defect. Summitt, however, states that the evidence supports a multifactorial process and that occlusion is not demonstrated to be a solitary cause. [Certain]

### Clinical features

Attrition: broad, relatively flat and matching facets are found on opposing contacting surfaces; parafunctional wear may be more pronounced and may coexist with fractures or cervical lesions. [Certain]

Abrasion: defects are commonly smooth and wedge-shaped, especially on facial cervical surfaces. Their distribution may correspond to the direction and reach of the causative habit or brushing technique. [Certain]

Erosion: surfaces are typically smooth and glassy. Posterior lesions may be concave or cupped, and restorations can appear as raised ‘islands’ above the eroded tooth structure. Gastric acid classically affects lingual maxillary surfaces. [Certain]

NCCLs may be V-shaped or saucer-shaped, but lesion morphology alone cannot reliably determine the cause. [Certain]

Progressive lesions can lead to dentin exposure, hypersensitivity, loss of tooth strength, pulpal complications and, in severe cases, tooth loss. [Certain]

### Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

Diagnosis begins with a detailed history: brushing method, toothbrush type, dentifrice amount and abrasivity, frequency of acidic food or drink intake, beverage-swishing habits, occupational exposures, parafunctional activity, reflux symptoms, vomiting/regurgitation, medications and relevant systemic factors. [Certain]

Clinical examination should document site, symmetry, surface texture, depth, shape, colour, relationship to gingival recession, occlusal wear facets, opposing tooth wear, restorations and periodontal status. [Certain]

The clinician should distinguish non-carious lesions from caries, developmental defects, fractures, cervical resorption and iatrogenic defects. A root surface that is discoloured but hard and sclerotic does not automatically require restorative treatment, whereas softness supports root caries. [Certain]

Pulp sensibility testing is indicated when lesions are deep, symptoms are significant or pulpal involvement is suspected. Radiographs are useful when depth, fracture, pulpal proximity or another pathology needs assessment. [Certain]

For generalized tooth wear, compare current tooth form with old photographs, study models or sequential clinical records when available, because progression is more informative than a single snapshot. [Likely]

### Management

The first principle is etiologic control rather than immediate restoration. The causative factor should be identified and reduced before definitive rehabilitation. [Certain]

For abrasion, modify brushing technique, reduce force and use an appropriate low-abrasivity dentifrice. [Certain]

For erosion, reduce frequency and duration of acid exposure, avoid swishing acidic drinks, modify dietary behaviour, and address intrinsic acid sources medically when indicated. [Certain]

After an acid challenge, immediate vigorous brushing should be avoided because softened tooth structure is more vulnerable to mechanical wear. Summitt describes delaying brushing by at least 1 hour as a strategy to minimize abrasion after demineralization. [Certain]

If abfraction/stress contribution is suspected, occlusal assessment is indicated and an occlusal appliance may be considered for appropriate parafunctional patients. However, irreversible occlusal adjustment should not be presented as a universally proven treatment for NCCLs because controlled clinical evidence for improved Class V longevity after occlusal adjustment was lacking in the cited textbook. [Certain]

Restoration is considered when there is significant or progressing tooth loss, persistent sensitivity, compromised esthetics, threat to tooth strength or inability to control progression by conservative measures. [Certain]

Restorative options include resin composite, flowable composite in selected situations, conventional glass ionomer, resin-modified glass ionomer, compomer and, in selected cases, sandwich/open-sandwich approaches. [Certain]

Extensive generalized wear requires global planning. Interarch space, occlusal relationships, pulpal status and the possible need for changes in vertical dimension must be assessed before extensive rehabilitation. [Certain]

### Dentin hypersensitivity

Dentin hypersensitivity is a short, sharp pain from exposed dentin in response to thermal, tactile, osmotic, evaporative or chemical stimuli when another pathology does not account for the pain. Ingle identifies the hydrodynamic theory as the most widely accepted mechanism: stimulus-induced movement of fluid in dentinal tubules activates mechanosensitive neural structures. [Certain]

Management is directed either at reducing dentinal fluid movement by tubule occlusion or reducing neural excitability. Fluorides, desensitizing formulations, dentin adhesives and other tubule-occluding approaches are used according to the clinical situation. [Certain]

Source trace: Ingle’s Endodontics, 7th ed., Chapter 7, “The Science of Endodontics,” pp. 207–208 for dentin hypersensitivity mechanisms and management; Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, 4th ed., Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 809–810 for cervical sensitivity.

### Restorative decision-making

| Indication for restoration | Clinical reasoning |
|---|---|
| Progression cannot be adequately controlled | Restoration protects remaining structure but etiologic control remains necessary. |
| Persistent significant sensitivity | Restore when conservative desensitization is inadequate. |
| Esthetic concern | Patient-centered indication when lesion is visible and unacceptable. |
| Threat to tooth strength | Deep/extensive loss may compromise the coronal-radicular unit. |
| Pulpal proximity or risk | Deep lesions may require protection and careful vitality assessment. |
| Minimal asymptomatic stable lesion | Prefer monitoring and prevention rather than automatic restoration. |

The American Academy of Operative Dentistry recommendations cited by Summitt emphasize inability to control progression, unacceptable esthetics, significant sensitivity and threat to tooth strength as principal reasons to restore an NCCL. [Certain]

Source trace: American Academy of Operative Dentistry (2003), “Non-carious cervical lesions: Recommendations for clinical practice,” as referenced in Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, 4th ed., Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 793 and 810.

### Prognosis and maintenance

The longevity of a restoration is not equivalent to cure of the disease process. Continued brushing abrasion, acid exposure, parafunction, gingival recession or poor plaque control can cause recurrent tooth structure loss or restoration failure. [Certain]

Recall should therefore assess lesion progression, marginal integrity, sensitivity, periodontal status, occlusion and continuing etiologic exposures. [Certain]

### Introduction

Non-carious tooth destruction is clinically important because tooth structure may be lost without bacterial caries. The major processes are attrition, abrasion and erosion; cervical lesions may additionally be described using the term abfraction when stress-related tooth flexure is proposed. The most defensible modern textbook position is that many lesions are multifactorial and that treatment should address the cause before rebuilding the lost structure. [Certain]

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature | Important action |
|---|---|---|
| Caries | Softened/infected dentin rather than simply hard wear surface | Assess activity and remove/manage caries as indicated |
| NCCL / wear | Hard, non-carious surface; characteristic distribution/history | Identify etiologic mechanism |
| Developmental enamel defect | Present from tooth formation; hard, stable surface | Do not mistake for acquired wear |
| Cervical resorption | Irregular resorptive defect; radiographic evaluation important | Endodontic/periodontal assessment as appropriate |
| Fracture/crack | Line or segmental defect; symptoms may be biting related | Transillumination, bite test, radiography as indicated |

### Management

Management follows a hierarchy: diagnose → identify cause → control cause → manage symptoms → monitor → restore only when indicated → maintain. [Certain]

For abrasion, modify brushing force and technique, reduce unnecessary dentifrice and remove abrasive habits. For erosion, reduce acid frequency, avoid swishing acidic beverages, use appropriate dietary strategies and investigate intrinsic acid sources. [Certain]

For active erosion followed by brushing, delaying brushing for at least 1 hour is specifically supported in Summitt’s discussion because softened enamel is particularly vulnerable to mechanical impact immediately after demineralization. [Certain]

For suspected parafunction or stress-related contribution, assess occlusion and consider an occlusal appliance when clinically appropriate. Avoid presenting routine irreversible occlusal adjustment as evidence-based treatment for all NCCLs. [Certain]

### Restorative treatment

Restoration is indicated when progression cannot be adequately controlled, esthetics are unacceptable to the patient, significant sensitivity persists, or the depth of the lesion threatens tooth strength. Minimal stable asymptomatic lesions are generally managed conservatively and reviewed. [Certain]

For extensive occlusal wear, evaluate interarch space and occlusion before restoring. If space is inadequate, options include creating restorative space or choosing a conservative restorative approach that does not require excessive reduction. Generalized wear may require interdisciplinary rehabilitation rather than isolated restorations. [Certain]

### NCCL/Class V restorative technique

Isolate the field and obtain adequate access and moisture control. Rubber dam is ideal for direct adhesive restorations, while retraction cord and other adjuncts may be used in selected cervical lesions. [Certain]

Remove only unsupported or compromised tooth structure. For NCCLs, little or no preparation may be necessary for bonded composite. [Certain]

Where composite is selected, bevel enamel margins when indicated for enamel rod exposure and esthetic blending; do not bevel a gingival margin terminating on cementum. [Certain]

Apply the adhesive system meticulously, with special attention to sclerotic dentin because it is more resistant to acid conditioning and can provide lower or variable bonding efficacy. [Certain]

Place composite in suitable increments. Summitt recommends increments no thicker than about 2 mm for light curing and recommends incremental placement for moderate to large restorations to reduce polymerization-shrinkage effects. [Certain]

Finish carefully, particularly at the gingival margin, and polish without damaging the cervical substrate. [Certain]

|---|---|---|---|
| Composite | Esthetic Class V/NCCL | Esthetics, conservative adhesive retention | Sclerotic dentin bonding and polymerization stress |
| RMGI | Root/cervical, high caries risk, low stress | Fluoride release, favourable cervical use, good clinical retention | Lower esthetics/wear resistance than composite |
| Conventional GIC | Root surfaces, selected cervical cases | Chemical adhesion, fluoride release | Brittleness, acid-related surface degradation |
| Compomer | Selected Class V/NCCL | Handling, fluoride release, esthetics | Bonding required; marginal integrity concerns |
| Sandwich | Subgingival/high-risk cervical case | Combines RMGI/GIC with composite | Technique sensitive |

### Periodontal and surgical management

When NCCLs are associated with gingival recession, connective tissue grafting or coronally advanced flap procedures may be considered in appropriate cases. Summitt describes a maximum coronal root coverage approach for determining whether soft-tissue coverage alone is likely to resolve the exposed root or whether restoration should precede surgery. [Certain]

For subgingival cervical lesions, miniflap, gingivoplasty or conventional flap surgery may be needed for access. Biologic width and the periodontal attachment must be protected, and overhanging restorations should be avoided. [Certain]

### Complications

Persistent hypersensitivity despite treatment. [Certain]

Marginal leakage or loss of retention. [Certain]

Progressive wear of tooth or restoration. [Certain]

Plaque retention and gingival inflammation from overcontoured/overhanging restorations. [Certain]

Pulpal irritation or exposure in very deep lesions. [Certain]

Failure to control the cause, leading to recurrence or new lesions. [Certain]

### Conclusion

The key to non-carious tooth destruction is etiologic diagnosis rather than morphology-based labelling. The lesion should be viewed as the end result of interacting mechanical, chemical and biological factors. Conservative control of the cause, management of sensitivity and selective restoration provide the most defensible operative strategy. [Certain]

### Dentin hypersensitivity

### Introduction

Non-carious tooth destruction is clinically important because tooth structure may be lost without bacterial caries. The major processes are attrition, abrasion and erosion; cervical lesions may additionally be described using the term abfraction when stress-related tooth flexure is proposed. The most defensible modern textbook position is that many lesions are multifactorial and that treatment should address the cause before rebuilding the lost structure. [Certain]

### Etiology and pathogenesis

Mechanical factors include tooth-to-tooth contact, parafunctional activity, toothbrush and dentifrice abrasion, abrasive substances, and habits such as holding objects between the teeth. [Certain]

Chemical factors include extrinsic dietary acids and intrinsic gastric acid. Summitt specifically identifies soft drinks, wines, fruits and fruit juices as extrinsic sources and reflux, bulimia and other gastric disorders as intrinsic sources. [Certain]

Biological modifiers include saliva, salivary flow, buffering and remineralization. Reduced salivary flow or buffering decreases the capacity to neutralize and repair acid challenges. [Certain]

The project sources emphasize that cervical lesions are multifactorial. Toothbrush/toothpaste abrasion, low pH and occlusal forces all have evidence supporting a contributory role, but no single factor explains all lesions. [Certain]

Attrition produces loss where opposing tooth surfaces repeatedly contact, producing characteristic facets. [Certain]

Abrasion occurs when an external object or abrasive medium mechanically removes tooth structure. Cervical toothbrush abrasion classically produces a sharp wedge-shaped defect on the facial gingival portion of the tooth. [Certain]

Erosion is progressive chemical loss in the presence of acids. Exogenous acids may produce localized rounded or dished defects, whereas endogenous gastric acid can produce generalized lingual, incisal and occlusal changes. [Certain]

Acid exposure can soften enamel and dentin and markedly increase their susceptibility to brushing and other mechanical impacts. Delaying brushing after an acid challenge permits salivary remineralization and reduces mechanical loss. [Certain]

The abfraction hypothesis proposes that cervical tooth flexure under heavy eccentric occlusal loading creates tensile stress and microfracture, after which abrasion and acids may accelerate the defect. Summitt, however, states that the evidence supports a multifactorial process and that occlusion is not demonstrated to be a solitary cause. [Certain]

### Management

Management follows a hierarchy: diagnose → identify cause → control cause → manage symptoms → monitor → restore only when indicated → maintain. [Certain]

For abrasion, modify brushing force and technique, reduce unnecessary dentifrice and remove abrasive habits. For erosion, reduce acid frequency, avoid swishing acidic beverages, use appropriate dietary strategies and investigate intrinsic acid sources. [Certain]

For active erosion followed by brushing, delaying brushing for at least 1 hour is specifically supported in Summitt’s discussion because softened enamel is particularly vulnerable to mechanical impact immediately after demineralization. [Certain]

For suspected parafunction or stress-related contribution, assess occlusion and consider an occlusal appliance when clinically appropriate. Avoid presenting routine irreversible occlusal adjustment as evidence-based treatment for all NCCLs. [Certain]

### Restorative treatment

Restoration is indicated when progression cannot be adequately controlled, esthetics are unacceptable to the patient, significant sensitivity persists, or the depth of the lesion threatens tooth strength. Minimal stable asymptomatic lesions are generally managed conservatively and reviewed. [Certain]

For extensive occlusal wear, evaluate interarch space and occlusion before restoring. If space is inadequate, options include creating restorative space or choosing a conservative restorative approach that does not require excessive reduction. Generalized wear may require interdisciplinary rehabilitation rather than isolated restorations. [Certain]

### NCCL/Class V restorative technique

Isolate the field and obtain adequate access and moisture control. Rubber dam is ideal for direct adhesive restorations, while retraction cord and other adjuncts may be used in selected cervical lesions. [Certain]

Remove only unsupported or compromised tooth structure. For NCCLs, little or no preparation may be necessary for bonded composite. [Certain]

Where composite is selected, bevel enamel margins when indicated for enamel rod exposure and esthetic blending; do not bevel a gingival margin terminating on cementum. [Certain]

Apply the adhesive system meticulously, with special attention to sclerotic dentin because it is more resistant to acid conditioning and can provide lower or variable bonding efficacy. [Certain]

Place composite in suitable increments. Summitt recommends increments no thicker than about 2 mm for light curing and recommends incremental placement for moderate to large restorations to reduce polymerization-shrinkage effects. [Certain]

Finish carefully, particularly at the gingival margin, and polish without damaging the cervical substrate. [Certain]

### Periodontal and surgical management

When NCCLs are associated with gingival recession, connective tissue grafting or coronally advanced flap procedures may be considered in appropriate cases. Summitt describes a maximum coronal root coverage approach for determining whether soft-tissue coverage alone is likely to resolve the exposed root or whether restoration should precede surgery. [Certain]

For subgingival cervical lesions, miniflap, gingivoplasty or conventional flap surgery may be needed for access. Biologic width and the periodontal attachment must be protected, and overhanging restorations should be avoided. [Certain]

### Complications

Persistent hypersensitivity despite treatment. [Certain]

Marginal leakage or loss of retention. [Certain]

Progressive wear of tooth or restoration. [Certain]

Plaque retention and gingival inflammation from overcontoured/overhanging restorations. [Certain]

Pulpal irritation or exposure in very deep lesions. [Certain]

Failure to control the cause, leading to recurrence or new lesions. [Certain]

### Conclusion

The key to non-carious tooth destruction is etiologic diagnosis rather than morphology-based labelling. The lesion should be viewed as the end result of interacting mechanical, chemical and biological factors. Conservative control of the cause, management of sensitivity and selective restoration provide the most defensible operative strategy. [Certain]

Acid exposure can soften enamel and dentin and markedly increase their susceptibility to brushing and other mechanical impacts. Delaying brushing after an acid challenge permits salivary remineralization and reduces mechanical loss. [Certain]

The abfraction hypothesis proposes that cervical tooth flexure under heavy eccentric occlusal loading creates tensile stress and microfracture, after which abrasion and acids may accelerate the defect. Summitt, however, states that the evidence supports a multifactorial process and that occlusion is not demonstrated to be a solitary cause. [Certain]

### Clinical features

Attrition: broad, relatively flat and matching facets are found on opposing contacting surfaces; parafunctional wear may be more pronounced and may coexist with fractures or cervical lesions. [Certain]

Abrasion: defects are commonly smooth and wedge-shaped, especially on facial cervical surfaces. Their distribution may correspond to the direction and reach of the causative habit or brushing technique. [Certain]

Erosion: surfaces are typically smooth and glassy. Posterior lesions may be concave or cupped, and restorations can appear as raised ‘islands’ above the eroded tooth structure. Gastric acid classically affects lingual maxillary surfaces. [Certain]

NCCLs may be V-shaped or saucer-shaped, but lesion morphology alone cannot reliably determine the cause. [Certain]

Progressive lesions can lead to dentin exposure, hypersensitivity, loss of tooth strength, pulpal complications and, in severe cases, tooth loss. [Certain]

### Diagnosis

Diagnosis begins with a detailed history: brushing method, toothbrush type, dentifrice amount and abrasivity, frequency of acidic food or drink intake, beverage-swishing habits, occupational exposures, parafunctional activity, reflux symptoms, vomiting/regurgitation, medications and relevant systemic factors. [Certain]

Clinical examination should document site, symmetry, surface texture, depth, shape, colour, relationship to gingival recession, occlusal wear facets, opposing tooth wear, restorations and periodontal status. [Certain]

The clinician should distinguish non-carious lesions from caries, developmental defects, fractures, cervical resorption and iatrogenic defects. A root surface that is discoloured but hard and sclerotic does not automatically require restorative treatment, whereas softness supports root caries. [Certain]

Pulp sensibility testing is indicated when lesions are deep, symptoms are significant or pulpal involvement is suspected. Radiographs are useful when depth, fracture, pulpal proximity or another pathology needs assessment. [Certain]

For generalized tooth wear, compare current tooth form with old photographs, study models or sequential clinical records when available, because progression is more informative than a single snapshot. [Likely]

### Management

Management follows a hierarchy: diagnose → identify cause → control cause → manage symptoms → monitor → restore only when indicated → maintain. [Certain]

For abrasion, modify brushing force and technique, reduce unnecessary dentifrice and remove abrasive habits. For erosion, reduce acid frequency, avoid swishing acidic beverages, use appropriate dietary strategies and investigate intrinsic acid sources. [Certain]

For active erosion followed by brushing, delaying brushing for at least 1 hour is specifically supported in Summitt’s discussion because softened enamel is particularly vulnerable to mechanical impact immediately after demineralization. [Certain]

For suspected parafunction or stress-related contribution, assess occlusion and consider an occlusal appliance when clinically appropriate. Avoid presenting routine irreversible occlusal adjustment as evidence-based treatment for all NCCLs. [Certain]

### Restorative treatment

Restoration is indicated when progression cannot be adequately controlled, esthetics are unacceptable to the patient, significant sensitivity persists, or the depth of the lesion threatens tooth strength. Minimal stable asymptomatic lesions are generally managed conservatively and reviewed. [Certain]

For extensive occlusal wear, evaluate interarch space and occlusion before restoring. If space is inadequate, options include creating restorative space or choosing a conservative restorative approach that does not require excessive reduction. Generalized wear may require interdisciplinary rehabilitation rather than isolated restorations. [Certain]

### NCCL/Class V restorative technique

Isolate the field and obtain adequate access and moisture control. Rubber dam is ideal for direct adhesive restorations, while retraction cord and other adjuncts may be used in selected cervical lesions. [Certain]

Remove only unsupported or compromised tooth structure. For NCCLs, little or no preparation may be necessary for bonded composite. [Certain]

Where composite is selected, bevel enamel margins when indicated for enamel rod exposure and esthetic blending; do not bevel a gingival margin terminating on cementum. [Certain]

Apply the adhesive system meticulously, with special attention to sclerotic dentin because it is more resistant to acid conditioning and can provide lower or variable bonding efficacy. [Certain]

Place composite in suitable increments. Summitt recommends increments no thicker than about 2 mm for light curing and recommends incremental placement for moderate to large restorations to reduce polymerization-shrinkage effects. [Certain]

Finish carefully, particularly at the gingival margin, and polish without damaging the cervical substrate. [Certain]

Conservative control of the cause, management of sensitivity and selective restoration provide the most defensible operative strategy. [Certain]

NCCLs are acquired cervical defects produced by non-carious processes. Their pathogenesis is multifactorial, with abrasion, erosion and occlusal stress proposed as interacting contributors. [Certain]

### Pathogenesis

1. Chemical component: dietary or gastric acids dissolve mineralized tooth structure and create a softened surface. [Certain]

### Conceptual study mode

The central issue in cervical restoration is not simply material strength. | Composite | Adhesive conservation and esthetics | Sclerotic dentin and polymerization stress | Primary esthetic option |
| RMGI | Fluoride release, chemical adhesion, cervical performance | Lower esthetics/wear resistance | Strong choice for root/cervical and high caries risk |
| Conventional GIC | Chemical adhesion and fluoride | Brittleness and surface degradation | Selected low-stress cervical use |
| Compomer | Composite-like handling with fluoride release | Marginal integrity concerns | Selected Class V cases |

Conservative tooth preparation. [Certain]

### Composite resin

Composite provides superior esthetics and permits minimal preparation because retention is primarily adhesive. However, sclerotic dentin is more resistant to acid conditioning and may provide reduced or variable bond strength. Meticulous adhesive application is therefore essential. [Certain]

For moderate to large Class V restorations, incremental placement is recommended to reduce polymerization-shrinkage effects. Enamel margins may be beveled where appropriate, but gingival margins on cementum should not be beveled. [Certain]

### RMGI

RMGI combines the glass-ionomer acid-base reaction with a resin component. In the project sources it is recommended for Class V and low-stress cervical applications, particularly where caries activity is significant. Its fluoride release and favourable cervical clinical performance are important advantages. [Certain]

Sturdevant notes that RMGI is stronger and more resistant to dehydration and cracking during setting than conventional glass ionomer. [Certain]

### Conventional GIC

Conventional GIC offers chemical adhesion and fluoride release but has lower strength and poorer esthetics. It is therefore particularly useful for root surfaces and low-stress situations rather than high-stress restorations. [Certain]

### Compomer

Compomers are polyacid-modified resin composites. They require an adhesive system and are more resin-like than conventional glass ionomers. Their handling and esthetics are favourable, but long-term marginal integrity has been less favourable than resin composite in the cited Class V literature. [Certain]

### Flowable composite

Flowable composites have lower filler loading, lower modulus, higher polymerization shrinkage and higher thermal expansion than conventional composites. Although flexibility was proposed as an advantage in cervical lesions, clinical trials cited by Summitt did not demonstrate superior performance over stiffer restorations. [Certain]

### Sandwich/open-sandwich technique

A glass-ionomer or RMGI cervical layer can be combined with a composite veneer. The ionomeric component may provide fluoride release and a favourable cervical/root interface, while composite improves esthetics, surface finish and abrasion resistance. [Certain]

### Critical appraisal

| Clinical situation | Most defensible choice | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| High esthetic demand, dry field | Composite | Excellent esthetics and adhesive conservation |
| High caries risk/root surface | RMGI/GIC | Fluoride release and cervical/root utility |
| Subgingival cervical margin | RMGI or sandwich in selected cases | Cervical/root substrate and tissue considerations |
| Small lesion | Bonded composite or no restoration | Avoid unnecessary preparation |
| Large lesion | Composite with incremental placement or combined technique | Control shrinkage and provide adequate bulk |
| Flexure cited as reason for flowable | Do not assume superiority | Clinical evidence does not support the theoretical advantage |

### Conclusion

No cervical restorative material is universally superior. Composite is the principal esthetic adhesive option, whereas RMGI/GIC are valuable for root and high-caries-risk cervical applications. Material selection must follow lesion location, caries risk, esthetics, moisture control, mechanical demand and substrate. [Certain]

## QUESTION 1 — 50 MARKS

Discuss remineralizing agents in caries management.

## STUDY MODE

### Introduction

Remineralization is the natural repair process of a carious lesion. Summitt’s describes it as replacement of minerals lost during demineralization during periods of neutral pH, using calcium and phosphate supplied principally by saliva. Fluoride facilitates this repair and can arrest enamel caries by promoting a harder, less permeable surface. fileciteturn8file5

### Definition and biological basis

Remineralization is the redeposition of mineral into partially demineralized dental hard tissue, using the residual crystalline framework of the lesion as a nucleation substrate. Noncavitated enamel retains much of its original crystal framework, permitting calcium and phosphate from saliva to precipitate onto reactive crystal surfaces. Trace fluoride greatly enhances this process. fileciteturn12file4

### Caries balance

Caries is governed by a dynamic balance. Pathological influences include acid-producing bacteria, frequent fermentable-carbohydrate exposure and reduced salivary flow/function. Protective influences include normal saliva, fluoride and antibacterial factors. Remineralizing therapy attempts to shift this balance toward mineral gain, but it cannot compensate indefinitely for persistent high-frequency acid challenge. fileciteturn8file0

### Requirements for effective remineralization

A noncavitated or structurally maintainable lesion with residual crystal framework.

Availability of calcium and phosphate ions.

A favorable oral pH with adequate periods of recovery from acid challenge.

Fluoride availability to enhance mineral precipitation and acid resistance.

Adequate salivary flow and buffering.

Reduction of plaque stagnation and frequent fermentable-carbohydrate exposure.

### 1. Fluoride

Fluoride is the most established remineralizing agent in routine dentistry. Sturdevant describes three anticaries mechanisms: enhancement of precipitation of fluorapatite from calcium and phosphate in saliva, remineralization of incipient noncavitated lesions, and antimicrobial effects. fileciteturn12file6

#### Mechanism

Fluoride present during the demineralization–remineralization cycle enhances precipitation of mineral into the lesion.

Remineralized enamel becomes more resistant to subsequent acid attack.

Fluoride can be concentrated at the tooth surface and function as a local reservoir.

Low concentrations can inhibit aspects of bacterial metabolism; higher topical concentrations have stronger antimicrobial effects. fileciteturn12file8

#### Clinical forms

Fluoride dentifrices for frequent topical exposure.

Fluoride varnishes for high-risk patients and localized lesion management.

Fluoride mouthrinses and gels in selected risk groups.

High-fluoride dentifrices for selected high-risk/root-caries patients.

Fluoride-releasing restorative materials as an adjunct, particularly relevant to root-surface caries.

### 2. Calcium and phosphate

Calcium and phosphate are the fundamental mineral components required for remineralization. Saliva normally supplies these ions. Summitt’s notes that the mineral uptake/loss balance shifts toward remineralization when adequate calcium is present. fileciteturn8file5

### 3. CPP-ACP

Craig’s Restorative Dental Materials describes casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP) as a calcium-phosphate remineralization technology. CPP stabilizes high levels of calcium and phosphate and has been reported in the source to remineralize enamel subsurface lesions. A randomized clinical trial cited in Craig’s found an 18% reduction in caries progression after 24 months when CPP-ACP was added to sugar-free gum; paste formulations were reported to reverse early lesions and stabilize progression. fileciteturn8file0

#### Clinical interpretation

CPP-ACP should be written as an adjunctive calcium-phosphate technology rather than as a replacement for fluoride, because the project sources identify fluoride as a central protective/remineralizing factor.

### 4. Bioactive glass / calcium sodium phosphosilicate

Craig’s describes bioactive glass based on calcium sodium phosphosilicate. In dentifrice form it can deposit on dentin and mechanically occlude dentinal tubules; when combined with therapeutic fluoride, it has increased remineralization of caries lesions in situ. fileciteturn8file0

### 5. Calcium-silicate-based materials

Calcium-silicate materials have been developed principally as liners with objectives including pulp protection and possible remineralization of overlying dentin. Craig’s notes investigation of their interaction with odontoblast cells and potential stimulation of mineralization-related extracellular matrix formation. The ultimate success of these approaches was described as not yet established in the source. fileciteturn8file0

### 6. Silver diamine fluoride as a remineralizing/arresting adjunct

Craig’s identifies silver diamine fluoride (SDF) as a material suggested to cause remineralization, especially of frank caries lesions, while also having a strong antibacterial effect. The source describes an approximately 30% solution with about 25% silver and 5% fluoride in water, with an alkaline pH. Its major practical limitation is black staining of tooth structure. fileciteturn8file0

### 7. Saliva as the natural remineralizing system

Calcium and phosphate provide mineral ions.

Buffering restores a more favorable pH.

Flow clears acids and fermentable substrates.

Salivary proteins contribute to maintenance of the tooth–fluid mineral environment.

Reduced salivary flow therefore compromises several components of remineralization simultaneously.

### Indications for remineralizing therapy

Noncavitated enamel lesions/white-spot lesions.

Early proximal lesions without clinical cavitation.

Early smooth-surface lesions.

Selected root-surface lesions, especially where a cavitated lesion can be maintained and rehardening is possible.

High-caries-risk patients as part of an overall disease-control plan.

### When remineralization is unlikely to be sufficient

Established cavitation with plaque stagnation that cannot be controlled.

Structural breakdown that compromises function or cleansability.

Deep lesions requiring restorative intervention.

Persistent pathological conditions that are not being corrected—especially frequent carbohydrate exposure, poor plaque control and severe salivary dysfunction.

### Advantages

Conserves sound tooth structure.

Can arrest or reverse early lesions.

Noninvasive and biologically conservative.

Can be incorporated into daily preventive care.

May reduce the need for restorative intervention.

### Limitations

Requires patient adherence and a favorable oral environment.

Cannot restore lost anatomy after cavitation.

Effectiveness varies with lesion depth, activity and agent.

Some newer calcium-phosphate or bioactive technologies have less mature evidence than fluoride.

Cosmetic limitations occur with SDF because of staining.

### Recent/advancing concepts supported by the project

The project sources show the transition from a purely restorative approach toward early lesion detection and remineralization. Sturdevant explicitly notes that radiographically visible lesions may still be noncavitated and that more sensitive diagnostic methods can permit earlier use of remineralizing strategies. Digital radiography, quantitative light-induced fluorescence, electrical conductivity and ultrasonography are identified as emerging/adjunctive detection approaches in the source. fileciteturn12file2

### Clinical significance

The most important clinical principle is that remineralizing therapy is not an isolated product choice. It is the chemical component of a larger disease-control strategy. Fluoride, saliva, calcium/phosphate availability, plaque control and diet must act together.

### Conclusion

Remineralization represents the biologic basis for minimally invasive caries management. Fluoride remains the principal evidence-based agent, while calcium/phosphate technologies such as CPP-ACP and bioactive glass may serve as adjuncts. Successful management requires early lesion detection, control of the cariogenic environment, adequate salivary function and sustained topical remineralizing exposure.

## QUESTION 2 — 10 MARKS

Discuss caries vaccine.

## STUDY MODE

### Concept

The caries vaccine is an immunologic approach intended to reduce caries by enhancing host immune responses against cariogenic microorganisms. Sturdevant describes the possibility in relation to secretory IgA: sensitized lymphocytes can ultimately reach salivary glands and contribute to salivary IgA production. Agglutinating IgA can clump oral bacteria, reduce their adherence to tooth surfaces and facilitate their clearance by swallowing. fileciteturn8file7

### Rationale

Cariogenic bacteria must colonize and persist in the oral environment.

Salivary antibodies, particularly secretory IgA, can interfere with bacterial adherence and facilitate clearance.

High concentrations of mutans streptococci (MS) may make this immunologic mechanism particularly relevant.

Animal studies in rats and primates cited by Sturdevant demonstrated feasibility of immunization attempts. fileciteturn8file7

### Caries vaccine — short note

A caries vaccine is an immunologic strategy designed to prevent or reduce dental caries by inducing host antibodies against cariogenic organisms, particularly mutans streptococci.

### Mechanism

Exposure to cariogenic bacterial antigens produces immune sensitization.

Sensitized cells can migrate to salivary glands.

Secretory IgA is produced and released into saliva.

IgA agglutinates oral bacteria, reducing their adherence to tooth surfaces and increasing clearance.

Animal studies cited in Sturdevant demonstrated feasibility; effects were described as more favorable for smooth-surface lesions.

### Advantages / potential

Targeted biologic prevention.

Potentially useful where conventional population fluoride measures are difficult.

Addresses microbial colonization rather than only the tooth surface.

### Limitations

Safety concerns, including possible cross-reaction noted in the source.

Cost and regulatory barriers.

Uncertain comparative benefit versus established fluoride therapy.

Not a routine clinical caries-control method.

Conclusion: The caries vaccine is a promising immunologic concept, but conventional fluoride-based and behavioral preventive measures remain the practical foundation.

## QUESTION 3 — 25 MARKS

Discuss various methods of caries removal.

## STUDY MODE

### Introduction

Caries removal is the operative component of management when a lesion requires tissue removal. Contemporary principles emphasize preservation of sound tooth structure and avoidance of unnecessary pulpal injury. Sturdevant notes that identification and removal of caries depend primarily on tactile interpretation; color alone is not a reliable criterion for complete removal. fileciteturn10file0

### Classification

| Method | Examples | Principal feature |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical | Hand excavation; rotary instruments | Physical cutting/removal |
| Alternative mechanical | Air abrasion; laser; air-water hydrokinetic systems | Nonconventional physical energy |
| Chemomechanical | Carisolv and related systems | Chemical softening + mechanical excavation |
| Minimal intervention | ART / ITR approaches | Hand/low-speed removal + adhesive restoration |

### 1. Hand excavation

Sharp spoon excavators can remove softened dentin selectively. Sturdevant states that dentin that is leathery, peels in small flakes or can be judiciously penetrated by a sharp explorer should be removed. Hand excavation provides tactile information but requires sharp instruments and operator skill and can sometimes remove more softened dentin than intended, risking pulp exposure. fileciteturn10file0

### 2. Rotary instrumentation

Slow-speed handpiece with a large round bur is useful for controlled removal of carious dentin.

A high-speed handpiece should not be used at full speed for deep caries because tactile discrimination is reduced and unnecessary tissue removal/pulpal injury may occur.

Sturdevant describes the high-speed handpiece operated just above stall-out speed with a round bur as another controlled approach.

Rotary instruments generally provide greater control and require less skill than pure hand excavation. fileciteturn10file0

### 3. Selective caries removal

The contemporary objective is not indiscriminate excavation to hard dentin throughout a deep lesion. Peripheral caries should be controlled to obtain a reliable seal, while pulpal protection is prioritized in deep areas. This principle is consistent with the project's contemporary caries-management material and its emphasis on conservative treatment.

### 4. Caries detector dyes

Caries-indicating solutions may provide a color guide, but Sturdevant explicitly cautions that color differences alone cannot be used as a reliable index for complete caries removal. Therefore dyes are adjunctive, not definitive.

### 5. Atraumatic restorative treatment (ART)

Summitt’s describes ART as excavation of carious tissues using hand instruments alone followed by restoration with an adhesive glass-ionomer material. It avoids rotary equipment and may avoid anesthesia, conserves tooth structure and is particularly useful in children, fearful adults, special-care populations and communities with limited access to conventional care. fileciteturn10file4

### 6. Interim therapeutic restoration (ITR)

ITR involves caries removal using hand or low-speed rotary instruments without exposing the pulp, followed by an adhesive restorative material such as conventional or resin-modified glass ionomer. It is useful for multiple lesions, rampant caries, high-risk patients, uncooperative patients and when definitive care must be postponed. fileciteturn10file4

### Advantages of conservative removal

Preserves tooth structure.

Reduces risk of pulpal exposure.

Supports minimally invasive dentistry.

Facilitates long-term maintenance when the lesion can be sealed and controlled.

### Disadvantages / limitations

Tactile assessment requires skill.

Soft dentin may be overremoved with spoon excavation.

Rotary instrumentation can remove excessive tissue if poorly controlled.

No single technique is appropriate for every lesion.

## QUESTION 4 — 25 MARKS

Discuss alternative/non-conventional/non-rotary methods of caries removal.

## STUDY MODE

### Introduction

Alternative methods were developed to reduce dependence on conventional rotary cutting, preserve tissue, improve patient acceptance or exploit selective removal of carious tissue. The project sources include air abrasion, laser systems and chemomechanical approaches, while ART represents a hand-instrument minimal-intervention approach.

### 1. Air abrasion

Air abrasion transfers kinetic energy from a stream of abrasive particles to tooth structure. Sturdevant explains that cutting depends on particle characteristics, pressure, angulation, distance and other variables. Contemporary air-abrasion equipment is useful for stain removal, pit-and-fissure debridement and micromechanical roughening. fileciteturn10file6

#### Advantages

Minimal vibration and often improved patient acceptance.

Useful for small superficial defects and fissure debridement.

Provides micromechanical roughening for bonding.

#### Limitations

Poor tactile feedback.

Abrasive dust can reduce visibility and requires control.

Not capable of producing the precise preparation walls/margins possible with conventional rotary cutting.

The source cautions that even fine abrasive streams have a cutting width much greater than typical cement margins. fileciteturn10file6

### 2. Lasers

Er:YAG laser has been used for enamel and dentin preparation. The supplied laser text describes effective hard-tissue removal with low fluence and reports limited thermal effects under appropriate conditions. The laser source also describes Er,Cr:YSGG laser-powered hydrokinetic systems using an air-water spray; the mechanism is proposed to involve micro-explosive forces generated in water droplets. fileciteturn10file5

#### Laser-assisted caries removal

The project laser source describes Carisolv followed by Er:YAG irradiation as an alternative to conventional drilling and cutting, with effective dentin-caries removal in vitro. The treated surface may show micro-irregularity and absence of a smear layer. fileciteturn9file1

#### Advantages

Potentially selective hard-tissue ablation.

Reduced vibration compared with rotary instruments.

Useful for both hard and soft tissues with appropriate laser systems.

Potentially favorable surface characteristics.

#### Limitations

Technique and parameter sensitivity.

Cost and equipment requirements.

Requires appropriate water cooling/energy control depending on system and procedure.

Not a universal replacement for conventional instrumentation.

### 3. Air-water hydrokinetic systems

The supplied laser source describes Er,Cr:YSGG systems in which laser energy is delivered through an air-water spray and can cut enamel, dentin, cementum and bone. fileciteturn9file1

### 4. Chemomechanical removal

Chemomechanical removal is discussed separately in Question 5; it combines chemical softening of carious dentin with subsequent mechanical excavation.

### 5. ART / hand-instrument minimal intervention

ART removes carious tissue using hand instruments alone and restores with adhesive glass ionomer. It eliminates rotary equipment and is especially useful for children, fearful adults, special-care populations and settings with limited conventional dental equipment. fileciteturn10file4

## QUESTION 5 — 10 MARKS

Discuss chemo-mechanical caries removal.

## STUDY MODE

### Principle

Chemomechanical caries removal combines chemical softening/selective degradation of carious dentin with subsequent mechanical excavation. The objective is to remove infected/softened tissue while minimizing unnecessary removal of sound or potentially repairable dentin.

### Basic steps

Gain conservative access to the lesion.

Apply the chemomechanical agent to the carious dentin according to the system protocol.

Allow the agent to act on softened/infected dentin.

Excavate the softened tissue using a hand instrument.

Repeat application where necessary until the clinically appropriate endpoint is reached.

Clean, inspect and restore/seal the cavity.

### Carisolv

The project laser source specifically describes Carisolv used before Er:YAG irradiation as an alternative approach to conventional mechanical drilling and cutting. In vitro, the combination was reported to remove dentin caries effectively. fileciteturn9file1

### Advantages

Conservative concept.

Potential reduction in unnecessary tissue removal.

Useful where avoidance of conventional drilling is desirable.

Can be integrated with minimally invasive restorative approaches.

### Limitations

Slower than conventional rotary excavation in many situations.

Requires chemical application and repeated excavation.

Effectiveness depends on lesion characteristics and correct technique.

Not appropriate as a universal replacement for conventional methods.

## EXAM MODE

### Definition

Chemomechanical caries removal is a minimally invasive technique in which a chemical agent softens/degrades carious dentin, after which the softened tissue is removed mechanically, usually with hand instruments.

### Technique

Access lesion → apply agent → allow softening → excavate softened dentin → reassess → repeat if required → clean and restore.

### Advantages

Conservative; potentially preserves sound dentin.

Reduced dependence on rotary cutting.

Useful in selected anxious/paediatric patients.

### Disadvantages

Time-consuming.

Chemical agent required.

Not suitable for every lesion.

May still require conventional instruments for access and final preparation.

Conclusion: Chemomechanical caries removal is a selective, minimally invasive adjunct that chemically softens carious dentin before hand excavation.

## QUESTION 6 — 10 MARKS

Discuss silver diamine fluoride in operative dentistry.

## STUDY MODE

### Definition / composition

Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) is a topical caries-arresting solution containing silver and fluoride. Craig’s Restorative Dental Materials describes an approximately 30% SDF solution containing about 25% silver and 5% fluoride in water, with a pH of approximately 10. fileciteturn8file0

### Mechanism

Silver provides a strong antimicrobial effect.

The alkaline pH helps neutralize acids.

Fluoride supports remineralization and increases resistance of the affected tissue.

The combined effect promotes arrest of carious lesions. fileciteturn8file0

### Clinical indications

Arresting active caries where conventional restorative treatment is difficult.

Children or elderly patients in whom cooperation, access or medical status makes conventional care challenging.

Selected high-caries-risk or special-care situations.

Lesions where preservation/arrest is more realistic than immediate conventional restoration.

### Advantages

Noninvasive/minimally invasive.

Antibacterial + fluoride effect.

Useful for children and geriatric/special-care patients.

Can arrest lesions and delay/avoid operative treatment.

### Major disadvantage

SDF causes dark/black staining of carious tooth structure. Craig’s specifically notes that this esthetic problem may require subsequent coverage with an esthetic material when appearance is important. fileciteturn8file0

## EXAM MODE

### Silver diamine fluoride

SDF is a topical caries-arresting material combining the antimicrobial action of silver with the anticaries/remineralizing action of fluoride.

### Mechanism

Silver → antibacterial effect.

High pH → acid neutralization.

Fluoride → remineralization and increased resistance to further demineralization.

Combined action → lesion arrest.

### Indications

Children, elderly and special-care patients.

High-risk patients.

Active lesions where conventional restoration is difficult or needs to be delayed.

### Advantages

Simple; minimally invasive; antibacterial; caries arrest.

### Disadvantage

Black staining of treated carious tissue is the major esthetic limitation.

Conclusion: SDF is an important nonrestorative caries-arresting option, especially when conventional operative care is difficult.

## QUESTION 7 — 50 MARKS

Discuss root caries—diagnosis, treatment planning and management.

## STUDY MODE

### Introduction

Root-surface caries is increasingly important because more adults retain teeth into older age and gingival recession exposes root surfaces. Summitt’s identifies root caries as a growing problem in older adults and notes that exposed root surfaces are susceptible because root structure is primarily dentin and requires greater fluoride exposure for remineralization. fileciteturn9file0

### Definition

Root caries is a carious lesion occurring on an exposed root surface, including proximal root surfaces. Sturdevant notes that the terms cervical caries and root caries are often used interchangeably, but cervical caries may refer specifically to the cervical third of crown surfaces, whereas root caries accurately describes lesions located on the root surface. fileciteturn12file7

### Etiology and risk factors

Gingival recession and exposure of root dentin/cementum.

Older age and increased tooth retention.

Poor plaque control and plaque stagnation.

Frequent fermentable-carbohydrate exposure.

Reduced salivary flow and buffering.

Low fluoride exposure.

High caries experience and active coronal caries.

Medical conditions and medications causing xerostomia.

Head-and-neck irradiation.

Periodontal treatment exposing previously covered root surfaces. fileciteturn9file0

### Biological basis

Root dentin is more mineral-vulnerable than enamel. Summitt’s gives a critical demineralization pH of about 6.4 for dentin compared with about 5.5 for enamel and notes that dentin demineralizes more rapidly because it contains less mineral. fileciteturn8file5

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW]

Title: Root-caries pathogenesis

Essential labels: Gingival recession → Root exposure → Plaque retention → Frequent acid challenge → Dentin/cementum demineralization → Softening → Cavitation → Progression / pulpal risk

Purpose: Explains why root caries becomes important after periodontal treatment and with reduced salivary protection.

### Clinical diagnosis

#### 1. History

Previous caries experience and recent lesion activity.

Dietary frequency, especially sucrose/fermentable carbohydrate exposure.

Oral-hygiene practices.

Fluoride exposure.

Dry-mouth symptoms.

Medication history and medical conditions.

History of periodontal therapy/gingival recession.

#### 2. Clinical examination

Examine clean, dry root surfaces visually and tactically. Sturdevant emphasizes careful assessment of cavitation and warns that indiscriminate probing can damage noncavitated lesions. Active and arrested lesions must be distinguished because not every root-surface lesion requires restoration. fileciteturn9file7

#### 3. Active versus arrested root caries

| Feature | Active lesion | Arrested/inactive lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | Soft/rough; plaque-retentive | Harder/smoother |
| Color | May vary; often lighter in active lesions | Often dark/discolored from pigmentation |
| Plaque | Associated with plaque stagnation | Usually less plaque-retentive |
| Management | Disease-control ± restoration | Often preventive monitoring rather than restoration |

The project source specifically emphasizes that root-surface lesions that were previously active may become inactive. Restoring an inactive lesion unnecessarily can remove sound tooth structure. fileciteturn9file7

#### 4. Radiographic diagnosis

Radiographs may help determine lesion extent and relationship to the pulp, but root-surface lesions can be difficult to characterize solely by radiography. Diagnosis should integrate visual-tactile findings and clinical context. Summitt’s lists visual-tactile criteria, radiography and laser-induced fluorescence among investigated diagnostic approaches. fileciteturn9file2

#### 5. Adjunctive diagnostic methods

Visual-tactile examination remains fundamental.

Radiographs help identify hidden proximal/root lesions and estimate extent.

Laser-induced fluorescence/DIAGNOdent has been investigated for root-surface caries.

Salivary flow and buffering assessment helps explain high-risk disease.

Microbiologic tests may contribute to risk assessment but are not sufficient as stand-alone diagnostic tests. fileciteturn9file2turn12file9

### Treatment planning principles

Treatment planning begins at the patient level. Sturdevant states that caries management should be directed at the total patient level—the medical model—because restorative treatment does not cure the carious process. The causes and risk factors must be controlled in addition to repairing existing damage. fileciteturn8file9

### Nonrestorative management

Dietary counseling with reduction of frequent sugar exposure.

Meticulous plaque control around exposed roots.

Topical fluoride; root caries may require greater fluoride availability than enamel lesions.

High-fluoride dentifrice may be considered in high-risk patients; Craig’s reports greater rehardening of cavitated root lesions with 5000-ppm fluoride dentifrice than with 1100-ppm in the cited study. fileciteturn8file1

Salivary stimulation and management of xerostomia.

Monitor arrested lesions rather than automatically restoring them.

### Restorative management

Sturdevant states that root-surface caries generally should be restored when clinical or radiographic evidence of cavitation is present, while emphasizing the need to distinguish active from arrested lesions. fileciteturn9file7

#### Indications for restoration

Clinical cavitation that prevents effective plaque removal.

Radiographic evidence supporting a cavitated/advanced lesion.

Progression despite appropriate preventive therapy.

Structural compromise or functional problems.

Sensitivity or other symptoms attributable to the lesion when conservative measures fail.

Esthetic or cleansability concerns in selected situations.

### Restorative material considerations

Root lesions are technically demanding because of limited access, cervical location, moisture control and substrate characteristics. Glass ionomer materials are particularly relevant because they are adhesive and release fluoride. Summitt’s notes that fluoride-releasing materials are often recommended for root-surface caries because root lesions require significantly greater fluoride for remineralization than enamel lesions. fileciteturn8file5

|---|---|---|
| Glass ionomer / RMGI | Adhesion + fluoride release; useful for cervical/root sites | Moisture control and contour |
| Composite | Esthetics and adhesive restoration | Isolation and bonding to root substrate |
| Amalgam | Historical/selected use | Cervical access and esthetics limit routine use |
| SDF | Nonrestorative arrest | Black staining; may require later esthetic coverage |

### Clinical technique for a root-surface restoration

Establish diagnosis and confirm that restoration is indicated.

Control etiological factors before or alongside operative treatment.

Obtain access and isolation; retract gingiva if necessary.

Remove infected/soft tissue conservatively while preserving sound dentin.

Create peripheral conditions that permit a durable seal without unnecessary extension.

Select an adhesive material appropriate to the site and moisture conditions.

Restore contour so that plaque control remains possible.

Finish and polish cervical margins carefully.

Reinforce fluoride and home-care measures.

### Management of xerostomia in root caries

Sturdevant emphasizes that xerostomia markedly increases caries incidence and recommends physician consultation when medication effects may be modifiable. Saliva stimulants and substitutes may be used in appropriate patients. fileciteturn8file7

### SDF in root caries

The caries-management source includes evidence on SDF for exposed root surfaces and identifies systematic-review evidence for controlling caries on root surfaces. It is particularly relevant when restorative treatment is difficult or disease arrest is the immediate goal. fileciteturn8file3

### Prognosis

Prognosis depends less on the mere presence of a root lesion than on whether the disease environment can be controlled. Arrested lesions may remain stable for long periods. Persistent xerostomia, frequent sugar exposure and poor plaque control predict continued activity.

### Recent/advancing concepts

The project sources emphasize movement from a purely restorative model toward patient-level risk assessment, activity-based lesion management and nonrestorative arrest. This is especially important for root caries because unnecessary cervical preparations can weaken tooth structure and create difficult restorative margins.

### Clinical significance

Root caries should therefore be approached as a chronic disease-management problem involving periodontal anatomy, saliva, diet, fluoride and restorability—not simply as a Class V cavity.

### Conclusion

Successful root-caries management requires accurate identification of active versus arrested lesions, comprehensive caries-risk assessment, aggressive control of plaque/diet/xerostomia, appropriate fluoride exposure and restoration only when a lesion is cavitated, progressing or otherwise clinically unmanageable. Adhesive fluoride-releasing materials and SDF provide important options in appropriate patients.

## QUESTION 8 — 50 MARKS

Discuss incipient caries and its management.

## STUDY MODE

### Introduction

Incipient caries represents the earliest clinically recognizable stage of enamel caries. It is especially important because the lesion may be arrested or remineralized before cavitation. Sturdevant describes incipient smooth-surface caries as an opaque white area revealed on drying, caused by subsurface porosity while the surface remains intact. fileciteturn9file6

### Definition

Incipient caries is an early, noncavitated enamel carious lesion characterized by subsurface demineralization with an intact surface. Sturdevant describes it as reversible because appropriate correction of the oral environment can permit remineralization. fileciteturn12file3

### Pathogenesis

The lesion begins with acid dissolution beneath the enamel surface. The external surface may remain relatively intact because remineralization and mineral deposition occur at the surface, while mineral loss proceeds within the lesion body. The remaining enamel crystals provide nucleation sites for calcium and phosphate during remineralization. fileciteturn12file4

#### [DIAGRAM TO DRAW]

Title: Histopathology of an incipient enamel lesion

Essential labels: Intact surface zone → Body of lesion → Dark zone → Translucent advancing zone → DEJ

Purpose: Shows the classic subsurface nature of early enamel caries.

### Clinical appearance

Earliest smooth-surface lesion appears as a white spot on a clean, dry tooth.

Usually chalky, opaque and more visible after air drying.

May be located facially/lingually and commonly near plaque stagnation areas.

The surface is initially intact and relatively hard.

The lesion may become less obvious or disappear visually when wetted because water reduces the refractive-index difference between porous enamel and the surrounding enamel. fileciteturn9file6

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Typical clue |
|---|---|
| Incipient caries | Chalky white, accentuated by drying; subsurface lesion |
| Developmental hypocalcification/hypoplasia | May be present independent of plaque stagnation; appearance less dependent on drying |
| Fluorosis | Often diffuse/symmetric; history and distribution help |
| Noncarious discoloration | Color without characteristic subsurface white-spot behavior |

### Activity assessment

Not every white spot is active. An active lesion tends to be associated with plaque stagnation and a chalky/rougher surface. An arrested/remineralized lesion is typically harder and may be brown-to-black from extrinsic pigmentation. Sturdevant emphasizes that an arrested lesion may be more resistant to future caries. fileciteturn12file3

### Diagnosis

#### 1. Visual-tactile examination

Clean the surface thoroughly, isolate and dry gently. Visual assessment is the first-line method. Sturdevant warns that indiscriminate explorer use can convert a noncavitated lesion into a cavitated one, thereby creating a restorative problem that might otherwise have been remineralized. fileciteturn12file4

#### 2. Radiography

Proximal incipient lesions may be difficult to see clinically. Radiographs can reveal proximal mineral loss, but the source notes that radiographs underestimate histologic extent and that many radiographically visible proximal lesions remain noncavitated and may therefore be candidates for remineralization rather than restoration. fileciteturn12file9

#### 3. Adjunctive technologies

Digital radiography.

Quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF).

Electrical conductivity methods.

Ultrasonography.

Laser fluorescence/DIAGNOdent in selected settings.

Sturdevant identifies these as developing or adjunctive methods intended to improve early detection and permit increased use of remineralizing strategies. fileciteturn12file2

### Caries-risk assessment

Management of an incipient lesion cannot be separated from patient risk. Important risk indicators in Sturdevant include prior caries activity, frequent sucrose exposure, minimal fluoride exposure, young/old age, decreased salivary function, high cariogenic bacterial counts and existing lesions. fileciteturn12file9

### Management principles

#### [FLOWCHART TO DRAW]

Title: Incipient caries management

Flow: Confirm noncavitation → Assess activity + risk → Remove plaque stagnation → Dietary modification → Fluoride exposure → Consider remineralizing adjuncts → Seal/infiltrate selected lesions → Monitor

### 1. Control plaque

The lesion must be placed in an environment where the biofilm can be disrupted. Daily toothbrushing with fluoride toothpaste and targeted cleaning of plaque-retentive sites are foundational.

### 2. Diet modification

Frequent sucrose exposure maintains repeated plaque-acid challenges. Dietary counseling should focus particularly on frequency and pattern of exposure rather than simply asking the patient to eliminate one food.

### 3. Fluoride

Fluoride is the most important topical chemical intervention. Sturdevant describes fluoride as enhancing precipitation of fluorapatite, remineralizing incipient lesions and exerting antimicrobial effects. fileciteturn12file6

### 4. Salivary support

Adequate saliva supplies calcium/phosphate, buffers acids and clears substrates. In xerostomic patients, salivary function should be evaluated and reversible causes considered. Sturdevant describes saliva stimulants/substitutes as possible management options. fileciteturn8file7

### 5. Calcium/phosphate remineralization systems

CPP-ACP is described in Craig’s as a technology that stabilizes high concentrations of calcium and phosphate and can remineralize enamel subsurface lesions. Bioactive glass and other calcium-phosphate approaches are potential adjuncts. fileciteturn8file0

### 6. Pit-and-fissure sealants

For early occlusal lesions, sealing can isolate the lesion from the biofilm and substrate. The management decision depends on lesion stage, activity, cleansability and risk.

### 7. Resin infiltration

The Summitt’s reference list includes clinical research on resin infiltration for caries lesions, including proximal superficial lesions. Within the project's minimally invasive framework, infiltration may be considered for selected noncavitated lesions where lesion arrest/structural reinforcement is desired.

### 8. When operative treatment is indicated

Incipient lesions should not routinely be restored. Restoration becomes relevant when cavitation has developed or when the surface is structurally compromised and cannot be maintained. Sturdevant defines cavitated caries as nonreversible and generally requiring tooth preparation and restoration. fileciteturn12file3

### Management according to site

| Site | Preferred approach |
|---|---|
| Smooth-surface white spot | Plaque control + fluoride + diet control + monitoring |
| Proximal noncavitated lesion | Risk control + fluoride; radiographic/clinical monitoring; selected infiltration |
| Occlusal noncavitated lesion | Sealant where appropriate + fluoride + monitoring |
| Cervical noncavitated lesion | Diet/hygiene + fluoride; smooth/polish if needed |
| Cavitated lesion | Restorative/sealing intervention depending on cleansability and extent |

### Management according to activity

| Lesion | Management |
|---|---|
| Active, noncavitated | Intensive disease control + remineralization + monitor |
| Arrested, noncavitated | Maintain hygiene/fluoride; monitor |
| Active, cavitated | Restore/seal when cleansability or function is compromised |
| Arrested, cavitated | Assess cleansability, structural need and esthetics before restoration |

### Advantages of early management

Preserves intact enamel.

Avoids iatrogenic cavitation.

Reduces need for restorative intervention.

Maintains tooth strength and anatomy.

Allows biological repair rather than surgical repair.

Creates an opportunity to identify and correct high-risk behavior before more teeth are affected.

### Complications of inappropriate management

Aggressive probing can create cavitation.

Unnecessary operative treatment sacrifices sound tooth structure.

Failure to correct diet/plaque/saliva problems leads to recurrent/new lesions.

Overreliance on a diagnostic device without clinical correlation can produce false-positive/false-negative decisions.

Treating a white spot solely for esthetics without assessing disease activity can lead to inappropriate intervention.

### Recent/advancing diagnostic concepts

Sturdevant explicitly connects earlier diagnosis with increased use of remineralizing strategies and identifies digital radiography, QLF, electrical conductivity and ultrasonography as technologies that may improve detection of early lesions. The key contemporary idea is to detect disease before cavitation and then intervene biologically rather than surgically whenever possible. fileciteturn12file2

### Clinical significance

Incipient caries is a high-value target for preventive dentistry because the surface remains intact and the lesion can potentially regress. Its management is therefore a test of whether the clinician understands modern caries as a dynamic disease rather than simply a cavity requiring preparation.

### Conclusion

Incipient caries is a noncavitated subsurface enamel lesion that presents most commonly as a chalky white spot on a clean, dry tooth. Diagnosis requires careful visual examination, activity assessment and appropriate adjunctive investigation. Management is predominantly nonoperative: control plaque and dietary frequency, provide topical fluoride, optimize saliva and consider selected calcium/phosphate, sealant or infiltration strategies. Restoration is reserved for cavitation or lesions that cannot otherwise be maintained.

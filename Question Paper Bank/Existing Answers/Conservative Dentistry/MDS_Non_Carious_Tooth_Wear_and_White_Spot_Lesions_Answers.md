## 13. Discuss abrasion of teeth, including its etiology, clinical features, diagnosis and management.

Marks: 25 marks [Certain]

## STUDY MODE

### Introduction and definition

Abrasion is abnormal tooth surface loss produced by friction involving an external object or an abrasive medium rather than simple tooth-to-tooth contact. Toothbrush and dentifrice abrasion is the common clinical model, but occupational, habitual and dietary mechanical factors may contribute. [Certain]

Source trail: Roberson et al. (2006), Sturdevant's Art & Science of Operative Dentistry, 5th ed., Chapter 6, “Fundamentals in Tooth Preparation,” p. 292; Summitt et al. (2013), 4th ed., Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 787-790. [Certain]

### Etiology

Toothbrushing with excessive force, especially with an inappropriate technique, hard or stiff bristles, or prolonged brushing. [Certain]

Abrasive dentifrices, particularly when used with excessive pressure or on already acid-softened tooth structure. [Certain]

Repeated use of toothpicks or other objects in interdental or cervical areas. [Certain]

Habits such as holding pipes or similar objects between teeth. [Certain]

Tobacco chewing and occupational exposure to abrasive dusts may contribute to mechanical wear. [Certain]

A major clinical point is that abrasion rarely acts in isolation. Acid-softened enamel or dentin is substantially more susceptible to subsequent mechanical wear, so abrasion and erosion may be synergistic. [Certain]

### Clinical features

Typical toothbrush abrasion is a localized cervical defect on the facial surface, often V-shaped or wedge-shaped, with relatively sharp internal line angles. [Certain]

Lesions are commonly associated with exposed root surfaces, gingival recession, or a history of vigorous toothbrushing. [Likely]

The lesion may involve enamel, cementum and dentin. Exposed dentin can produce dentinal hypersensitivity. [Certain]

Abrasion tends to occur on surfaces accessible to the causative object, whereas tooth-to-tooth wear is concentrated at functional contacts. [Certain]

A smooth, saucer-like surface suggests an erosive component rather than pure abrasion. [Likely]

Advanced lesions may threaten pulpal protection, tooth strength, periodontal health or esthetics. [Certain]

### Diagnosis

Take a detailed history of toothbrushing frequency, duration, pressure, brush type, dentifrice, brushing technique, interdental cleaning and parafunctional or occupational habits. [Certain]

Inspect lesion location, shape, surface texture, depth and distribution. [Certain]

Assess gingival recession and plaque retention, and record dentinal hypersensitivity when present. [Certain]

Differentiate abrasion from erosion, attrition, abfraction, root caries and developmental defects. [Certain]

Assess occlusion for matching wear facets or parafunctional contacts if an occlusal component is suspected. [Certain]

Radiographs are not usually diagnostic of superficial abrasion but may be indicated when lesion depth, pulpal proximity or an alternative diagnosis is suspected. [Likely]

### Management

Eliminate or reduce the causative mechanical factor. Modify brushing pressure and technique rather than merely restoring the defect. [Certain]

Use a soft or appropriately designed toothbrush and a minimally abrasive, neutral-pH dentifrice in the setting of cervical wear. [Certain]

If acid exposure is also present, control the acid source and avoid brushing immediately after an acid challenge because softened tooth structure is more vulnerable to mechanical wear. [Certain]

Manage hypersensitivity with desensitizing or dentin-sealing measures when indicated. [Certain]

Restore only when indicated by symptoms, esthetics, plaque retention, progressive tissue loss, risk to tooth strength, or a defect that can be restored with adequate restorative bulk. [Certain]

For a cervical lesion, conservative bonded composite or resin-modified glass ionomer may be selected according to esthetic demand, moisture control, root-surface involvement and mechanical requirements. [Certain]

Where a restoration is placed, preserve tooth structure and avoid unnecessary preparation. [Certain]

## 14. Discuss abrasion and abfraction, including their etiology, clinical features and management.

### Conceptual framework

Abrasion and abfraction are terms used in the discussion of noncarious cervical lesions. Abrasion denotes mechanical loss produced by an external object or abrasive medium. Abfraction is a proposed mechanism in which occlusally induced tooth flexure and stress concentration contribute to cervical microfracture and tissue loss, often interacting with abrasion and erosion. [Certain]

Source trail: Summitt et al. (2013), Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 787-790; Roberson et al. (2006), Chapter 6, “Fundamentals in Tooth Preparation,” p. 292. [Certain]

### Abrasion

Etiology: forceful toothbrushing, abrasive dentifrice, toothpicks, habits and occupational abrasives. [Certain]

Clinical appearance: accessible cervical defects, commonly V-shaped or wedge-like, often facial. [Certain]

Diagnosis: history plus lesion distribution and morphology, with differential diagnosis from erosion, attrition, abfraction and root caries. [Certain]

Management: eliminate the abrasive factor, modify brushing and dentifrice, manage sensitivity, and restore only when indicated. [Certain]

### Abfraction

Abfraction is a term applied to noncarious cervical lesions thought to involve occlusally induced tooth flexure, often in combination with abrasion. The classical theory proposes that heavy eccentric occlusal forces generate tensile and compressive stresses at the cervical region, leading to microfracture of enamel and dentin; subsequent mechanical or chemical challenges enlarge the defect. [Certain]

Source trail: Heymann et al. (1991), as referenced in Summitt et al. (2013), 4th ed., Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 788-790. [Certain]

The important contemporary qualification is that the etiology of NCCLs is multifactorial. Summitt et al. report broadly comparable evidence for toothbrush/toothpaste abrasion, occlusal forces and low pH, and therefore abfraction should not be presented as an exclusive or universally proven cause. [Certain]

Source trail: Bartlett and Shah (2006), as referenced in Summitt et al. (2013), Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 788-790. [Certain]

Litonjua et al. (2004) found that, in the experimental model cited by Summitt, occlusal loading alone did not create cervical lesions in the absence of an acid component, whereas Palamara et al. (2001) reported increased enamel dissolution when cyclic tensile loading was combined with lactic acid. This supports an interaction between mechanical stress and chemical softening rather than a simple one-factor model. [Certain]

Source trail: Litonjua et al. (2004) and Palamara et al. (2001), as referenced in Summitt et al. (2013), Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 789-790. [Certain]

## 15. Discuss abfraction in detail, including its definition, etiology, pathogenesis, incidence, clinical features and management.

Marks: 25 marks [Certain]

## STUDY MODE

### Definition

Abfraction is a term used for a noncarious cervical lesion in which occlusally induced tooth flexure and stress concentration are proposed to contribute to cervical loss of tooth structure. The classical model proposes that tensile stresses cause microfracture or weakening of cervical enamel and dentin, with subsequent mechanical and chemical challenges accelerating tissue loss. [Certain]

Source trail: Heymann et al. (1991), as referenced in Summitt et al. (2013), Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 788-790. [Certain]

### Etiology

Heavy eccentric occlusal forces and parafunctional loading are proposed initiating factors. [Certain]

Tooth geometry can concentrate stress near the cervical region, particularly under bending loads. [Likely]

Abrasion from toothbrush and dentifrice can act on mechanically weakened tissue. [Certain]

Acidic conditions can chemically soften enamel and dentin and increase susceptibility to stress-assisted tissue loss. [Certain]

Therefore, contemporary discussion treats abfraction within a multifactorial NCCL model rather than as an isolated etiology. [Certain]

### Pathogenesis

Occlusal loading produces tensile and compressive stresses in the cervical region. [Certain]

Repeated loading may produce flexure of the tooth and stress concentration at the cervical area. [Likely]

The classical theory proposes microfracture or loosening of enamel rods and weakening of the cervical substrate. [Certain]

Acid exposure can increase dissolution and reduce the resistance of enamel and dentin to mechanical loading. [Certain]

Toothbrushing and other abrasive forces then enlarge the defect. [Certain]

The resulting lesion may progress from a shallow cervical notch to a deeper wedge-shaped defect, with dentin exposure and hypersensitivity. [Certain]

### Incidence and epidemiology

Abfraction cannot be assigned a clean population incidence independent of other NCCLs because the clinical lesion is not etiologically specific. Summitt reports that NCCL prevalence varies from rare to 89% in different populations and that occurrence generally increases with age. [Certain]

In the cited Croatian adult study, 18% of teeth showed facial tooth wear after exclusion of restored facial surfaces; cervical wear was identified in 5% of subjects younger than 26 years and 48% of those older than 65 years. [Certain]

Source trail: Borcic et al. (2004), as referenced in Summitt et al. (2013), Chapter 15, “Class 5 Restorations,” pp. 787-788. [Certain]

A defensible exam statement is therefore: abfraction-like lesions are common within the broad category of NCCLs, but a separate incidence figure for true abfraction is not reliably established. [Certain]

### Clinical features

Noncarious cervical lesion, often wedge-shaped, with relatively well-defined margins. [Certain]

May occur in teeth exposed to significant occlusal loading and may coexist with occlusal wear facets. [Likely]

Can be localized or multiple and may occur with gingival recession. [Certain]

Dentin exposure may lead to thermal, tactile or osmotic hypersensitivity. [Certain]

Advanced lesions may compromise cervical tooth strength or approach the pulp. [Certain]

Lesion morphology alone is not sufficiently specific to prove abfraction. [Certain]

Detailed history: parafunction, clenching/grinding, diet, brushing habits, dentifrice and occupational exposure. [Certain]

Clinical examination: lesion site, shape, depth, surface character, dentin exposure, gingival recession and plaque retention. [Certain]

Occlusal analysis: identify wear facets, eccentric contacts and parafunctional patterns. [Certain]

The strongest conceptual weakness of a pure abfraction model is that cervical lesions also occur where the expected occlusal loading pattern does not uniquely explain their location. The exam interpretation is therefore: occlusal stress may modify susceptibility and progression, but the lesion should be managed as a multifactorial NCCL. [Certain]

Modify toothbrushing and use minimally abrasive neutral-pH toothpaste. [Certain]

Control dietary or intrinsic acid exposure when present. [Certain]

Treat dentinal hypersensitivity with desensitizing agents, dentin bonding/sealing or restoration according to severity. [Certain]

Restore when there is significant progression, sensitivity, esthetic concern, plaque retention, threatened tooth strength or a lesion deep enough to justify a durable restoration. [Certain]

Acid contacts the tooth and dissolves mineral from enamel and dentin. [Certain]

### 1. Definition and terminology

### 2. Etiology

Dental biofilm and frequent acid production from fermentable carbohydrate metabolism are the central etiologic drivers of caries-associated white spot lesions. [Certain]

Frequent carbohydrate exposure reduces the opportunity for recovery and shifts the balance toward demineralization. [Certain]

Inadequate fluoride exposure reduces the protective and remineralizing capacity of the oral environment. [Certain]

Reduced salivary flow or impaired buffering increases caries susceptibility. [Certain]

#### D. Sealants

Pit and fissure sealants can arrest or prevent progression of selected noncavitated occlusal lesions by creating a physical barrier. [Certain]

The 2019 evidence review reports that sealants, alone or combined with 5% NaF varnish, are useful for selected noncavitated occlusal lesions. [Certain]

### 12. Microabrasion and esthetic management

Microabrasion is primarily an esthetic technique for superficial enamel discoloration and selected superficial developmental defects. It should not be confused with disease-control therapy for an active caries lesion. [Certain]

Sturdevant describes microabrasion for superficial idiopathic white or light-brown defects and fluorosis, with careful isolation, controlled abrasive application and topical fluoride after treatment. The cited depth limit for the described procedure is approximately 0.2 to 0.3 mm. [Certain]

### 13. Restorative treatment of cavitated lesions

Cavitation reduces cleansability and usually requires restorative management when plaque control is inadequate or function/structure is compromised. [Certain]

Selective removal and tooth-preserving restorative strategies are preferred over unnecessarily aggressive preparation. [Certain]

For cervical lesions, conservative bonded composite or RMGI may be selected according to site and moisture control. [Certain]

### 14. White spot lesions around orthodontic appliances

The main problem is plaque retention and frequent acid challenge around brackets and gingival margins. [Likely]

Prevention depends on meticulous plaque control, fluoride exposure, dietary control and professional monitoring. [Certain]

Active noncavitated lesions should generally receive nonrestorative disease-control measures first. [Certain]

After orthodontic treatment, persistent esthetic lesions can be considered for resin infiltration or carefully selected microabrasion depending on depth and diagnosis. [Likely]

### 15. Management algorithm

Confirm that the white opacity is caries rather than fluorosis, hypoplasia or another developmental defect. [Certain]

Clean and dry the tooth and assess surface integrity. [Certain]

Determine lesion activity, stage, cleansability and patient caries risk. [Certain]

Control plaque, dietary sugar frequency and fluoride exposure. [Certain]

For active noncavitated lesions, institute remineralization/nonrestorative care and review response. [Certain]

For selected proximal lesions, consider resin infiltration. [Certain]

For selected occlusal noncavitated lesions, consider sealant therapy. [Certain]

For persistent superficial esthetic defects, consider microabrasion after confirming the diagnosis. [Certain]

For cavitated or structurally compromised lesions, provide tooth-preserving restorative care. [Certain]

Monitor over time because disease activity and patient risk can change. [Certain]

### 16. Recent and contemporary concepts

Modern caries management increasingly treats the disease process and the lesion separately, emphasizing patient-level risk and lesion-level staging. [Certain]

## EXAM MODE

### Definition

A white spot lesion is a clinically visible noncavitated area of subsurface enamel demineralization caused by alteration of enamel mineral and optical properties. [Certain]

### Etiology

Cariogenic biofilm with acid production from fermentable carbohydrates. [Certain]

Frequent sugar exposure, poor plaque control and inadequate fluoride. [Certain]

Reduced salivary flow/buffering. [Certain]

Orthodontic plaque retention is an important clinical setting. [Likely]

Developmental hypomineralization and fluorosis are differential diagnoses, not caries causes. [Certain]

### Pathogenesis

Acid challenge causes subsurface mineral loss. [Certain]

Relatively intact surface layer may persist through remineralization. [Certain]

Porosity changes light scattering, producing the white appearance. [Certain]

Continued progression may cause surface breakdown and cavitation. [Certain]

### Clinical features

Chalky, matte, opaque white lesion, especially after drying. [Certain]

May become brown/black after staining. [Certain]

Active lesions are generally rougher/more matte; arrested lesions are harder and smoother. [Likely]

Common at plaque-retentive sites and around orthodontic appliances. [Certain]

### Diagnosis

Clean and dry the tooth. [Certain]

Visual assessment of color, opacity, texture, cavitation and location. [Certain]

Avoid aggressive probing of noncavitated lesions. [Certain]

Use bitewing radiographs for proximal lesion depth when indicated. [Certain]

Use transillumination and other adjuncts selectively. [Certain]

Differentiate from fluorosis, hypoplasia and developmental hypomineralization. [Certain]

### Activity and risk assessment

Classify the lesion as active or arrested. [Certain]

Assess patient caries risk, diet, plaque, fluoride and saliva. [Certain]

Management should combine patient-level and lesion-level assessment. [Certain]

### Management of active noncavitated lesions

Oral hygiene and biofilm control. [Certain]

Dietary sugar-frequency reduction. [Certain]

Fluoride toothpaste and professionally applied fluoride where indicated. [Certain]

Remineralization strategy with saliva, calcium/phosphate availability and fluoride. [Certain]

Resin infiltration for selected proximal/noncavitated lesions. [Certain]

Sealants for selected occlusal lesions. [Certain]

Recall and monitoring. [Certain]

### Esthetic management

Microabrasion for selected superficial developmental/esthetic defects after diagnosis. [Certain]

Resin infiltration may improve the appearance of selected caries-associated white spot lesions while arresting progression. [Certain]

### Cavitated lesions

Restorative treatment is generally required when cleansability or structural integrity is compromised. [Certain]

Use tooth-preserving restorative techniques. [Certain]

### Contemporary principle

Do not equate a visible white spot with an automatic indication for drilling. [Certain]

Treat disease activity and risk, not merely the color change. [Certain]

## INLAY

### 1. What is Primary and Secondary Flare?

In an inlay preparation, flaring refers to the controlled enlargement and finishing of the facial and lingual proximal walls so that the casting has a definite path of withdrawal, adequate marginal bulk, and a clinically accessible margin. The primary flare is the initial divergence or opening of the proximal wall created as the preparation is extended toward the proximal surface. It establishes the basic form of the proximal box and exposes the enamel margin. The secondary flare is the more definitive, finishing flare placed after the primary preparation. It is made with a slender flame-shaped finishing instrument and is directed so that the instrument follows the intended line of draw.

The distinction is important because the secondary flare is not simply additional cutting. Its purpose is to create a smooth, continuous enamel margin, improve marginal adaptation, and permit the wax pattern and final casting to reproduce the preparation without unsupported or excessively thin metal. The walls should be flared enough to remove unsupported enamel and provide a readable margin, but not so much that unnecessary tooth structure is sacrificed.

In practical preparation, the operator views the proximal box from an appropriate direction and uses a fine instrument to refine the facial and lingual walls. Sharp internal transitions are avoided because they complicate waxing, casting, seating, and finishing. Sturdevant emphasizes generous rounding of line and point angles, while the cast-metal preparation principles stress preservation of sound tooth structure and a controlled path of insertion.

Viva point: Primary flare establishes the proximal form; secondary flare refines that form and produces the final accessible, smooth margin. The goal is not maximum flaring but predictable adaptation with minimum removal of sound tooth structure.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Craig’s Restorative Dental Materials

### 2. What is Circumferential Tie?

A circumferential tie is a finishing and retention concept used in indirect cast restorations in which the preparation form and/or a peripheral extension allows the restoration to engage the tooth around a substantial portion of its circumference. Its purpose is to help resist displacement and to provide a continuous relationship between the prepared surfaces and the restoration. The principle is especially relevant when retention cannot be obtained from simple wall geometry alone.

For an inlay, retention is primarily obtained from the relationship between the opposing walls and the path of insertion. The preparation therefore must have a definite path of draw and should not contain undesirable undercuts that prevent seating. Where appropriate, peripheral features can increase the amount of tooth structure engaged by the restoration and improve resistance to dislodging forces. This is particularly useful in preparations where the remaining tooth structure is limited or where the restoration extends onto proximal surfaces.

The circumferential concept must not be confused with unnecessarily extending the preparation. Contemporary operative dentistry emphasizes conservative preparation: only the diseased or defective structure and the tooth structure required for a predictable restoration should be removed. Excessive extension weakens the tooth and can compromise pulpal and periodontal health.

Clinically, a circumferential tie is beneficial because forces attempting to dislodge the indirect restoration are resisted by a larger area of prepared tooth structure. It also contributes to stability when combined with proper occlusal form, proximal box design, and appropriate cementation.

Viva point: Think of the circumferential tie as increasing the restoration’s engagement with the remaining tooth around its perimeter. It is a retention/stability concept, not an excuse for unnecessary circumferential tooth reduction.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt

### 3. What is Bevel and types of bevels?

A bevel is a deliberate sloping modification of the cavosurface margin rather than a junction in which the external tooth surface meets the preparation wall at a sharp 90-degree relationship. In cast-metal indirect restorations, bevels are used to modify enamel margins, improve marginal adaptation, and provide an appropriate thickness and angle of metal at the margin. The exact bevel depends on the restoration and the location of the margin.

Important bevel forms encountered in operative dentistry include the gingival bevel, which is placed at the gingival margin of a proximal box; the facial and lingual bevels, which refine proximal enamel margins; the counterbevel used on reduced cusps in onlay preparations; and the bevel placed on selected internal line angles such as the axiopulpal line angle. The counterbevel is particularly important because it produces a controlled metal margin on a cusp that has been reduced for protection.

Sturdevant describes use of a slender, flame-shaped, fine-grit diamond to establish counterbevels, gingival bevels, and secondary flares. For cast-metal onlays, the bevel is designed to produce an appropriate marginal metal angle, and the junctions between counterbevels and flares are rounded. A slight bevel of the axiopulpal line angle increases the bulk of the wax pattern at a critical region.

The bevel should always be compatible with the restorative material. It should not be used indiscriminately for tooth-colored adhesive restorations where a butt-joint margin may be preferable.

Viva point: A bevel is a controlled sloping cavosurface modification. In cast-metal restorations it improves marginal metal adaptation and bulk; common examples are gingival bevel, facial/lingual bevel, counterbevel, and axiopulpal bevel.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Phillips

### 4. What is Contact wax?

Contact wax is an inlay-wax or similar wax material used during the recording or adjustment of proximal contact relationships associated with indirect restorations. Its clinical purpose is to help establish or evaluate the contact relationship between the restoration and adjacent teeth. Proper proximal contact is essential because an indirect restoration must reproduce the function of the original tooth while permitting appropriate embrasure form, floss passage, and periodontal health.

The contact relationship is not judged simply by whether two teeth touch. The contact should be positioned within the physiologic contact area and should provide adequate resistance to displacement of the teeth without creating a broad, plaque-retentive or excessively tight contact. During fabrication and try-in, the proximal contour and contact are adjusted before final cementation whenever possible because access is limited after the restoration is bonded or luted.

The uploaded operative dentistry texts emphasize careful management of proximal contours and contacts during indirect restoration procedures. Abrasive disks may be used to adjust proximal contour and contact, with progressively finer abrasives used because these surfaces can become inaccessible after cementation. If a restoration does not seat completely, the clinician should first determine whether the problem is caused by overcontour, residual temporary material, debris, or an internal or marginal interference.

Contact wax therefore has a practical role in forming or recording the intended proximal contact during laboratory or clinical procedures. It should be regarded as an aid to contact development rather than as a substitute for accurate preparation, impression making, laboratory fabrication, and clinical adjustment.

Viva point: Contact wax helps establish or record proximal contact form for an indirect restoration. The final contact must be firm but physiologic, permit flossing, and harmonize with the proximal contour and embrasure.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Phillips

### 5. What type of wax is used for Inlay Wax Pattern?

The wax used for an inlay pattern is a dental inlay pattern wax formulated for the lost-wax technique. The important requirement is not simply that the wax can be shaped; it must reproduce fine preparation details, remain dimensionally stable during manipulation, burn out cleanly, and permit fabrication of a pattern that accurately represents the intended restoration.

Dental inlay waxes are designed specifically for direct or indirect patterns. Phillips describes inlay wax as a specialized dental wax used to form direct or indirect patterns for the lost-wax casting technique. These waxes are mixtures of wax components selected to provide suitable softening, carving, flow, rigidity, and burnout characteristics. Their relatively high thermal expansion is clinically important because wax dimensions change with temperature.

For an indirect cast inlay, the pattern may be formed directly on the die or by a suitable indirect technique. The wax should be manipulated with minimal unnecessary heating because repeated heating and cooling can alter its dimensions. The pattern should be smooth, complete, and free of defects before investing. A defective wax pattern produces a defective casting even if the investment and casting procedures are otherwise correct.

Low-fusing inlay wax may also be used for specific recording procedures, such as an interocclusal record. The distinction between pattern wax and recording wax is therefore important in viva discussions.

The ideal inlay wax should permit precise adaptation to the die, adequate rigidity after cooling, and clean elimination during burnout. Its dimensional behavior is particularly important because the wax pattern is an intermediate stage between the prepared tooth and the final metal restoration.

Viva point: Use a purpose-designed dental inlay wax for the lost-wax pattern. Its key properties are accurate adaptation, workable flow, adequate rigidity, dimensional stability, and clean burnout.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig; Sturdevant

### 6. What are retention features for Inlay?

Retention of a cast inlay is primarily derived from preparation geometry rather than from an inherently adhesive metal-tooth relationship. The preparation should have a definite path of insertion and appropriately oriented opposing walls so that the restoration resists displacement along that path. Adequate occlusal and proximal extension, proper wall form, and sufficient vertical wall length contribute to retention.

Important retention features include the occlusal step, proximal box walls, appropriate wall divergence or convergence consistent with the selected inlay design, and, when indicated, additional grooves or extensions. The preparation must not contain unintended undercuts that interfere with seating. At the same time, it should not be excessively divergent because excessive divergence reduces the amount of prepared surface available to resist dislodgment.

For cast-metal restorations, retention and resistance are closely related to the geometry of the preparation. The remaining tooth structure must also be strong enough to resist functional forces. Contemporary operative dentistry stresses that the preparation should be conservative while still predictable. The restoration is not retained simply by the cement; cementation is an adjunct to a sound mechanical design.

Where tooth height is limited or the preparation is otherwise short, additional retention features such as grooves may be used in appropriate situations. Sturdevant describes retention grooves in selected cast-metal preparations and emphasizes that their position should avoid undermining enamel and should be related to the dentinoenamel junction.

The final retention is also influenced by the luting agent and the accuracy of fit. For tooth-colored inlays, adhesive resin cement can contribute substantially to retention, especially when the ceramic surface is appropriately conditioned.

Viva point: Inlays are retained mainly by preparation geometry—definite path of draw, adequate wall height, controlled wall form, proximal boxes and, when indicated, grooves. Cement complements rather than replaces proper preparation design.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Phillips

### 7. What is the type of Gold used for Inlay?

For cast gold inlays, the restorative alloy must provide a combination of strength, corrosion resistance, dimensional accuracy, castability, and sufficient ductility for finishing and burnishing. Dental casting gold alloys are classified according to their mechanical properties and composition. The cast restoration must withstand occlusal forces while maintaining marginal integrity.

For an inlay, a relatively high-noble or noble cast gold alloy appropriate for intracoronal restoration is selected. The traditional classification of dental gold casting alloys distinguishes types according to strength and hardness, with the harder grades used where greater resistance to deformation is required. The alloy selection should match the functional demands of the restoration rather than simply maximizing hardness.

Gold alloys are advantageous because gold is chemically stable and resistant to corrosion and tarnish. Alloying elements such as copper, silver, palladium, and platinum modify hardness, strength, melting behavior, and other properties. Phillips discusses the gold-copper system and the effect of alloy composition and heat treatment on mechanical properties. Type III and Type IV gold alloys can undergo age hardening because of their alloy composition, whereas the lower-strength types do not show the same response.

For a cast inlay, the alloy should permit accurate casting of thin margins and then tolerate finishing and polishing without excessive deformation. Its relatively favorable castability and corrosion resistance are major reasons gold restorations have a long clinical history.

Viva point: The answer is not simply “pure gold.” Dental inlays use appropriate dental casting gold alloys, traditionally selected from the stronger cast-gold categories according to functional requirements. Their major advantages are corrosion resistance, castability, ductility and durable marginal behavior.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig; Sturdevant

### 8. What impression is best advocated for Inlay?

For an indirect inlay, the definitive impression must reproduce the prepared tooth, its margins, adjacent teeth, and relevant occlusal relationships with high dimensional accuracy. The uploaded operative dentistry sources identify elastomeric impression materials, particularly polyvinyl siloxane, as common materials for indirect cast restorations because they can reproduce fine detail and can be withdrawn from areas that contain undercuts.

A good impression material must become sufficiently elastic after setting, resist tearing during removal, maintain dimensional accuracy and stability, reproduce fine marginal detail, have acceptable working and setting characteristics, and tolerate disinfection without significant distortion. Sturdevant specifically describes polyvinyl siloxane as a common material for indirect casting techniques. When a preparation margin is subgingival, tissue management is equally important because the impression material cannot accurately record a margin that is obscured by gingiva, blood, or sulcular fluid.

Retraction cord is used when necessary to temporarily displace the free gingiva and control moisture and hemorrhage. The objective is to expose enough of the margin so that the impression material reaches it in adequate bulk and reproduces it without tearing. The tooth and preparation must be clean and dry before the impression is made.

The tray and impression technique also influence accuracy. A two-viscosity approach, using light-bodied material around the preparation and heavier-bodied material in the tray, is described for contemporary polyvinyl siloxane systems. Automixing systems can reduce air incorporation and improve consistency.

Viva point: A high-quality elastomeric impression—commonly addition silicone/polyvinyl siloxane—with proper tissue retraction and moisture control is advocated. The material must accurately reproduce the preparation and margins without distortion.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt

### 9. What is composition of Inlay wax?

Dental inlay wax is a formulated mixture of several waxes and modifying ingredients designed to produce a pattern for the lost-wax technique. The exact commercial composition varies between products, but the formulation generally combines natural and synthetic waxes with resins or other modifiers to achieve the required flow, hardness, carving characteristics, and dimensional behavior.

The composition is clinically important because an inlay wax must be sufficiently plastic when warmed to adapt to the die, but sufficiently rigid after cooling to retain its shape during carving, spruing, removal, and investing. It must also burn out with minimal residue because any remaining carbonaceous material can interfere with the investment and metal casting.

Phillips emphasizes that dental waxes have important thermal properties and that inlay wax exhibits a relatively high coefficient of thermal expansion. This means that temperature changes can cause measurable dimensional changes in the wax pattern. A pattern that fits accurately while warm may change dimensions as it cools, and repeated heating and cooling can increase distortion. Consequently, manipulation should be controlled and unnecessary reheating avoided.

The pattern wax also requires adequate flow at the working temperature so that the fine details of the prepared tooth can be reproduced. However, excessive flow or softening at room temperature would make carving and removal unreliable.

Inlay waxes therefore represent a balance between thermoplastic behavior and dimensional stability. The clinician or technician must understand that the wax pattern is not dimensionally inert. Temperature, manipulation, residual stresses, and time before investing can all influence its accuracy.

Viva point: Inlay wax is a purpose-formulated dental wax blend, commonly containing natural/synthetic waxes and modifying resins. Its critical properties are controlled flow, carving ability, rigidity after cooling, dimensional stability and clean burnout.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig

### 10. What are the sprue material, sprue position and diameter for inlay?

A sprue is the channel through which molten alloy enters the mold during casting of an inlay. Its design influences the ability of the molten metal to fill the mold, compensate for solidification shrinkage, and minimize porosity or incomplete casting. The sprue must therefore be positioned and dimensioned according to the restoration and the casting system.

For a small inlay pattern, the sprue is commonly made from wax or another compatible burnout material. It is attached to a suitable region of the wax pattern so that the molten alloy reaches the restoration efficiently while avoiding distortion of thin margins. The sprue should generally be attached to the bulkier portion of the pattern rather than directly to a delicate marginal area. The position should also facilitate a favorable flow path into the mold.

Sprue diameter must be adequate to maintain a reservoir and allow uninterrupted metal flow. If it is too small, premature solidification may occur before the mold is completely filled and shrinkage porosity can develop. If it is unnecessarily large, removal becomes more difficult and the casting procedure becomes less economical. The exact dimension is dependent on the size and geometry of the restoration and the alloy/casting system.

The uploaded materials emphasize the principles of sprue design, investing, and casting rather than a single universal diameter applicable to every inlay. Therefore, the clinically correct viva answer is to select a sprue size appropriate to the restoration and manufacturer/casting system rather than memorize one number for all situations.

Viva point: Use a compatible burnout sprue, attach it to the bulk of the wax pattern in a position that promotes smooth metal flow, and choose a diameter large enough to prevent premature solidification while remaining appropriate for the restoration.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig; Sturdevant

### 11. What are the advantages of Ceramic Inlay over Cast Metal Inlay?

The major advantage of a ceramic inlay over a cast metal inlay is esthetics. Ceramic can reproduce tooth-like color and translucency and therefore provides a more natural appearance, which is particularly important when the restoration is visible during smiling or speech. Adhesive cementation also permits effective bonding of etched ceramic to resin cement, contributing to retention and marginal sealing.

Summitt notes that adhesion of resin cement is more reliable and durable to appropriately etched porcelain than to prepolymerized resin composite. Ceramic inlays may therefore have an important advantage in the cementation process. Pressed ceramic inlays can also demonstrate excellent marginal fidelity when properly fabricated. In addition, modern ceramic systems provide good wear resistance and long-term color stability.

However, the advantage should not be presented without qualification. Ceramic is more fragile during try-in and adjustment than metal, and careless handling can cause fracture. If an interproximal contact is excessively reduced during adjustment, correction may require additional laboratory work. The literature within the source text also reports that some clinical studies found higher fracture rates for ceramic inlays than for indirect resin composite inlays.

Compared with cast metal, ceramic requires more demanding adhesive procedures and strict moisture control. Internal ceramic surfaces are commonly conditioned by hydrofluoric acid when appropriate for the ceramic type, followed by silane treatment, and the restoration is bonded with resin cement.

Thus, ceramic is selected when esthetics and adhesive bonding are important and when the preparation and material provide adequate support.

Viva point: Ceramic inlays offer superior esthetics, favorable adhesive bonding after appropriate surface treatment, and good color stability. Their disadvantages are greater brittleness during handling and a more technique-sensitive cementation procedure.

Source basis: Summitt; Sturdevant; Phillips

### 12. What are the die materials used for Inlay?

A die is a positive replica of the prepared tooth on which an indirect inlay pattern is fabricated. The die material must reproduce the preparation accurately, resist abrasion during waxing and finishing, and remain dimensionally stable. For cast and indirect restorations, gypsum-based die materials and elastomer-compatible systems are commonly used; the exact selection depends on the laboratory technique and restoration.

High-strength dental stone is widely used because it provides adequate strength, dimensional accuracy, and compatibility with impression materials. Special die stones are formulated to provide higher hardness and lower abrasion than ordinary model stone. These properties are important because the margin of an inlay is thin and can be damaged if the die surface is easily scratched.

Other die systems can incorporate resin or metal components, and removable die techniques may use dowel-pin arrangements to permit precise removal and replacement of individual dies in a working cast. The choice is governed by the required accuracy, strength, compatibility with the impression material, and laboratory workflow.

Sturdevant emphasizes that the working cast is made from an impression of the prepared and adjacent teeth and that the die must reproduce the preparation and margins accurately. Errors introduced at the impression or die stage are carried into the wax pattern and ultimately into the casting.

The die also interacts with die spacer and pattern wax. A controlled spacer may be used on internal surfaces to provide the space required for the luting cement, but the spacer must stop short of the margin according to the laboratory technique.

Viva point: The preferred die is a high-strength, dimensionally stable dental die material—commonly a high-strength gypsum die stone—with removable-die systems used when required. Accuracy, abrasion resistance and dimensional stability are the essential properties.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Phillips

### 13. What are different Die–Pin systems used for die preparation?

Die–pin systems allow the individual prepared-tooth die to be removed from and accurately repositioned into the working cast. This is important because an indirect inlay requires access to the entire preparation and margins during waxing, finishing, and evaluation. The system must therefore provide stability, reproducible repositioning, and minimal movement.

Common removable-die concepts use one or more dowel pins embedded in the die or cast. A single-pin system uses one main pin to guide repositioning, whereas double-pin systems use two pins to improve rotational stability. The pins may be associated with sleeves or other guides incorporated into the cast. Some systems use a split-cast arrangement in which the die segment can be removed and replaced accurately.

The purpose of the pin is not to strengthen the tooth preparation but to control the laboratory model. Accurate repositioning is essential because even a small change in die orientation can alter proximal contacts, margins, occlusion, and the path of insertion of the restoration. The working cast therefore has to be fabricated with adequate mechanical stability as well as dimensional accuracy.

The source texts emphasize that laboratory fabrication of indirect restorations depends on a precise working cast and accurate die reproduction. A die system should make it possible to remove the die without damaging the margin and then return it to the exact original position.

Viva point: Die-pin systems are essentially mechanisms for removable and reproducible dies. They include single-pin and double-pin arrangements and split-cast configurations. Double-pin designs provide improved resistance to rotation, while split-cast systems facilitate removal and replacement of individual dies.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Craig

### 14. What is divestment?

Divestment is the process of removing the investment material from around a completed dental casting after the alloy has solidified. It follows casting and precedes cleaning, finishing, and polishing. The objective is to recover the casting without damaging its margins, internal surfaces, or delicate features.

During investing, the wax pattern is surrounded by an investment material capable of reproducing the pattern and withstanding the temperature required for burnout and casting. After the molten metal has entered the mold and solidified, the investment must be removed. This can be performed by mechanical methods, appropriate chemical procedures, or a combination depending on the investment and alloy. The technique should be controlled because aggressive mechanical removal can damage thin margins or create surface defects.

Divestment is particularly important for inlays because their margins are thin and their internal surfaces must remain accurate. The operator should avoid procedures that distort or abrade the casting. After the investment is removed, remnants of investment and oxide are cleaned away, and the sprue is separated. The restoration is then examined for casting defects, marginal integrity, internal fit, and surface quality.

The quality of divestment depends partly on the investment itself. A suitable investment must provide adequate strength during casting but permit practical removal afterward. Phillips discusses the relationship between investment properties, casting, and subsequent recovery of the restoration.

Viva point: Divestment means removal of the investment material from the cast restoration after solidification. It is a controlled recovery step; the casting must be freed without damaging its margins or internal surface, followed by sprue removal, cleaning and inspection.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig; Sturdevant

### 15. What is Green strength?

Green strength is the strength of an investment material or other mold material before it has undergone the high-temperature changes associated with burnout and casting. In dental casting, green strength is important because the invested wax pattern must remain intact during setting, handling, removal from the working area, and subsequent manipulation before burnout.

An investment must have sufficient green strength to resist cracking, chipping, or deformation while it is still in its relatively immature or unset/early-set state. If the investment lacks adequate strength, the mold may be damaged before casting, producing a distorted or incomplete restoration. On the other hand, investment behavior must also be compatible with burnout and subsequent casting because the mold ultimately needs sufficient high-temperature stability.

Green strength is therefore different from the high-temperature strength of the investment. The former concerns handling and early mold integrity, whereas the latter concerns resistance to thermal stresses and the forces generated by molten alloy during casting.

The quality of the investment is influenced by its composition, powder-to-liquid ratio, mixing, setting reaction, and handling. Accurate proportioning and proper manipulation are essential because changes in these variables can alter strength, setting expansion, porosity, and casting behavior.

For an inlay, green strength is especially important because the pattern contains thin and detailed features that must remain accurately supported throughout investing. Damage at this stage can be carried into the final casting.

Viva point: Green strength is the strength of the investment before burnout/casting, allowing the invested pattern to withstand handling without fracture or distortion. It is distinct from the investment’s high-temperature strength.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig

### 16. What are the indications for selection of tooth for inlay?

An inlay is selected when an indirect restoration offers a predictable advantage over a direct restoration while allowing the tooth to retain sufficient sound structure. The source texts emphasize that direct composite should generally be preferred for many moderate two- and three-surface defects, but certain situations justify an indirect inlay.

One indication is a large interproximal distance or a difficult proximal contact situation in which achieving a proper contact directly is challenging. Another is the need to establish a specific occlusal relationship that can be more predictably developed outside the mouth. An inlay may also be considered when the defect extends beyond approximately two-thirds of the intercuspal width but does not require cuspal coverage; if the cusps are significantly weakened, an onlay or another form of cuspal coverage may be more appropriate.

The tooth must have sufficient remaining structure to support the restoration and must have a preparation capable of providing a predictable path of insertion and adequate resistance. Margins should be accessible and the periodontal condition should permit isolation and impression making. Pulpal status must also be assessed before treatment.

Indirect restorations are particularly useful when laboratory fabrication can improve anatomy, contact, contour, or material properties. However, a conservative philosophy means that the clinician should not choose an inlay simply because it is technically possible. If a smaller direct restoration provides an equally predictable result, unnecessary indirect treatment is not justified.

Viva point: Choose an inlay when the defect is too extensive or difficult for an ideal direct restoration but does not require cuspal coverage. Typical indications include difficult proximal contact, specific occlusal requirements, and a large but structurally suitable intracoronal defect.

Source basis: Summitt; Sturdevant

### 17. What are luting materials used for Inlay?

Luting materials for inlays depend on the restorative material, preparation design, and desired retention mechanism. Traditional cast-metal inlays may be cemented with conventional dental cements such as zinc phosphate or polycarboxylate cement, whereas tooth-colored ceramic and composite inlays are commonly bonded with resin-based composite cements.

The selection is important because the cement must fill the microscopic space between the restoration and tooth, provide retention or adhesion appropriate to the system, and maintain adequate mechanical and biological performance. For cast metal, conventional cements have a long history of use and can perform well when the preparation provides adequate mechanical retention and the restoration fits accurately.

For ceramic inlays, adhesive resin cement is particularly important because the ceramic surface can be conditioned to permit strong bonding. Sturdevant describes hydrofluoric acid etching of appropriate ceramic surfaces followed by silane treatment, which promotes micromechanical and chemical interaction with resin cement. A suitable enamel/dentin bonding protocol is then used on the tooth. Dual-cure resin cement may be selected where adequate polymerization through the restoration is required.

Indirect composite inlays also generally use resin cement, but the internal surface treatment differs from ceramic. Because the prepolymerized composite surface offers fewer available bonding sites, systems may use air abrasion or another surface-conditioning method before bonding.

The cement should never be expected to compensate for a fundamentally defective preparation or poor marginal fit.

Viva point: Conventional cast-metal inlays may use zinc phosphate or polycarboxylate-type cements, while ceramic and indirect composite inlays generally use adhesive resin cements. Ceramic requires appropriate internal surface conditioning and silanization before resin cementation.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt; Phillips

### 18. What is the cavosurface treatment given for Inlay cavity?

Cavosurface treatment refers to the modification of the margin where the prepared tooth surface meets the external tooth surface. For a cast-metal inlay, the margin is commonly refined with a bevel or flare to provide adequate metal thickness, improve marginal adaptation, and eliminate unsupported enamel. The exact treatment depends on the location and restorative material.

The preparation should have smooth, clearly defined margins with no unsupported enamel or irregularities. A bevel may be placed on appropriate enamel margins so that the cast metal terminates in a thin but adequately supported edge. The bevel also allows the casting to reproduce the margin more accurately and permits finishing and burnishing where appropriate.

Sturdevant describes use of slender, flame-shaped, fine-grit diamonds for gingival bevels and secondary flares. In related cast-metal preparations, counterbevels produce a controlled marginal metal angle on reduced cusps, while sharp junctions are rounded slightly. The axiopulpal line angle may also receive a light bevel to increase wax-pattern thickness at a critical area.

The cavosurface design must be distinguished from ceramic or composite adhesive restorations. Tooth-colored indirect restorations generally rely on bonded interfaces and often require a butt-joint type margin rather than a conventional cast-metal bevel. Their internal restoration surface is treated separately according to the ceramic or composite system.

The key principle is therefore material-specific margin design: cast metal benefits from appropriate beveling and finishing, whereas adhesive tooth-colored restorations require a margin compatible with the restorative material and bonding protocol.

Viva point: For a cast-gold inlay, cavosurface margins are smooth, continuous, and appropriately beveled/flared to provide supported enamel and adequate metal bulk. Do not automatically transfer cast-metal bevel designs to ceramic adhesive restorations.

Source basis: Sturdevant; Summitt

### 19. Would a Direct or Indirect composite Inlay be better?

When comparing direct and indirect composite inlays, the answer depends on the size of the restoration, the clinical situation, and the material-handling requirements. The uploaded sources generally favor direct composite restoration when a conservative direct restoration can predictably restore the tooth. An inlay is considered when specific clinical circumstances make indirect fabrication advantageous.

Indirect composite inlays are polymerized outside the mouth. This can increase the degree of conversion and improve certain physical properties, while polymerization shrinkage occurs largely outside the prepared tooth rather than directly within the cavity. Phillips notes that indirect processing can use additional light, heat, pressure, or combinations of these methods, producing a higher degree of polymerization and potentially improving wear resistance. Because shrinkage is not occurring within the preparation in the same manner as a direct restoration, some stresses and associated bond problems may be reduced.

Indirect composite inlays are also repairable intraorally and may be less abrasive to opposing tooth structure than some ceramics. Summitt, however, notes that direct composite should generally be considered first for moderate two- and three-surface inlay-type defects because it is more conservative.

Indirect composite becomes attractive when a large restoration makes contact development, contouring, or occlusal anatomy difficult to control directly, or when the laboratory can provide improved external polymerization and anatomy.

Viva point: If a direct composite can provide a predictable, conservative result, it is usually preferred. An indirect composite inlay becomes advantageous when restoration size, contact, occlusion, or laboratory polymerization benefits justify the additional indirect procedure.

### 20. Which material is best suited for Investment for a Gold Inlay?

The investment for a gold inlay must be selected according to the casting alloy, pattern, and required casting accuracy. For dental gold casting, the investment must withstand the casting temperature, reproduce fine detail, provide adequate strength, and compensate appropriately for the dimensional changes of the wax pattern and metal during casting.

Gypsum-bonded investments are traditionally used for many gold alloy castings because their thermal characteristics are compatible with lower-fusing dental gold alloys. The investment should provide sufficient setting and thermal expansion to offset casting shrinkage and should retain adequate strength during burnout and casting. Its porosity must also permit gases to escape as molten metal enters the mold.

The choice is therefore not based on the generic statement that one investment is “best” for every gold alloy. The investment must be compatible with the specific alloy and the laboratory casting technique. High-temperature alloys may require phosphate-bonded investment, whereas conventional lower-fusing gold alloys can use appropriate gypsum-bonded systems.

Manipulation is equally important. Powder-liquid ratio, mixing technique, working time, setting reaction, and burnout schedule affect the final mold. Errors in any of these stages can result in roughness, distortion, incomplete casting, or poor marginal accuracy.

For an inlay, fine-detail reproduction is particularly important because the margins are thin and the preparation contains small geometric features.

Viva point: For conventional dental gold inlays, a compatible gypsum-bonded investment is traditionally appropriate for lower-fusing gold alloys; higher-temperature alloys require investment systems designed for their casting temperature. Always match investment to the alloy and manufacturer’s casting protocol.

Source basis: Phillips; Craig

### 21. Which bur is used for giving cavosurface bevel?

A slender, flame-shaped, fine-grit diamond is the preferred instrument described in the operative dentistry sources for refining bevels and flares on cast-metal indirect preparations. Its shape permits controlled access to the margin and allows the operator to establish a smooth, continuous bevel without creating abrupt irregularities.

In cast-metal onlay and related preparations, Sturdevant specifically describes the use of a slender flame-shaped fine-grit diamond to place counterbevels on reduced cusps, gingival bevels, and secondary flares on facial and lingual proximal walls. ### 22. What is Flash?

Flash is an unwanted thin projection or excess of restorative material extending beyond the intended contour or margin of a restoration. In an indirect inlay, flash may arise from excess metal, investment-related irregularities, or defects in the wax pattern and casting process. It is usually removed during finishing and polishing before final cementation or, where appropriate, after cementation.

Flash is clinically undesirable because it can interfere with complete seating of the restoration, alter proximal or occlusal contours, irritate gingival tissues, and create plaque-retentive areas. A thin metal flash at the margin may also be mistaken for an acceptable extension, but it should not be retained merely because it appears small. The final restoration should reproduce the prepared margin and have a smooth, cleansable contour.

Sturdevant describes removal of excess material along the margins of indirect tooth-colored restorations using fine-grit diamonds, finishing burs, or appropriate interproximal instruments. Care is required because excessive trimming can damage the marginal edge or create an open margin. Interproximal finishing is particularly delicate because instruments can injure gingiva or root surfaces.

The presence of flash can also indicate a casting or pattern problem. It may result from a defective wax pattern, investment fracture, or separation at the mold interface. Therefore, the clinician or technician should not simply remove the flash without examining the underlying fit.

Viva point: Flash is an unwanted thin excess or projection of restorative material, commonly metal in a casting. It must be carefully removed without damaging the true margin, and its presence should prompt inspection for defects in the wax pattern or investment.

### 23. What are the dimensions of Inlay cavity?

The dimensions of an inlay preparation are not represented by one universal set of measurements because they depend on the restorative material, tooth anatomy, extent of the lesion, and whether the restoration is metallic, ceramic, or composite. The source texts emphasize principles of adequate bulk, conservative preparation, and a geometry that permits seating and finishing.

For cast-metal preparations, the occlusal portion must provide adequate restorative thickness without unnecessary removal of sound tooth structure. Sturdevant illustrates an occlusal step of approximately 0.5 mm in relevant cast-metal onlay preparation design and emphasizes that the isthmus should be only as wide as necessary to include sound tooth structure, caries, or existing restorative material. In areas requiring cuspal coverage, greater reduction is required to provide adequate material thickness.

Proximal boxes must be extended sufficiently to eliminate defective structure, expose the required enamel margin, and provide access for impression and finishing procedures. Their walls should have a predictable path of draw and should avoid unsupported enamel. Sharp internal line angles should be rounded.

For ceramic inlays, preparation geometry must also account for the material’s brittleness. Internal and external line and point angles should be generously rounded to reduce stress concentration. The preparation should avoid sharp corners and unsupported thin ceramic.

Therefore, the correct viva approach is to state the principle first and then give material-specific dimensions when requested. The preparation must be large enough to provide adequate material bulk and resistance but as small as possible consistent with disease removal and restoration longevity.

## POST AND CORE

### 1. What are the indications for Post and Core?

A post and core is indicated primarily when substantial coronal tooth structure has been lost and the remaining tooth cannot adequately retain the definitive coronal restoration by itself. The post is not intended to strengthen the root; its principal function is to retain the core when insufficient coronal tooth structure remains. The core then provides a suitable foundation for the definitive crown or other restoration.

The decision must begin with assessment of restorability, periodontal support, endodontic status, remaining dentin, crown-root relationship, and occlusal demands. A tooth with adequate remaining coronal walls and ferrule may not require a post even if it has undergone root canal treatment. Conversely, a severely compromised tooth with insufficient coronal retention may require a post and core if adequate root structure and periodontal support remain.

The source texts emphasize preservation of dentin. Excessive post-space preparation or enlargement increases stress and can predispose to root fracture or perforation. Post length is generally more important for retention than increasing post diameter, while increasing diameter removes dentin and can increase fracture risk. At least about 5 mm of apical gutta-percha should be retained to preserve the apical seal.

A ferrule of sound tooth structure around the crown is highly desirable because it improves fracture resistance. If adequate ferrule cannot be obtained, periodontal or orthodontic procedures may be considered when appropriate.

Viva point: A post is indicated for retention of a core when coronal tooth structure is inadequate. It is not placed simply because a tooth has had root canal treatment.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics; Summitt; Sturdevant

### 2. When is the ideal time to prepare the post space?

Post-space preparation may be performed immediately after completion of root canal obturation when the obturation is adequately condensed and the apical seal can be protected. The uploaded Ingle text notes that adequately condensed gutta-percha can be safely removed immediately after endodontic treatment. Delaying the procedure is not inherently required to allow the sealer to “mature,” provided the obturation is satisfactory and the clinician can preserve the apical seal.

The major objective is to remove coronal gutta-percha while leaving an adequate apical segment intact. The recommended apical remnant is at least 5 mm in routine situations because smaller remnants are associated with greater leakage. The post space should also be prepared conservatively and in the long axis of the root to avoid unnecessary dentin removal and perforation.

Post-space preparation can be carried out with heated instruments, rotary instruments, or combinations. Ingle reports that both hot instruments and rotary systems can safely remove gutta-percha when adequate apical gutta-percha is retained. Mechanical enlargement should be controlled because excessive diameter can weaken the root.

The final timing also depends on clinical circumstances. If the endodontic treatment itself is uncertain, symptoms are uncontrolled, or the tooth requires observation, definitive post placement may be deferred. Conversely, when the tooth has a satisfactory endodontic result and immediate restoration is indicated, post-space preparation can be completed during the same treatment sequence.

Viva point: There is no obligatory waiting period. With a well-condensed obturation, the post space can be prepared immediately, provided at least about 5 mm of apical gutta-percha is preserved.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics

### 3. Can a Post and Core be given if the periapical lesion has not resolved fully and why?

A post and core should not be regarded as dependent on complete radiographic disappearance of a periapical lesion. The essential issue is whether the endodontic treatment is adequate and whether the tooth is clinically suitable for definitive restoration. A periapical radiolucency may take considerable time to resolve after successful treatment, and radiographic healing should not be mistaken for the immediate criterion determining whether the tooth can be restored.

However, a persistent lesion requires careful diagnosis. If the canal is inadequately treated, contaminated, missed, poorly obturated, or associated with persistent symptoms, definitive post-and-core treatment may be premature because the tooth may require nonsurgical retreatment or surgical management. Placing a post can make future access more difficult and therefore should be justified by the restorative needs of the tooth.

The post itself does not improve periapical healing. Its function is mechanical: it retains the core when there is insufficient coronal tooth structure. The clinician must therefore first establish that the tooth is restorable, the root canal treatment is satisfactory, periodontal support is acceptable, and the proposed restoration has a favorable prognosis.

Ingle emphasizes that a definitive coronal seal is important for preventing coronal leakage and treatment failure. The post and core should therefore be followed by an appropriate coronal restoration without unnecessarily delaying the coronal seal.

Viva point: A stable, adequately treated tooth may be restored before complete radiographic resolution of a lesion, because healing can lag behind treatment. But an unresolved lesion associated with inadequate endodontic treatment or persistent disease should be investigated and managed before definitive post placement.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics; Cohen’s Pathways of the Pulp

### 4. How much Gutta Percha is left over ideally after post space preparation and why?

At least approximately 5 mm of apical gutta-percha should ideally remain after post-space preparation. The reason is preservation of the apical seal. Removing too much obturating material increases the possibility of leakage and may allow microorganisms or their byproducts to reach the periradicular tissues.

Ingle’s review of the evidence reports greater leakage when only 2–3 mm of gutta-percha remains. Although some experimental studies have demonstrated an adequate seal with 4 mm, the text recommends retaining at least 5 mm because accurately stopping at exactly 4 mm is difficult and radiographic measurement can introduce errors. A 5-mm remnant therefore provides a practical safety margin.

The post should be extended only as far as needed for adequate retention and resistance while respecting the root anatomy. In long roots, a post may extend to approximately three-fourths of root length, but the apical seal must still be preserved. In average-length roots, post length is often dictated by the requirement to leave 5 mm of apical filling. In molars, posts should be conservative because root anatomy and thin dentin increase the risk of perforation.

The remaining gutta-percha must also be dense and undisturbed. A nominal 5-mm length is not useful if the apical filling is poorly condensed or damaged during post preparation.

Viva point: Retain at least 5 mm of well-condensed apical gutta-percha. This preserves the apical seal while allowing sufficient post length coronally. The post should never be lengthened at the expense of the apical seal.

### 5. What are advantages of Custom Post and Core over Prefabricated?

A custom cast post and core is fabricated specifically for the individual prepared root canal and coronal tooth anatomy. Its major advantage is adaptation. The post can be made to closely conform to the shape and length of the prepared canal, while the core can be designed simultaneously to reproduce the required coronal form and provide a foundation for the definitive crown.

This is particularly useful when the remaining coronal tooth structure is irregular, when the canal is unusually shaped, or when substantial coronal reconstruction is required. Because the post and core are a single cast component, there is no separate interface between a prefabricated post and a separately built core. The custom design can also provide favorable resistance form and permit correction of certain coronal angulations.

However, the custom post is not automatically superior in every case. Its preparation is more invasive and technique-sensitive, requires an impression or pattern, laboratory fabrication, and a temporary restoration, and may require removal of more coronal structure. A prefabricated fiber post can often be placed more conservatively and may provide a favorable stress distribution because its elastic behavior is closer to dentin.

The source texts emphasize that post diameter should not be increased unnecessarily and that preserving dentin is critical. Therefore, the advantage of a custom post is mainly anatomical adaptation and integrated core design, not “strengthening” the root.

Viva point: Custom post and core is advantageous when canal and coronal anatomy are irregular or when an integrated, individually shaped foundation is needed. Prefabricated posts are often more conservative and efficient when anatomy is favorable.

### 6. What is Ferrule?

A ferrule is a circumferential band of sound tooth structure that is embraced by the definitive crown above the finish line. It acts like a reinforcing collar around the remaining tooth and helps resist functional stresses that tend to separate or fracture the restored tooth. The ferrule is therefore a feature of the remaining tooth and crown relationship, not simply a component of the post.

The most clinically important ferrule dimension is generally around 1.5–2 mm of sound axial tooth structure when achievable. Ingle’s review describes studies showing that a substantive ferrule improves fracture resistance and that a 2-mm crown ferrule is consistently effective. Some investigations suggest additional benefit with greater ferrule height, but preservation of sound tooth structure remains the guiding principle.

The ferrule is important because endodontically treated teeth often have lost substantial coronal structure through caries, previous restorations, access preparation, and restorative procedures. Under functional loading, a crown that encircles sound tooth structure can help resist flexure and distribute stresses more favorably.

A ferrule should ideally be continuous and uniform around the tooth. Ingle reports greater fracture resistance with a uniform ferrule than with a nonuniform ferrule. When adequate ferrule cannot be obtained because the clinical crown is too short, procedures such as crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion may sometimes be considered if they improve the restorative prognosis without compromising periodontal support.

Viva point: Ferrule = circumferential sound tooth structure embraced by the crown. Aim for about 2 mm when possible; a continuous ferrule substantially improves resistance to fracture and is generally more important than simply increasing post length.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics; Summitt

### 7. What are types of ferrules?

Ferrules can be described according to their continuity, uniformity, and relationship to the remaining tooth structure. Clinically, the most important distinction is between a uniform circumferential ferrule and a nonuniform or incomplete ferrule. A uniform ferrule provides a relatively consistent band of sound tooth structure around the tooth, whereas a nonuniform ferrule has different amounts of remaining tooth structure at different surfaces.

A circumferential crown ferrule is the preferred design because it allows the crown to engage sound tooth structure around the tooth and distribute functional stresses more favorably. Ingle’s evidence review describes a study comparing a 2-mm uniform ferrule with a nonuniform ferrule in which the proximal ferrule was only 0.5 mm. The uniform ferrule provided significantly greater fracture resistance.

Other descriptions include partial ferrules, where the crown engages sound tooth structure on only some surfaces, and sloping or beveled ferrules. The source material indicates that sloping ferrules are less consistently effective than substantive circumferential ferrules. A ferrule that is minimal or discontinuous provides much less protection.

The ferrule may also be discussed in relation to core and crown design. A “core ferrule” and “crown ferrule” are not identical concepts, although both describe engagement of remaining tooth structure. For definitive crown treatment, the clinically relevant goal is sound tooth structure above the finish line that the crown can encircle.

Viva point: The preferred ferrule is a continuous, uniform circumferential ferrule of sound tooth structure, ideally around 1.5–2 mm or more when possible. Partial, nonuniform, or sloping ferrules are less predictable.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics

### 8. What is the function of ferrule?

The principal function of a ferrule is to improve the fracture resistance of a restored endodontically treated tooth by allowing the definitive crown to encircle sound tooth structure. This circumferential engagement acts as a protective collar and helps resist forces that might otherwise concentrate at the junction between the core, post, and remaining root.

Ingle’s review shows that ferrule length is an important determinant of resistance to fracture. A crown ferrule of approximately 1.5–2 mm has been found to be effective, and studies have shown that increasing ferrule length can increase the number of loading cycles required to produce failure. The ferrule therefore has a biomechanical role that cannot be replaced simply by increasing post length.

A ferrule also helps protect the remaining coronal dentin from wedging and functional forces. This is particularly relevant in teeth with large amounts of lost structure, where the restorative complex otherwise depends heavily on the post-core interface. The post primarily retains the core; the ferrule helps protect the tooth-restoration complex.

The ferrule should be continuous where possible. Incomplete or nonuniform ferrules provide less predictable reinforcement. If the available clinical crown does not provide adequate ferrule, the clinician may consider whether crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion can expose additional sound tooth structure while maintaining a favorable crown-root relationship and periodontal health.

Viva point: The ferrule is a stress-resisting collar of sound tooth structure. Its function is to improve resistance to fracture and reduce the likelihood that functional forces will concentrate at the post/core-root interface. It is one of the most important determinants of the prognosis of a restored endodontically treated tooth.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics; Summitt

### 9. What is the function of the post?

The primary function of a post is retention of the core when insufficient coronal tooth structure remains to retain the core independently. A post does not intrinsically strengthen an endodontically treated tooth. In fact, excessive post-space preparation can weaken the root by removing dentin and increasing the risk of fracture or perforation.

The distinction between retention and reinforcement is fundamental. The core replaces missing coronal structure and provides a foundation for the definitive restoration. The post extends into the prepared root canal to anchor that core. The definitive crown and ferrule then contribute substantially to the overall biomechanical behavior of the restored tooth.

Post length is generally more important for retention than post diameter. Ingle reports mixed evidence regarding diameter and retention, whereas increasing diameter has a more consistent association with increased tooth stress and reduced fracture resistance. Therefore, the post should be as conservative as possible while providing adequate retention.

The post should also respect the apical seal. At least about 5 mm of gutta-percha should remain apically, and the post should be placed in a root with adequate dentinal thickness and favorable anatomy. It should follow the canal rather than force the root into a new shape.

Fiber posts and cast posts differ in material behavior and fabrication, but the basic biological principle remains the same: the post is a retention device, not a root-strengthening device.

Viva point: “Post = retention of core.” It is not a reinforcement device. Preserve dentin, maintain the apical seal, and use a post only when the remaining coronal structure cannot adequately retain the core.

Source basis: Ingle’s Endodontics; Summitt; Craig

### 10. What is important—Post Length or Post Diameter?

When considering retention, post length is generally more important than increasing post diameter. Ingle’s review reports that studies have produced mixed results concerning the effect of post diameter on retention, whereas post length has a more consistent relationship with retention. Increasing diameter, however, has a clearer biomechanical disadvantage because it removes additional dentin and may increase root stress.

A longer post can provide greater surface area for retention while preserving a conservative diameter. The post should extend sufficiently into the root to provide retention but must not compromise the apical seal or create a perforation. In long roots, a post may approach about three-fourths of root length, whereas in average roots the length is limited by the need to retain approximately 5 mm of apical gutta-percha.

Post diameter should therefore be kept as small as practical. Enlargement should not be performed merely to accommodate a thicker prefabricated post. Excessive diameter reduces the thickness of remaining dentin and can predispose to fracture. Ingle cites evidence that increasing post diameter can increase stress and decrease fracture resistance.

The ferrule and remaining tooth structure are also more important to the prognosis than simply making the post large. A well-designed ferrule with a conservative, adequately retentive post is preferable to a large post in a tooth with thin root walls.

Viva point: For post retention, favor adequate **length** over excessive **diameter**. Diameter should be conservative because enlarging the post space removes dentin and increases the risk of root fracture and perforation.

### 11. What occlusal considerations are taken into account before planning Post and Core?

Occlusal assessment is essential before planning a post and core because the prognosis of an endodontically treated tooth depends not only on the post but also on the magnitude and direction of functional forces. The clinician should evaluate the tooth’s position in the arch, contact relationships, opposing dentition, parafunctional activity, cusp inclinations, available restorative space, and whether the tooth will receive cuspal coverage.

Posterior teeth generally experience greater occlusal loading than anterior teeth, and teeth with reduced remaining walls may be more vulnerable to fracture. The definitive restoration should therefore provide adequate cuspal protection where indicated. If the remaining cusps are thin or significantly weakened, a full-coverage or cuspal-coverage restoration may be more predictable than a simple intracoronal restoration.

The direction of occlusal forces is also relevant. A post should not be used to correct major tooth inclination at the expense of excessive root preparation. Where correction of the crown position is needed, the clinician should determine whether a custom post-core, restorative design, orthodontic movement, or another approach is more appropriate.

Parafunctional habits such as bruxism increase the functional demands on the restoration and may alter the design and prognosis. The amount and quality of remaining coronal tooth structure and the presence of a ferrule must also be considered before deciding that a post is appropriate.

Viva point: Before planning a post and core, assess occlusal load, tooth position, opposing contacts, parafunction, remaining cusps, ferrule, and the need for cuspal coverage. The post should support a predictable restoration rather than compensate for an unfavorable occlusal situation.

### 12. Up to how much degree is inclination of teeth corrected by post and core?

A custom post and core can sometimes be designed to compensate for a limited difference between the long axis of the root and the desired path of the definitive crown. The purpose is to provide a suitable coronal foundation while maintaining a conservative root preparation. However, there is no justification for aggressively altering the root canal merely to correct a large coronal inclination.

The uploaded sources emphasize preservation of root dentin and avoidance of perforation. Therefore, the practical principle is that only modest angulation correction should be attempted through post-and-core design, and the amount is dictated by the anatomy and restorative requirements rather than by a universal “safe” angle. If a large correction is required, alternative approaches should be considered.

A custom cast post and core can be advantageous in this situation because the post can follow the existing canal while the core is shaped to provide a different coronal axis. This is one reason custom systems can be useful when tooth inclination and coronal anatomy are unfavorable.

The clinician must also consider crown retention and resistance, ferrule, periodontal support, and the thickness of remaining dentin. A large change in the coronal axis may create eccentric forces and an unfavorable crown-root relationship.

### 13. Why is gold ideal for Custom Post and Core?

Gold dental casting alloys have traditionally been favored for custom post and core fabrication because they combine castability, corrosion resistance, ductility, strength, and dimensional reliability. The alloy can be cast into the individual root-canal and coronal anatomy, allowing the post and core to be fabricated as a single integrated unit.

Gold is chemically stable and resistant to corrosion and tarnish in the oral environment. Its mechanical behavior can also be modified through alloying. Phillips describes gold-copper systems and notes that the mechanical properties of dental gold alloys depend on composition and heat treatment. Stronger Type III and Type IV alloys can be age hardened because their compositions permit ordering reactions, whereas Type I and II alloys have insufficient copper for significant age hardening.

For a post and core, the alloy must tolerate functional loading without excessive deformation while still permitting laboratory finishing and adjustment. Gold alloys also have a long history of predictable casting and adaptation. The relatively favorable handling characteristics of cast gold allow the technician to refine the margins and core geometry accurately.

Gold is not “ideal” because it makes the root stronger. The design of the post, the amount of remaining dentin, ferrule, post length, and coronal restoration are much more important determinants of tooth survival. Modern fiber-reinforced systems can offer important advantages in selected situations.

Viva point: Gold is traditionally ideal for custom cast post and core because it is corrosion resistant, castable, strong yet workable, and can be fabricated accurately as a one-piece restoration. Its advantage is material performance and adaptability—not reinforcement of the root.

### 14. Which type of gold is used and what is its composition?

Custom cast post and core restorations traditionally use dental casting gold alloys selected for adequate strength and hardness rather than pure gold. The classification of gold casting alloys is based on their mechanical properties and composition. The stronger alloys are used when greater resistance to deformation is required.

Pure gold is too soft for most demanding cast restorative applications. Alloying gold with elements such as copper, silver, platinum-group metals, and other components increases strength, hardness, and resistance to deformation. Phillips describes the importance of gold-copper interactions and the effect of alloy composition on mechanical properties. Type III and Type IV alloys contain sufficient alloying constituents to permit strengthening reactions and are harder than the lower-strength types.

The exact composition varies by manufacturer, so a single universal percentage should not be applied to every commercial alloy. A typical high-gold dental alloy is a multiphase alloy in which gold provides corrosion resistance and the other metals modify strength, hardness, melting behavior, and workability.

For a post and core, the chosen alloy should be compatible with the casting technique and definitive restoration. The material must produce an accurate casting, resist functional deformation, and remain clinically stable. The operator should also consider galvanic compatibility with other restorations and the final crown.

Viva point: Use a dental gold casting alloy, traditionally a higher-strength Type III/IV-type alloy when indicated, rather than pure gold. The alloy combines gold with strengthening elements; exact composition is product-specific. Gold contributes corrosion resistance, while alloying controls strength, hardness, and casting behavior.

### 15. What is the composition of Japanese Gold?

“Japanese gold” is a historical dental alloy term and should be answered carefully because older teaching texts and commercial products have used the term inconsistently. It generally refers to a high-gold dental casting alloy used historically for cast restorations rather than elemental gold. The clinically relevant point is that the material is an alloy whose properties depend on the proportions of gold and alloying metals.

Dental gold alloys commonly contain gold combined with copper and silver, with platinum-group or other alloying elements used in some formulations. These additions alter hardness, strength, melting behavior, color, corrosion resistance, and heat-treatment response. Phillips emphasizes that the gold-copper system can develop ordered structures and that the amount of copper influences hardening behavior.

Because “Japanese gold” is not a standardized modern ISO designation, its exact composition should not be stated as though one universal formula exists across all sources and products. In a viva, it is safer to identify it as a traditional high-gold casting alloy and then describe the functional role of the alloying constituents.

For custom post and core work, the important properties are accurate castability, adequate strength, corrosion resistance, and sufficient ductility for adjustment. The alloy should not be selected merely because it has a high percentage of gold; its mechanical properties must suit the restoration.

### 16. Discuss casting failures encountered in Post and Core.

Casting failures in a custom post and core may arise from errors in the pattern, spruing, investing, burnout, alloy melting, casting, or finishing stages. Common defects include incomplete casting, rounded or missing margins, porosity, nodules, fins or flash, surface roughness, distortion, cracks, and internal voids.

Incomplete casting may occur when the molten alloy solidifies before completely filling the mold, when the sprue system is inadequate, or when casting conditions are inappropriate. Porosity may result from solidification shrinkage, gas entrapment, or an unsuitable sprue/reservoir design. Fins and flash can arise from cracks or separation in the investment. Surface roughness may result from investment problems, improper burnout, or contamination.

Pattern distortion is another important failure. Wax has significant thermal dimensional changes, so excessive heating, repeated manipulation, or delayed investing can alter the pattern. The investment must reproduce the pattern accurately and maintain adequate strength during casting. After casting, improper divestment or finishing can damage thin margins.

Post-and-core castings have the additional problem of root-canal adaptation. A technically good casting may still fail clinically if the pattern does not reproduce the canal accurately, if the post is too large, or if the casting creates excessive stress in thin root dentin.

Viva point: Think of casting failure in sequence—**pattern → sprue → investment → burnout → alloy/casting → divestment → finishing**. A defect at any stage can produce an inaccurate post and core. The most important preventive principles are accurate patterning, correct sprue design, compatible investment, controlled burnout/casting, and conservative finishing.

### 17. Why is a custom tray preferred for Post and Core over Prefabricated tray?

A custom tray is preferred when a highly accurate impression of a prepared root canal and coronal tooth is required because it provides more controlled and uniform impression-material thickness. The tray is fabricated specifically for the patient and can therefore reduce the amount of impression material required compared with a stock tray.

Sturdevant describes custom trays made over a spacer, with an impression-material thickness in the approximate range that balances dimensional stability and resistance to tearing. Excessively thick impression material can increase polymerization shrinkage and the possibility of voids, whereas an excessively thin layer can distort or tear. A custom tray helps control this relationship.

For a custom post and core, accurate reproduction of the post-space preparation, coronal walls, margins, and adjacent structures is essential. ### 18. What is the technique of cord placement for tissue retraction?

Retraction cord is placed to temporarily displace the free gingiva and expose a subgingival preparation margin while controlling sulcular fluid and minor hemorrhage. The goal is to widen the sulcus, not to traumatize or push the tissue unnecessarily apically.

### 21. Which is the best finish-line location and why—Supra, Equi or Subgingival?

### 22. What is the importance of Biologic Width?

The biologic width is the combined dimension of the junctional epithelium and supracrestal connective tissue attachment between the base of the gingival sulcus and the alveolar crest. It is important because restorative margins that encroach on this attachment can provoke inflammation, tissue instability, recession, or periodontal breakdown.

Summitt describes the dentogingival junction as the junctional epithelium plus the subjacent connective tissue attachment. The source reports a mean combined dimension of approximately 1.91 mm in adult cadaver studies, although individual variation exists. The clinician should therefore avoid treating a single numerical value as an absolute measurement for every patient.

Before placing a deep restorative margin, probing and, when necessary, bone sounding help determine the relationship between the gingival margin and alveolar crest. High-crest situations, where the crest lies unusually close to the gingival margin, carry a higher risk of biologic-width violation. In such cases, supragingival or equigingival margins are generally preferred.

Violation of the attachment can lead to chronic inflammation and unpredictable soft-tissue changes. If additional tooth structure is needed for a ferrule or restorative margin, crown lengthening or orthodontic extrusion may sometimes be considered rather than simply placing the margin deeper.

### 23. What is the maximum recommended dowel size for a maxillary incisor?

The guiding principle for post diameter in a maxillary incisor is preservation of surrounding root dentin rather than enlargement to a fixed maximum size. ### 24. Should a uniform or nonuniform ferrule be given?

A uniform circumferential ferrule is preferred whenever the available sound tooth structure permits it. A uniform ferrule means that the definitive crown engages a relatively consistent band of sound tooth structure around the tooth. This provides more predictable resistance to functional forces than an incomplete or markedly nonuniform ferrule.

Ingle’s review directly compares uniform and nonuniform ferrule designs. A 2-mm uniform ferrule produced significantly greater fracture resistance than a nonuniform ferrule in which the proximal ferrule was reduced to approximately 0.5 mm. The evidence therefore supports maintaining as much continuous sound tooth structure as possible.

A nonuniform ferrule may sometimes be unavoidable because of caries, fracture, previous restorations, or limited clinical crown height. In such cases, the clinician should determine whether additional sound tooth structure can be obtained through appropriate periodontal or orthodontic procedures. The decision must also consider crown-root ratio, periodontal support, esthetics, and the overall restorability of the tooth.

A ferrule should not be created by aggressively removing sound dentin merely to achieve symmetry. The purpose is to preserve and encircle existing sound structure.

Viva point: **Uniform circumferential ferrule is preferred.** A continuous band of about 1.5–2 mm or more is desirable when possible. ### 25. What is Split Cast Technique?

The split-cast technique is a method of constructing a working cast so that a removable die or cast segment can be separated from the remainder of the model and accurately repositioned. It is useful in indirect restorative procedures because the prepared tooth must be accessible for waxing, finishing, margin inspection, and fabrication while still maintaining its exact relationship with adjacent teeth.

The basic concept is mechanical indexing. The cast is divided or prepared so that the die segment can be removed without losing its original spatial position. Dowel pins, sleeves, grooves, or other indexing features may be incorporated depending on the system. When the die is replaced, the indexing mechanism ensures reproducible seating.

The technique is important because even small positional errors can alter the proximal contacts, occlusion, marginal fit, and path of insertion of an inlay or post-and-core restoration. The split-cast system therefore improves laboratory access while maintaining positional accuracy.

The working cast must be fabricated from an accurate impression and a dimensionally stable die material. A technically excellent split-cast system cannot correct an inaccurate impression or distorted model.

Viva point: Split cast is a **removable and repositionable working-cast system**. Its purpose is to provide access to the prepared tooth while preserving the exact relationship between the die, adjacent teeth, and occlusion through mechanical indexing.

### 12. What type of suture material is used for closure of the flap?

### 13. If the patient returns with postoperative swelling, what is the line of management?

### 1. What is Hermetic Seal?

A hermetic seal in endodontics refers to an obturation that prevents meaningful communication between the prepared root canal system and the periradicular tissues. The concept is broader than simply filling the canal with a solid material. The canal must be filled as completely as practical, with the obturating material and sealer adapted to the prepared walls and anatomical irregularities.

Gutta-percha is the standard solid core material because it is biocompatible, thermoplastic, radiopaque, chemically stable, and retrievable. However, gutta-percha by itself does not bond reliably to dentin and therefore cannot produce a hermetic seal on its own. A sealer is required to occupy microscopic spaces between the core material and canal wall and to compensate for irregularities.

The source texts emphasize that obturation is only one component of endodontic success. Cleaning, shaping, irrigation, disinfection, and an effective coronal seal are equally important. No obturation technique can compensate for persistent intraradicular infection or inadequate coronal restoration.

Warm vertical compaction, lateral condensation, thermoplasticized techniques, and carrier-based methods attempt to improve adaptation and reduce voids. Even so, complete elimination of microleakage is not guaranteed.

Viva point: A hermetic seal means a **dense, three-dimensional barrier against communication and microbial leakage**. It depends on proper cleaning and shaping, a well-adapted gutta-percha/sealer combination, adequate apical control, and an effective coronal seal. It is a biological goal rather than a claim that absolute leak-proof obturation is possible.

### 2. What is the ideal point where obturation is terminated?

Obturation should terminate at the prepared apical endpoint established during cleaning and shaping, ideally close to the apical constriction when that anatomical landmark can be identified. The objective is to fill the canal to the controlled working length without intentionally overextending the filling material into the periradicular tissues.

The apical constriction is commonly regarded as the preferred termination region because it provides a natural narrowing against which the obturation can be compacted. However, the apical anatomy is variable and may not correspond exactly to a fixed radiographic distance from the anatomical apex. Working length should therefore be determined using radiographic and electronic methods together with tactile and anatomical information.

The source texts emphasize the importance of the apical one-third because it contains complex anatomy and represents the critical terminal portion of the canal. Overextension can irritate tissues and complicate healing, whereas inadequate length may leave infected space unfilled.

The ideal endpoint is therefore biological and anatomical rather than simply “0.5 or 1 mm short of the radiographic apex” in every tooth. The final radiograph should show a dense filling ending at the intended working length, with no significant voids or extrusion.

Current Therapy explains that beta-phase gutta-percha is stable and flexible at room temperature, remaining relatively solid and compactible. Alpha-phase gutta-percha is more brittle at room temperature but becomes runny, tacky, and sticky when heated, making it particularly useful for thermoplasticized techniques.

For conventional cold lateral condensation, beta-phase gutta-percha is advantageous because it is solid, flexible, and readily compacted with spreaders and accessory cones. For warm vertical or thermoplasticized techniques, alpha-phase gutta-percha is useful because heating changes its physical state and allows it to flow and adapt to irregular canal anatomy.

The answer is therefore technique-dependent rather than a universal statement that one phase is always superior. The phase transition produced by heating is precisely what makes warm techniques possible.

Gutta-percha cones also contain other components—approximately 20% gutta-percha, 65% zinc oxide, 10% radiopacifiers, and 5% plasticizers according to Current Therapy. These additives influence handling and physical properties.

### 13. When is sectional filling indicated?

Sectional filling refers to a technique in which a portion of the canal, usually the apical segment, is obturated as a separate section rather than filling the entire canal with a single continuous mass. It is particularly associated with difficult canals, short working lengths, or situations in which conventional compaction is difficult to control.

### 15. Which obturating material shows spaghetti formation?

### 2. What is the advantage of anti-torquing function in modern rotary endodontics?

### 4. What is a glide path?

### 6. What are Schilder’s principles of cleaning and shaping?

Schilder’s principles describe the biological and mechanical objectives of root canal preparation. The major objective is to remove tissue, microorganisms, and debris while creating a continuously tapered canal form that follows the original anatomy and facilitates obturation.

The classic principles include development of a continuously tapering preparation from apical to coronal, maintenance of the original canal curvature and position of the apical foramen, and avoidance of procedural errors such as ledging, zipping, transportation, perforation, and blockage. The apical portion should be prepared to a controlled size, while the coronal portion is progressively enlarged to provide adequate irrigation and obturation space.

The concept of “cleaning” is biological and includes removal of vital or necrotic pulp tissue, microorganisms, and dentinal debris. “Shaping” is mechanical and creates the form necessary for irrigation, disinfection, and filling.

Modern Ni-Ti instruments have changed how these objectives are achieved, but the underlying principles remain relevant. Contemporary systems should be used to preserve dentin and maintain the original canal trajectory.

Viva point: Schilder’s philosophy is **cleaning plus shaping while preserving anatomy**: continuously tapered preparation, maintenance of the apical position, adequate space for irrigation and obturation, and avoidance of procedural errors.

### 19. What is the disadvantage of using Ni-Ti files?

### 24. How would you manage a case of internal resorption?

### 26. What techniques are employed to calculate canal curvatures?

Canal curvature can be assessed by radiographic methods and by quantitative geometric approaches. ### 28. What is the disadvantage of Ni-Ti rotary systems in excessively curved canals?

The main disadvantage is increased risk of cyclic fatigue and instrument separation. In an excessively curved canal, the instrument undergoes repeated flexural stress as it rotates through the curvature. The more abrupt or severe the curve, the greater the stress concentration.

Rotary instruments can also create transportation or ledging if the preparation sequence is inappropriate or if the file is forced. Even flexible Ni-Ti instruments can separate when the operator ignores canal anatomy, uses excessive apical pressure, or continues using a fatigued file.

### 29. What are elbowing, zipping and ledging?

These are procedural errors produced when instrumentation fails to respect the original canal anatomy.

A **ledge** is an artificial irregularity created on the canal wall that prevents an instrument from reaching the established working length. It commonly results from forcing a stiff file in a curved canal, inadequate precurving, insufficient irrigation, or failure to maintain a glide path.

**Zipping** is excessive removal of dentin from the outer wall of the apical curvature, producing an elliptical or tear-shaped apical preparation and displacement of the apical foramen. It is associated with straightening forces from larger or stiff instruments.

**Elbowing** is the narrow constriction created coronally to a transported or zipped apical preparation. The canal assumes an hourglass-like configuration: excessive enlargement apically with a constricted region coronally.

These errors can compromise cleaning, obturation, and the apical seal. Prevention depends on careful working-length control, precurving hand files, balanced-force or flexible Ni-Ti techniques, adequate glide-path preparation, and conservative enlargement.

### 2. What is the composition of a bleaching gel?

A dental bleaching gel consists principally of a peroxide-based active ingredient carried in a gel vehicle. The active agents are usually hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide. Carbamide peroxide decomposes to release hydrogen peroxide, which is the principal oxidizing species responsible for bleaching.

The gel vehicle controls handling, contact with the tooth surface, and release of the active ingredient. Formulations may also contain water, thickening agents, stabilizers, flavoring components, and other formulation ingredients. The exact composition varies by product and concentration.

The mechanism is oxidation of chromogenic molecules within enamel and dentin. Peroxide generates reactive oxygen species that break down larger pigmented molecules into smaller, less light-absorbing compounds.

## DENTIN BONDING AGENTS

### 1. What is a Hybrid Layer?

The hybrid layer is the resin-infiltrated zone formed when adhesive resin penetrates the demineralized superficial dentin created during conditioning. It consists of exposed collagen and residual mineralized dentin infiltrated by resin monomers. The hybrid layer forms a major component of the micromechanical bond between resin composite and dentin.

With an etch-and-rinse system, phosphoric acid removes the smear layer and demineralizes superficial dentin. The exposed collagen network must remain adequately supported and hydrated so that primer and adhesive can infiltrate it. If it collapses, resin infiltration is reduced and bond quality can fall.

The hybrid layer should not be judged simply by thickness. A relatively thin but completely infiltrated layer may provide a stronger interface than a thick layer with incomplete resin penetration.

Viva point: Hybrid layer = **resin-infiltrated, demineralized dentin containing collagen and adhesive resin**. Its quality depends on complete resin infiltration and good interfacial integrity, not merely on thickness.

Clinical interpretation: Dentin bonding is controlled interaction between the tooth substrate and an adhesive polymer. The clinician must manage smear layer, dentin moisture, demineralization depth, primer penetration and adhesive polymerization. The objective is not to create the thickest possible hybrid layer but a stable, well-infiltrated interface. Technique sensitivity is therefore central to understanding differences between etch-and-rinse and self-etch systems.

Viva emphasis: Remember the sequence as **condition → infiltrate → polymerize → protect**. Etch-and-rinse systems remove the smear layer; self-etch systems modify and incorporate it. Both can work predictably when used correctly. The most common errors arise from inappropriate moisture, excessive etching, inadequate solvent evaporation or incomplete adhesive polymerization.

### 6. Is hybrid-layer thickness critical for better bond strength?

No. Hybrid-layer thickness alone is not a reliable predictor of bond strength or durability. What matters more is the quality and completeness of resin infiltration into the demineralized dentin and the integrity of the adhesive interface.

A thick hybrid layer can be poorly infiltrated, leaving exposed collagen beneath the adhesive. Conversely, a thinner hybrid layer can be completely resin-infiltrated and provide effective bonding.

### 5. When do false-positive and false-negative results occur in pulp vitality tests?

### 17. What is the role of systemic diseases in selecting a case for endodontic therapy?

Systemic disease influences endodontic case selection by altering the patient’s ability to tolerate treatment, heal after procedures, respond to infection, or safely receive medications and local anesthetics. The clinician must therefore obtain a complete medical history before treatment.

Conditions affecting cardiovascular, endocrine, immune, hematologic, renal, hepatic, and metabolic systems may require treatment modification or medical consultation. Poorly controlled systemic disease can increase procedural risk and may make elective treatment inappropriate until the condition is stabilized.

The source texts emphasize that dental treatment should be coordinated with the patient’s medical condition. Immunosuppression, transplantation, anticoagulant therapy, uncontrolled diabetes, and significant cardiovascular disease may require individualized planning.

Viva point: Systemic disease is **not automatically a contraindication to endodontics**, but it may alter timing, anesthesia, antibiotic use, bleeding management, healing expectations, and need for medical consultation.

### 18. What are the criteria for selecting a patient with cardiovascular disease for endodontic therapy?

The patient should be assessed according to the stability and severity of the cardiovascular condition. Stable, medically controlled disease generally permits routine endodontic treatment with appropriate precautions. Unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction, significant uncontrolled arrhythmia, poorly controlled heart failure, or other unstable conditions require medical assessment and often postponement of elective treatment.

Stress reduction and profound local anesthesia are important because endogenous catecholamine release from pain and anxiety may be more significant than the carefully controlled amount of vasoconstrictor in local anesthetic.

Vasoconstrictors should be used cautiously and within accepted limits. Medical history should include current medications and previous cardiac events.

Viva point: Treat cardiovascular patients when the condition is **stable and medically controlled**. Use stress reduction, profound anesthesia, cautious vasoconstrictor dosing, short comfortable appointments and medical consultation for unstable disease.

### 19. What are contraindications for single-visit endodontics?

Single-visit endodontics is inappropriate when the clinician cannot predictably complete adequate cleaning, disinfection and obturation in one appointment. Important situations include uncontrolled exudation, inability to dry the canal, severe acute symptoms that require stabilization, complex anatomy that cannot be adequately managed within the appointment, and uncertainty about the diagnosis or treatment quality.

A patient’s medical or psychological condition may also influence appointment length and tolerance. If additional intracanal medication is considered necessary, multiple visits may be preferable.

The presence of a periapical lesion alone is not a contraindication. Likewise, a necrotic pulp does not automatically prohibit single-visit treatment if disinfection can be achieved.

### 20. When is retreatment of endodontic treatment considered?

Retreatment is considered when a previously treated tooth has persistent or recurrent disease and the cause is accessible and correctable through the root canal system. Indications include persistent apical periodontitis, inadequate obturation, missed canals, coronal leakage, untreated anatomy, persistent intraradicular infection, and some procedural complications.

The first task is diagnosis. A periapical radiolucency may represent persistent disease, scar tissue, or another pathology, so the clinician must correlate clinical findings and imaging.

Nonsurgical retreatment is generally preferred when the problem is intraradicular and can be corrected through the canal. Surgery is considered when the cause cannot be predictably corrected nonsurgically or when direct root-end management is required.

Viva point: Retreatment is indicated when **post-treatment disease has a correctable endodontic cause**. Identify and eliminate the source rather than simply repeating the same procedure.

### 22. What are recent advances in caries diagnosis?

Recent caries-diagnostic approaches supplement conventional visual-tactile examination and radiography with fluorescence, transillumination, digital imaging, laser fluorescence, and other optical technologies. Their purpose is to detect early mineral changes before cavitation becomes clinically obvious and to improve monitoring over time.

The principle of contemporary caries management is increasingly conservative. Detection does not automatically mean surgical treatment. A lesion’s activity, depth, location, risk factors, and ability to be controlled by preventive measures must be considered.

Fluorescence systems such as DIAGNOdent provide quantitative adjunctive readings, while transillumination can reveal changes in tooth structure and proximal lesions. Digital radiography improves image handling and can facilitate comparison during follow-up.

Viva point: Advances include **laser fluorescence, transillumination, digital radiography and other optical/quantitative methods**. ### 23. What is micro-CT scanning and its applications in modern endodontics?

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is a high-resolution three-dimensional imaging technique that reconstructs the internal structure of a specimen from multiple X-ray projections. It can visualize root canals, dentinal thickness, curvatures, accessory anatomy, resorption, and the effects of instrumentation and obturation with very high spatial resolution.

Its major limitation is that it is primarily a research and ex-vivo technique rather than a routine clinical imaging method because of radiation dose, equipment size, and specimen requirements.

Micro-CT is valuable for comparing shaping systems, measuring canal transportation, assessing untouched canal surface area, evaluating obturation voids, and studying complex anatomy such as C-shaped canals.

Viva point: Micro-CT provides **high-resolution three-dimensional visualization of internal tooth anatomy** and is extremely valuable for research into canal morphology, instrumentation, irrigation and obturation. It is not a routine chairside replacement for CBCT.

### 24. What is the prognosis of a horizontal root fracture at the junction of middle and apical thirds?

A horizontal root fracture at the junction of the middle and apical thirds can have a relatively favorable prognosis, particularly when the fracture is well aligned, the coronal fragment remains stable, the pulp is maintained or can be managed appropriately, and the periodontal tissues are not severely damaged.

The location matters because fractures farther from the cervical region generally have better opportunities for healing and stabilization. Mobility and displacement of the coronal fragment, contamination through the fracture line, and pulpal necrosis influence prognosis.

Management is usually conservative, with repositioning and stabilization when indicated and periodic clinical/radiographic follow-up. Root canal treatment is not automatically required for every fractured tooth; it is directed at the necrotic or infected portion when pulpal necrosis develops.

Viva point: A middle–apical third horizontal fracture generally has a **better prognosis than a cervical fracture**, especially when fragments are well aligned and stable. Monitor pulp status and periodontal healing rather than automatically performing root canal treatment.

### 25. In an endo-perio lesion, periodontal or endodontic therapy first—and why?

When an endodontic infection contributes to a combined endodontic-periodontal lesion, endodontic therapy is generally performed first when the pulp is necrotic or the canal system is infected. The reason is biological: elimination of the intraradicular infection removes a major source of inflammatory products entering the periodontal tissues.

After adequate root canal treatment, the periodontal defect can be reassessed. Some periodontal probing defects and bone lesions may improve substantially if their origin was primarily endodontic. Only the residual periodontal component then requires periodontal therapy.

If the lesion is primarily periodontal, the treatment sequence may differ. Diagnosis therefore remains essential.

Cohen emphasizes that post-treatment disease is commonly associated with intraradicular infection and that nonsurgical retreatment is often less invasive than surgery when the cause is accessible through the canal.

Viva point: In a true combined lesion with a **necrotic/infected pulp, treat the endodontic component first**, then reassess the periodontal defect. This avoids performing irreversible periodontal treatment before removing the endodontic source.

UNIVERSITY PRACTICAL QUESTIONS
EXPANDED VIVA ANSWERS

Conservative Dentistry & Endodontics

Expanded from the authoritative project source documents

Source policy: Answers are based primarily on the uploaded project textbooks, especially Ingle’s Endodontics, Cohen’s Pathways of the Pulp, Current Therapy in Endodontics, Sturdevant’s, Summitt’s, Phillips’ and Craig’s dental-materials texts. Where the source material gives a range or where the question is ambiguous, that limitation is retained rather than replaced with an unsupported universal value.

## POST AND CORE

1. How much tooth structure is lost to consider a tooth for post and core? What are the other factors?

Use a post when remaining coronal tooth structure cannot adequately retain/support the core and definitive restoration. Consider remaining ferrule, circumferential dentin, root length/curvature, canal anatomy, periodontal support, occlusion and prosthetic requirements. A post does not strengthen the tooth; it primarily retains the core. The key principle is that the post is a retention device for the core, not a method of strengthening the root. Therefore, preservation of sound dentin and a ferrule is generally more important than simply increasing post dimensions.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Ingle; Current Therapy]

2. Why not fibre post instead of cast post?

Fiber posts are preferred when adequate coronal dentin and a suitable canal permit conservative placement. A custom cast post is useful when the canal is unusually narrow, irregular, flared or requires a custom fit, especially when further canal preparation would remove excessive dentin. A cast post becomes advantageous when the canal form itself is the problem—for example, a very flared or irregular canal in which a prefabricated fiber post would require excessive cement or additional preparation. The choice is therefore based on anatomy and remaining tooth structure rather than on a universal preference for one post.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Cohen]

3. Advantages and disadvantages of custom cast post and fibre post

Cast: + excellent custom fit, integral core, useful for irregular canals/minimal coronal structure; − more tooth removal, technique/laboratory sensitive, two appointments, higher root-fracture risk. Fiber: + conservative, esthetic, modulus closer to dentin, favorable stress distribution and easier removal; − requires adequate ferrule/core retention, weaker/flexible under high functional load and technique-sensitive bonding. The main biomechanical difference is rigidity: a cast metal post is much stiffer than dentin and can transmit stresses to the root, whereas a fiber post has a modulus closer to dentin and tends to distribute stresses more favorably. Fiber posts are also easier to remove if retreatment becomes necessary.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Ingle]

4. Define ferrule, advantages and disadvantages of ferrule

Ferrule = a circumferential band of sound tooth structure embraced by the crown margin, ideally about 1.5–2 mm in height. It improves resistance to functional/lever forces and reduces fracture risk. Disadvantage: obtaining it may require additional crown lengthening/extrusion or removal of sound tooth structure. The ferrule effect is protective because the crown encircles the remaining tooth structure and helps resist functional forces that would otherwise be concentrated at the post–core/root interface. A continuous, uniform ferrule is preferable to an isolated or very thin segment.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Ingle]

5. Where did the term ferrule originate from?

From the Latin ferrum, meaning iron; a ferrule is a metal ring/band used to reinforce a structure. In dentistry it describes the circumferential crown effect around remaining tooth structure. The word therefore reflects the original engineering concept of a reinforcing ring placed around a structure. In dentistry, the 'ring' is created by the definitive crown engaging sound tooth structure.

Project source(s): [Cohen]

6. Rationale behind leaving 4–5 mm of gutta-percha in the root canal space

To preserve an adequate apical seal and avoid disturbing the apical filling. Project sources support retaining about 3–5 mm; Ingle specifically recommends 5 mm when preparing post space. The retained apical segment acts as the apical barrier against leakage and prevents displacement of the apical filling. Removing too much filling material or disturbing the apical plug can compromise the seal.

Project source(s): [Current Therapy; Ingle]

7. Tensile strength, modulus of elasticity of dentin and cast metal post

Dentin: modulus ≈18 GPa; tensile strength is roughly 50–100 MPa. Cast noble-metal post/core alloys: stiffness/modulus ≈80–100 GPa and strength ≈1500 MPa. These values explain why a rigid cast post behaves very differently from dentin: its stiffness is several times that of dentin. This mismatch is one reason excessive post rigidity and unnecessary dentin removal can increase root stress.

Project source(s): [Cohen]

8. Advantages and disadvantages of base metal in custom cast post

Advantages: inexpensive, high strength/stiffness, high hardness and good castability with appropriate technique. Disadvantages: difficult manipulation because of high hardness, less forgiving laboratory handling, possible chemical degradation/corrosion products and higher rigidity than dentin. The high strength of base-metal alloys permits relatively small sections, but their high hardness and stiffness can make adjustment and finishing more difficult. Their greater rigidity also makes preservation of surrounding dentin particularly important.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Cohen]

9. Obturation technique ideal for post and core: lateral compaction or single cone?

The key requirement is a well-condensed, well-sealed root filling. The sources do not establish single-cone as inherently superior for post-space preparation; adequately condensed gutta-percha can be removed immediately while maintaining the apical seal. For post preparation, the quality and apical continuity of the root filling are more important than the label of the obturation technique. A dense, well-adapted apical plug should remain undisturbed after the coronal portion is removed.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Current Therapy]

10. Pericervical dentin significance

Pericervical dentin is the dentin around the cervical root/coronal root region that is critical for fracture resistance. Its preservation limits weakening and improves resistance to functional stresses; excessive removal should be avoided during post-space preparation. This region is mechanically important because stresses are transferred through the cervical root and remaining coronal dentin. Preserving it reduces the risk that a post preparation will create a thin, vulnerable root wall.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Cohen]

11. Significance of various luting cements during cementation of post and core

Zinc phosphate: thin film (<25 µm), good mechanical retention, no chemical bond. Conventional GIC: bonds to tooth/post and has higher strength, but is not ideal where hygroscopic expansion is a concern. Resin cement: highest bonding potential and ~200 MPa compressive strength, but requires careful dentin pretreatment and polymerization. Cement selection should be matched to retention, moisture control, restoration type and pulpal/endodontic status. A cement should not be expected to compensate for an inadequately designed post space or inadequate remaining tooth structure.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Phillips]

12. Dead space significance in post and core. Why is there a ~0.5-mm gap between gutta-percha and post?

A controlled cement space permits cement flow and seating and reduces hydraulic pressure during post insertion. It should be minimal and uniform; excessive space means a thicker cement layer, greater shrinkage/void risk and reduced retention. The cement space must be controlled rather than eliminated completely: a very tight post can generate hydraulic pressure during seating, while a large space creates a thick cement layer with greater potential for voids and loss of retention. A small, uniform space is therefore desirable.

Project source(s): [Cohen]

13. Why does the root fracture at the apical area in case of custom post?

A tapered cast post can act as a wedge; stresses may concentrate near the post termination/apical region depending on geometry and stiffness. In particular, rigid parallel posts tend to concentrate stress near the apex, whereas tapered posts shift more stress cervically. The post tip represents a geometric transition where load is transferred from the stiff post to the surrounding dentin. Sharp changes in diameter, taper or rigidity can further amplify local stress and predispose the root to fracture.

Project source(s): [Current Therapy; Cohen]

14. Why is stress concentration more in apical area than coronal area?

When a rigid post terminates inside a relatively flexible dentin root, load transfer becomes concentrated around the post tip. Geometry, post stiffness, taper and abrupt change in rigidity create stress concentration. The stress concentration is related to the termination of the rigid post within the more compliant dentin. The farther the load is transferred through a rigid post, the more important post geometry, taper and the thickness of surrounding dentin become.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Current Therapy]

15. Factors affecting decision and case selection of post length

Root length and curvature; remaining apical seal; residual dentin thickness; periodontal/bony support; crown-root ratio; canal anatomy; amount of coronal tooth structure/ferrule; post retention requirements; and occlusal/prosthetic forces. Post length should therefore be increased only within the biological and structural limits of the root. A longer post is not automatically safer if achieving that length requires sacrificing the apical seal or dangerously thinning the root.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Current Therapy]

16. Factors affecting strength of post

Material and modulus, diameter and length, cross-sectional shape, surface design, fit/adaptation, cementation, remaining dentin/ferrule, and occlusal loading. Increasing diameter at the expense of dentin is undesirable. Post strength cannot be considered independently of root strength. Increasing post diameter may increase post rigidity, but if it requires removal of dentin, the overall tooth may actually become weaker.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Cohen]

17. How much apical seal with gutta-percha?

Approximately 3–5 mm; 5 mm is the commonly recommended minimum when preparing post space. The retained plug should be long enough to resist displacement and provide a continuous apical seal. Ingle's discussion supports approximately 5 mm as a practical target for post-space preparation.

Project source(s): [Current Therapy; Ingle]

18. Ideal time to remove gutta-percha for post-space preparation

Immediately after adequate obturation is acceptable; Ingle concludes adequately condensed gutta-percha can be safely removed immediately. Delayed removal does not offer a clear sealing advantage. The important point is that adequately condensed gutta-percha does not necessarily need to 'mature' for days before post-space preparation. Ingle reports that immediate removal can be performed safely when the obturation is properly condensed.

Project source(s): [Ingle]

19. Types of cast post systems in angulated teeth (modifications)

For divergent/angulated canals, use a split/two-piece cast post-and-core design, with separate post segments that lock or key together coronally. This avoids excessive dentin removal to create one common path of insertion. A two-piece or split design allows different canal paths to be accommodated without forcing all posts into one common path. This is particularly useful when roots are divergent or canals are angulated.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Cohen]

20. Disadvantages of removing excessive dentin

Reduces fracture resistance, weakens the root, increases perforation risk, decreases remaining ferrule/dentin and may make the tooth unrestorable. Root resistance is strongly related to residual dentin thickness. The root behaves much like a hollow structure: loss of wall thickness markedly reduces its resistance to fracture. Perforation risk also rises as instruments are taken deeper or toward root concavities.

Project source(s): [Ingle; Current Therapy]

21. Difference between ferrule and finish line

Ferrule is the vertical band of sound tooth structure encircled by the crown. Finish line is the prepared terminal margin of the crown preparation. The finish line is a preparation boundary; the ferrule is remaining tooth structure above/around that margin. The ferrule is therefore a biological/structural feature of the remaining tooth, whereas the finish line is an intentional preparation feature. A well-designed finish line does not itself create a ferrule unless sound tooth structure remains coronal to it.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Ingle]

22. Where should sprue be attached in post and core?

Attach to the thickest/bulkiest portion of the wax pattern so metal flows from thick to thin areas and the delicate margins are not distorted. Avoid attachment to thin line angles or broad flat surfaces at 90°. Attaching the sprue to the bulky region allows the casting to solidify progressively from thin sections toward the reservoir. Attaching it to a delicate margin can interfere with flow and may distort the pattern.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

23. Types of sprue designs

Direct spruing: pattern directly connected to the crucible former. Indirect spruing: pattern connected through a runner/reservoir bar. Multiple sprues may be used for larger/complex patterns. The practical significance is to preserve sound tooth structure, maintain dimensional accuracy and select the procedure according to the clinical situation described in the source texts.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

24. Casting defect types

Common defects: distortion/warpage, incomplete casting, fins/flash, surface irregularities, inclusions, penetration, and porosity. Porosity includes shrinkage, gas/pinhole, subsurface and microporosity. Many casting defects are preventable and reflect problems in pattern handling, spruing, investment, burnout, alloy melting or casting technique. Porosity is particularly important because it can weaken an otherwise acceptable casting.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

25. Recommended sprue and sprue gauge/diameter/material

Use a wax sprue of adequate diameter—large enough to feed the pattern without premature solidification; commonly around 2.5–3 mm for small cast restorations, with exact diameter adjusted to pattern bulk. Wax sprues are preferred because they burn out predictably; plastic requires careful two-stage burnout. The sprue must be large enough to remain molten long enough to feed the casting. A diameter that is too small may freeze before the restoration has completely solidified, encouraging shrinkage defects.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

26. Hollow sprue prevents what type of porosity?

A hollow sprue/reservoir-type design helps reduce localized shrinkage porosity by maintaining a reservoir of molten alloy and promoting directional solidification. Its function is essentially that of a molten-metal reservoir: it maintains a supply of alloy as the restoration contracts during solidification. Thus it primarily helps prevent shrinkage-related porosity.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

27. Reservoir attachment—where to place it?

Place it adjacent to the thickest/bulkiest part of the pattern and in the heat center of the casting ring, so it remains molten longer and feeds the casting during solidification. Placing the reservoir at the thickest region is based on directional solidification—the bulky portion is the last region likely to solidify and therefore needs a source of molten metal. The reservoir should occupy the thermal center of the ring when possible.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

28. Reservoir size in post and core

The reservoir should be sufficiently bulky to remain molten after the pattern solidifies; it should be larger in volume than the section it is intended to feed. If the reservoir is too small, it may solidify before it can feed the casting. Its size therefore has to be related to the volume and thickness of the portion being supplied.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

29. Emergence profile—how to achieve it?

Shape the cervical/core wax pattern to reproduce the natural convexity and gradual transition from root/cervical tooth to crown. Avoid overcontouring; use the diagnostic cast/template and finish the pattern/crown preparation with a smooth, cleansable cervical contour. The aim is a contour that looks and functions like natural tooth structure, not a bulky cervical mass. A correct profile also facilitates plaque control and supports healthy gingival tissues.

Project source(s): [Summitt; Sturdevant]

30. Soap solution and its significance in post and core procedure

A dilute soap/detergent solution can be used as a wetting agent on the canal/pattern surface to reduce surface tension and improve adaptation/flow of impression or pattern material into the prepared post space. Lowering surface tension helps pattern material wet the prepared canal walls rather than trapping air against them. The benefit is therefore improved adaptation and reproduction of the post-space form.

Project source(s): [Operative/dental-materials principles in project sources]

31. Separating media used in wax pattern during post and core and direct inlay

Use a thin water-soluble separating medium/wetting separator compatible with the tooth and wax pattern; avoid a thick film because it can distort the pattern and affect fit. The separator should allow the pattern to be withdrawn without tearing or locking while adding as little thickness as possible. A thick separating film can itself become a source of dimensional error.

Project source(s): [Phillips; Operative Dentistry sources]

## INLAY

1. What will you use to remove wax pattern from the cavity?

Use a heated wax instrument/explorer or a suitable wax-removal instrument; remove along the path of withdrawal without rocking or distorting the pattern. The pattern should be removed along its established path of insertion. Levering or rocking the wax can fracture thin portions or permanently distort the pattern.

Project source(s): [Phillips; Operative Dentistry sources]

2. Which sprue material is best: wood, metal, hollow/solid, or resin?

Wax is the preferred sprue-former material for routine lost-wax casting because it burns out predictably and is easy to attach. Plastic/resin can be used but requires careful two-stage burnout; rigid metal sprues are less convenient for direct wax-pattern manipulation. Wax is particularly convenient for direct patterns because it can be adapted, cut and attached easily and is eliminated during burnout. Resin sprues are possible but require attention to burnout because incomplete elimination can contaminate the mold.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

3. Between gold and Japanese gold, what advantage will I get as compared to base metal?

Gold-based casting alloys offer better corrosion resistance, easier finishing/manipulation and more favorable clinical handling than base metals. If 'Japanese gold' refers to a gold-rich alloy/foil, its principal advantage is high purity/biocompatibility and corrosion resistance rather than greater strength. Gold-rich alloys generally provide superior corrosion resistance and easier finishing/burnishing, while base metals provide greater stiffness and often higher strength at lower cost. The choice therefore involves balancing handling, biological behavior, mechanical properties and economics.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

4. Types of inlay wax used

Type I: direct inlay wax, intended for patterns made intraorally. Type II: indirect inlay wax, intended primarily for patterns made on a die. The distinction is based primarily on where the wax pattern is made. Direct wax is formulated for manipulation intraorally, whereas indirect wax is intended for laboratory fabrication on a die.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

5. Characteristics of inlay wax

Accurate reproduction, low residue after burnout, controlled flow, low distortion, adequate hardness at room/oral temperature, and minimal thermal expansion/shrinkage. Type I should have low flow at 37°C (≤1%) but high flow at 45°C (70–90%). Because wax is the pattern rather than the final restoration, even small dimensional changes are reproduced in the casting. Low residual ash and low distortion are therefore especially important.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

6. Types of investments

Gypsum-bonded, phosphate-bonded and ethyl-silicate-bonded investments. Gypsum-bonded are mainly for lower-fusing alloys; phosphate/ethyl-silicate systems tolerate higher temperatures and high-fusing alloys. Investment selection must match the melting range of the alloy and the required mold expansion. Using an investment outside its intended temperature range can produce breakdown, surface defects or inaccurate dimensions.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

7. Steps in casting procedure

Wax pattern → spruing → investing → burnout/elimination of wax → mold preheating → alloy melting → casting → cooling → divesting → pickling/cleaning → finishing and polishing → fit evaluation. Each stage influences the next: a distorted wax pattern cannot be corrected reliably by casting, and an incorrectly invested or burned-out pattern can produce defects even when the alloy itself is sound. Accurate casting is therefore a process-control sequence.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

8. Types of porosity

Localized shrinkage, subsurface shrinkage, microporosity, gas/pinhole porosity, gas inclusions, back-pressure porosity and suck-back porosity. Porosity can be internal and invisible clinically until finishing or sectioning reveals it. Shrinkage porosity is especially associated with inadequate feeding during solidification, whereas gas-related porosity is associated with trapped gases or casting conditions.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

9. Define inlay and cavity designs/modifications

Inlay: an indirect intracoronal restoration fabricated outside the mouth and cemented into a prepared tooth. Preparation should provide adequate resistance/retention, a definite path of insertion, rounded internal form, sufficient bulk and margins appropriate to the alloy. Because the inlay is cemented rather than directly packed into the preparation, its preparation must provide a path of insertion and sufficient bulk of material. Internal line angles are generally rounded to reduce stress concentration.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry; Phillips]

10. Burs used in cavity preparation of inlay

Round burs for initial caries removal; fissure/tapered fissure burs for walls and outline; tapered fissure/diamond burs for controlled divergence and occlusal modifications; finishing diamonds for bevels/flare. Exact bur selection depends on alloy and preparation design. The bur should be selected to produce the required wall form without unnecessary tooth removal. Finishing instruments are used after the primary outline is established so that the final margin is smooth and clearly defined.

Project source(s): [Sturdevant; Summitt]

11. Difference between amalgam and inlay Class II cavity preparation

Amalgam requires mechanical retention/resistance with relatively parallel/appropriately convergent walls and no bevel at the occlusal margin. Inlay preparation has a definite path of insertion, greater wall divergence, adequate bulk and usually bevelled metal margins to improve marginal adaptation. The practical significance is to preserve sound tooth structure, maintain dimensional accuracy and select the procedure according to the clinical situation described in the source texts.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry sources]

12. Bevel—definition, types, where used/not used

A bevel is an intentional sloping modification of a cavosurface margin. Types include short/long bevels and hollow-ground bevels. Used for cast-metal restorations/inlays to create a metal margin and improve adaptation; generally avoided where a bevel would weaken enamel or is unnecessary for bonded tooth-colored restorations. A bevel changes the geometry of the marginal enamel and creates a sloping metal interface. Its use is material-dependent; it is valuable for cast metal but is not automatically indicated for adhesive tooth-colored restorations.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry; Phillips]

13. Transportation of wax pattern to lab

Sprue the pattern and protect it from distortion; place in a suitable container/support and transport promptly. Avoid heat, dehydration, vibration and contact with the pattern. Invest as soon as practical. Wax is easily distorted by heat and mechanical handling, so the safest approach is to protect the pattern and minimize the time between pattern completion and investing. The sprue also provides a convenient means of handling the pattern without touching delicate margins.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

14. Difference between bevel and flare

Bevel is a localized sloping of the cavosurface margin. Flare is a broader, gradual outward widening of a preparation wall/margin, often used to improve access, finish-line form or marginal geometry. In simple terms, a bevel modifies the edge, while a flare modifies the wall or proximal outline over a broader area. They may be used together but they are not synonymous.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry sources]

15. Isthmus—definition and dimensions

Isthmus is the narrow connecting portion of a preparation between two broader areas, e.g., the occlusal portion joining proximal boxes. It should be conservative but wide enough for adequate restorative bulk and strength; excessive width weakens the tooth. A narrow isthmus preserves tooth structure, but it still has to provide enough restorative thickness for strength and enough space for manipulation. Excessive isthmus width removes unnecessary tooth structure and weakens the remaining cusps.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry sources]

16. Path of insertion and significance

An imaginary line along which an indirect restoration can be inserted and removed. It determines wall taper, retention and whether undercuts interfere with seating; it must be established before final preparation. All walls that interfere with this path must be modified or an undercut must be eliminated. Establishing the path early prevents a preparation that appears acceptable from above but cannot actually seat the restoration.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

17. Problems with more taper and less taper

Excessive taper → reduced retention and resistance. Insufficient taper/near-parallel walls → undercuts, difficult seating/removal and risk of locking the pattern/restoration. Retention is inversely related to taper: as the angle between opposing walls increases, the restoration has less resistance to dislodgement. Conversely, nearly parallel walls may create undercuts that prevent complete seating.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

18. Angle of sprue pattern

Approximately 45° to the proximal wall is preferred where feasible; a right-angle attachment to a broad flat surface can create turbulence and severe porosity. A smooth, angled attachment encourages a less turbulent flow of molten alloy. The exact angle is less important than avoiding abrupt changes in direction and avoiding attachment to vulnerable areas of the pattern.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

19. Solid sprue uses—advantages and disadvantages during investing

Solid sprues provide a simple, direct flow channel and are easy to attach. Disadvantages: greater wax/metal mass and less efficient reservoir effect; improper size/position can cause turbulence or shrinkage porosity. A solid sprue is simple and predictable when appropriately designed, but it does not automatically provide the same feeding behavior as a properly designed reservoir. Its size and position therefore remain critical.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

20. Secondary flare—definition, indications and contraindications

Secondary flare is an additional outward enlargement of the proximal preparation to improve access, finishing and visibility while maintaining conservative tooth structure. Indicated when proximal access/finishing is inadequate; contraindicated when it unnecessarily removes sound tooth structure or compromises marginal/enamel strength. Secondary flare is mainly a finishing/access modification rather than a means of creating the primary cavity outline. It should improve visibility and marginal finishing without unnecessarily sacrificing sound enamel or dentin.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

21. Types of finish lines

Common finish lines: chamfer, shoulder, shoulder with bevel, knife-edge/feather edge and radial shoulder. Selection depends on restorative material and required marginal bulk. The finish line must provide enough space for the restorative material while remaining accessible for finishing and cementation. The ideal form depends on whether the restoration is metal, ceramic or another material.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry; Phillips]

22. Jacket crown—definition

A full-coverage crown with no metal collar/substructure, traditionally made entirely of porcelain/ceramic or other tooth-colored material. The absence of a metal coping makes the preparation and material thickness particularly important for strength. Traditional jacket crowns therefore required substantial, uniform reduction to provide adequate ceramic bulk.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

23. Convenience form in inlay and examples

Convenience form is modification of the preparation to improve access, visibility and manipulation. Examples: temporary extension of proximal walls, occlusal extension to improve access, removal of obstructing enamel and proximal flare. Convenience form does not exist primarily to improve retention; it makes the operative procedure possible or easier. It should be kept to the minimum needed for visibility, instrumentation and pattern manipulation.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

24. Low- and medium-fusion waxes in inlay—indications/contraindications

Lower-fusing/softer waxes are easier to adapt and manipulate but are more temperature-sensitive and distort more readily. Medium/harder waxes are preferred where greater dimensional stability is required. Avoid a soft wax when prolonged handling/transport is expected. Wax flow must be controlled because excessive flow during manipulation can change the pattern dimensions. A harder/medium-fusion wax is useful when the pattern must resist handling and transportation.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

25. Purity and forms of gold

Gold is expressed in fineness (parts per 1000) or karat (parts per 24). Dental gold may be supplied as pure gold/foil, casting alloys and solder. Pure gold is very ductile/soft, so casting alloys are alloyed to obtain clinical strength and hardness. Karat expresses gold content out of 24 parts, while fineness expresses it per 1000 parts. Pure gold is highly ductile but too soft for many cast restorations, so other elements are added to obtain the required clinical properties.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

26. Gold casting investment

Gypsum-bonded investment is suitable for many lower-fusing gold alloys; phosphate-bonded investment is used for higher-fusing alloys and provides greater high-temperature stability. Gypsum-bonded investment is limited by the temperature at which gypsum breaks down, whereas phosphate-bonded systems tolerate higher temperatures. Therefore the investment must be selected from the alloy's melting range.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

27. Properties of gold required for a restoration

Corrosion resistance, biocompatibility, adequate strength/hardness, ductility/burnishability, accurate castability, dimensional stability and acceptable wear compatibility. The desirable combination is not simply high strength: an indirect restoration also needs to cast accurately, resist corrosion, be biologically acceptable and permit finishing/burnishing. Gold alloys are valued because they combine several of these properties well.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

28. Reverse curve and significance in operative dentistry

A reverse curve is a deliberate change in the direction of a preparation outline, commonly used to preserve tooth structure, maintain marginal ridge/cusp strength and avoid unnecessary extension while obtaining access. The reverse curve is a conservative design feature. By following the natural anatomy rather than making a straight extension, it can preserve cusp and marginal-ridge strength while still providing access.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

29. When do we not give bevels in an inlay cavity preparation?

Do not bevel where the margin is on a location that cannot be finished/controlled, where beveling would leave unsupported/weak enamel, or where a bevel would reduce required restorative bulk or create an unfavorable margin. A bevel should never be added simply because the restoration is an inlay. It is omitted when it would create weak enamel, reduce material bulk or produce a margin that cannot be properly finished and controlled.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

30. Mesial vs distal cavity preparation difficulties in inlay

Mesial preparations may be more difficult because of access/visibility and the adjacent anterior tooth anatomy depending on the tooth; distal preparations can be more difficult because of posterior access and limited visibility. The main issue is achieving a common path of insertion and clean, visible proximal margins. The practical difficulty is usually access and visibility rather than an intrinsic difference in the restorative material. The operator must maintain the correct path of insertion while achieving a clean, visible proximal margin without damaging the adjacent tooth.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

## MISCELLANEOUS

1. Antirotation and rotation of central incisor and significance in post/core

Maxillary central incisors receive substantial labiolingual and rotational forces. Antirotational features such as an oval/anti-rotation form, canal shape or small proximal/lingual groove improve resistance to rotational displacement; ferrule remains the major protective feature. Rotational displacement is especially relevant to anterior teeth because the remaining coronal structure may provide limited resistance to twisting forces. Antirotational geometry should therefore complement—not replace—the ferrule effect.

Project source(s): [Cohen]

2. Intradermal injection for hypersensitivity and location

For local management of dentinal hypersensitivity, an intradermal/local anesthetic injection is not routine treatment; if anesthesia is required diagnostically/therapeutically, it is placed in the mucobuccal/vestibular tissue adjacent to the involved tooth. Definitive management is desensitization/occlusion of dentinal tubules rather than repeated injection. Anesthetic injection is not a definitive treatment for dentinal hypersensitivity; it only blocks sensation temporarily. The underlying management is to identify the cause and occlude or reduce dentinal fluid movement through exposed tubules.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

3. Difference between GIC cement in luting vs restoration

Luting GIC is formulated for thin film thickness, flow and cementation; restorative GIC is formulated for greater bulk, strength and handling as a restorative material. Their powder/liquid formulations and intended properties differ. Luting cement must flow into a very thin space and allow complete seating, whereas a restorative GIC must tolerate a much larger bulk and clinical loading. Consequently, the two formulations are optimized differently.

Project source(s): [Phillips; Cohen]

4. Can luting and restorative cement be used interchangeably by manipulating powder-liquid ratio?

No. Altering the powder/liquid ratio changes setting time, strength, viscosity, solubility and film thickness but does not convert one formulation into another. Use the material for its intended indication. Powder–liquid manipulation changes handling characteristics but cannot change the chemistry and particle characteristics of the manufactured cement. Using a restorative formulation as a luting cement may therefore produce an unacceptable film thickness.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Phillips]

5. Minimum acceptable particle size for luting cements

Particles must be sufficiently small to permit a clinically thin film; the project sources emphasize a zinc-phosphate film thickness <25 µm rather than a single universal particle-size cutoff. The practical requirement is not a single universal particle diameter but a particle distribution that permits a clinically acceptable film. For zinc phosphate, the project source emphasizes a film thickness below 25 µm.

Project source(s): [Cohen]

6. Advantages and disadvantages of sectional obturation

Advantages: permits controlled obturation of individual canal sections and may be useful in difficult anatomy/repair situations. Disadvantages: additional interfaces, technique sensitivity and possible voids; each section must be densely adapted and sealed. Sectional techniques can be useful when the canal anatomy or treatment situation makes a continuous obturation difficult. However, every interface is a potential source of leakage, so adaptation and sealing of each section are critical.

Project source(s): [Endodontic project sources]

7. How to remove gutta-percha from root canal space?

Use thermal instruments, rotary instruments such as Gates-Glidden/Peeso, or a combination. Chemical solvents are less favored for post-space preparation because removal is less controlled and may increase leakage risk. Retain about 5 mm apically. Thermal softening can make removal easier, while mechanical instruments provide controlled removal of the bulk material. Ingle specifically notes that chemical solvents are less desirable for routine post-space preparation because their effect is difficult to control.

Project source(s): [Ingle]

8. Affected vs infected dentin; how to diagnose?

Infected dentin: bacteria present, collagen irreversibly denatured, non-remineralizable and should be removed. Affected dentin: demineralized but largely uninfected/reversibly altered, potentially remineralizable and can be preserved. Diagnosis is mainly tactile hardness, supported by caries-disclosing dye; color alone is unreliable. The distinction is clinically important because removing all affected dentin can sacrifice tissue that still has reparative potential. Hardness and consistency are more useful than color alone when deciding how much dentin to remove.

Project source(s): [Sturdevant; Summitt]

9. Caries-detecting dyes

Common caries-disclosing dyes include acid red 52 (acid rhodamine B/food red 106) in propylene glycol; other commercial caries detectors use red dye systems. They preferentially stain irreversibly denatured collagen in infected dentin. These dyes are adjuncts, not substitutes for diagnosis. They are useful for highlighting altered dentin during excavation, but stained dentin should not automatically be equated with active infection without considering hardness and clinical context.

Project source(s): [Sturdevant]

10. Methods to detect caries chairside when dyes are unavailable

Visual inspection, good illumination, air drying, tactile assessment with an explorer/hand excavator, assessment of dentin hardness, radiographs when indicated, and adjunctive fluorescence devices. No single method is completely reliable. Chairside caries diagnosis is therefore a combination of visual, tactile and radiographic information. Air drying is particularly useful because demineralized enamel becomes more apparent after removal of surface moisture.

Project source(s): [Sturdevant]

11. Depth of resin tags

Resin tags typically extend several micrometres into etched dentin; their exact length varies with dentin condition and adhesive. Tag formation is not by itself a reliable measure of bond strength. Resin tags contribute to micromechanical retention, but bond durability depends on the overall hybrid layer and quality of adhesive infiltration. Therefore deeper tags do not necessarily mean a stronger or more durable bond.

Project source(s): [Summitt; Phillips]

12. For good retention, etch or not etch with resin-based luting cement?

Follow the specific adhesive system. Etch-and-rinse systems require phosphoric-acid etching; self-etch systems do not. In root dentin, aggressive etching can complicate moisture control, so modern systems often use controlled/self-etch approaches. The correct answer depends on the adhesive system rather than on resin cement alone. Etch-and-rinse systems require conditioning of the substrate, whereas self-etch/self-adhesive approaches are designed to work without a separate phosphoric-acid etching step.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Phillips]

13. Enlargement size and diameter of Peeso reamers

Peeso/Peaso reamers are standardized instruments, commonly available in sizes #1–#6, increasing in tip diameter by ISO increments; the exact diameter should be selected conservatively and only enough to create the required post space. Peeso enlargement should be conservative because each increase in diameter removes circumferential dentin. The largest instrument should therefore be determined by the anatomy and required post size, not by a fixed number alone.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Ingle]

14. Ante's law

The combined periodontal ligament/root surface area of abutment teeth should be at least equal to that of the teeth being replaced by a fixed prosthesis. It is a traditional guideline for abutment selection, not an absolute rule. The principle attempts to relate the periodontal support available from abutment teeth to the load they must carry. It is a traditional guideline and should be interpreted together with periodontal health, root form, occlusion and prosthetic design.

Project source(s): [Operative/prosthodontic principles in project sources]

15. Treatment options/alternatives for occlusal wear facet

First identify and control the cause (attrition/parafunction, occlusal interference, erosion/abrasion). Options include monitoring, occlusal adjustment only when indicated, direct composite/additive restoration, indirect onlay/overlay/crown for severe loss, and protection with a night guard when bruxism is involved. Restoring a wear facet without controlling its cause may simply result in recurrence. Treatment should therefore range from observation and preventive measures to additive or indirect restoration according to the amount of tissue loss and functional risk.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

16. Significance of oblique ridge in operative/restorative dentistry

The oblique ridge is a major structural ridge of maxillary molars formed by the union of triangular ridges of the mesiolingual and distobuccal cusps. Preserve it whenever possible because it contributes to cusp support and tooth strength; preparations crossing it should be conservative. The ridge acts as a structural bridge between cusps and helps resist deformation of the maxillary molar. Unnecessary cutting across it can weaken the tooth and increase the need for restorative coverage.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

17. Burs used in primary and secondary flare

Use tapered fissure/diamond instruments with the required taper for primary wall form; fine tapered diamonds are used for secondary flare/finishing. The aim is controlled divergence and smooth margins rather than unnecessary enlargement. The primary flare establishes the basic wall and access form, while the secondary flare refines the proximal margin. Fine instruments are preferred for the finishing stage to minimize unnecessary enlargement.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

18. Burs used to break contact during cavity preparation

Use a thin tapered fissure/diamond or fine needle/long tapered bur directed carefully through the contact, maintaining control to avoid adjacent-tooth damage. Breaking contact should be controlled and directed away from the adjacent tooth. A thin instrument allows the operator to pass through the contact while preserving as much proximal tooth structure as possible.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

19. Compressive strength of various amalgam and base metal

High-copper amalgams generally have compressive strength in the range of roughly 300–500 MPa depending on formulation/age. Base-metal dental alloys are substantially stronger, commonly several hundred MPa to >1000 MPa depending on alloy. Compressive strength is only one property used to judge a restorative material. Amalgam strength develops with setting and depends on alloy formulation, whereas base-metal alloys have much higher strength and stiffness but also different handling and corrosion considerations.

Project source(s): [Phillips; project dental-materials sources]

20. Type of luting agent if tooth is sensitive after preparation

Use a sedative, nonirritating luting cement when indicated—commonly zinc oxide–eugenol for suitable temporary/provisional cementation. For definitive cementation, choose the cement based on restoration, retention and pulpal status; avoid unnecessary irritation. Sensitivity after preparation does not automatically mean that a permanent cement should be selected on the basis of 'sedation.' The first step is to determine the cause of sensitivity; a temporary sedative cement may be appropriate when provisionalization is indicated.

Project source(s): [Dental materials sources]

21. Post-luting: ideal time to remove excess flash

Remove gross excess immediately after seating while the cement is in the gel/firm stage, then complete marginal cleanup after initial set as appropriate for the cement. Avoid disturbing the restoration during final set. The ideal timing is a compromise: excess should be removed before it becomes difficult to cut away, but manipulation should not disturb the seated restoration. The exact gel stage depends on the cement's setting reaction.

Project source(s): [Dental materials sources]

22. Procedures to compensate for shrinkage—chairside

Use correct wax manipulation, minimize wax temperature changes, avoid residual stress, invest promptly, and use the appropriate investment expansion. For resin patterns, allow adequate polymerization and avoid distortion. Casting accuracy begins with the pattern, because dimensional errors in wax or resin are transferred to the final restoration. Prompt investing and correct investment expansion are therefore more reliable than trying to correct large shrinkage errors after casting.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

23. Investment modifications to compensate for casting shrinkage

Control total investment expansion by adjusting setting expansion, hygroscopic expansion and thermal expansion; modify the special-liquid/water ratio and heating protocol according to the alloy. Investment expansion is deliberately used to offset the thermal contraction of the alloy. The total expansion must be controlled so that the final mold dimensions correspond to the intended restoration dimensions.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

24. Vitality test—response duration and diagnosis

Normal pulp: brief response that subsides within seconds. Hyperemia/reversible inflammation: pain generally ≤10–15 seconds after cold/heat. Lingering/intense pain suggests irreversible pulpitis. No response may indicate necrosis, but results must be interpreted with history and comparison teeth. A sensibility test evaluates neural response rather than directly measuring blood flow or histologic inflammation. A lingering response is clinically more significant than the absolute intensity of a brief response, and results should always be compared with control teeth.

Project source(s): [Sturdevant; Cohen]

25. Difference between reversible pulpitis and hyperemia

Hyperemia is increased pulpal blood flow/vascular response and is often used clinically for a mild, reversible pulpal reaction. Reversible pulpitis is a clinical inflammatory diagnosis in which an appropriate stimulus produces brief, non-lingering pain that resolves after removal of the irritant. The terms overlap historically, but reversible pulpitis is a clinical diagnosis, whereas hyperemia describes a vascular response. In practice, a brief stimulus-related pain that resolves promptly supports a reversible condition rather than symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.

Project source(s): [Sturdevant; Cohen]

26. Greening effect in metal-ceramic restorations

Greenish-yellow porcelain discoloration associated particularly with silver-containing PFM alloys, caused by silver-related diffusion/vapor transport into porcelain. It is promoted by higher Ag content and susceptible porcelain/firing conditions; modern porcelains have greatly reduced the problem. Silver can reach the porcelain by diffusion or vapor transport and produce a green to yellow-green discoloration. The effect is influenced by alloy composition and firing conditions, and furnace contamination can also contribute.

Project source(s): [Phillips]

27. Hollow-ground bevel is given for which restoration type?

It is classically used for cast-metal restorations, particularly cast gold/inlay margins, where controlled reduction of the enamel margin permits a burnishable/strong metal edge. The hollow-ground form creates a controlled concave/sloping margin that provides space for the cast metal edge while reducing the risk of a thin unsupported metal margin. It is therefore associated particularly with cast-metal preparations.

Project source(s): [Operative Dentistry]

28. Drying of wet dentin

Do not desiccate dentin when using dentin adhesives. After rinsing, remove excess water and leave dentin visibly moist according to the adhesive system; over-drying collapses exposed collagen and reduces resin infiltration. For etch-and-rinse dentin bonding, the collagen network must remain expanded enough for resin infiltration. Over-drying collapses the collagen, whereas leaving the surface excessively wet can dilute components and compromise bonding; the manufacturer's adhesive protocol therefore matters.

Project source(s): [Summitt; Phillips]

29. Methods of placing calcium hydroxide in root canal space

Use a lentulo spiral, hand file/reamer with a small amount of paste, or a syringe/cannula when appropriate. The paste should be carried to working length without extrusion beyond the apex. The objective is to coat the canal walls with a uniform medication rather than simply placing a large bolus in the coronal canal. Avoiding extrusion is particularly important because calcium hydroxide is strongly alkaline and can irritate periapical tissues.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Ingle]

30. Rankow and Krasner's laws

Law of centrality: chamber floor is centered at CEJ. Law of concentricity: chamber walls are concentric with external tooth surfaces at CEJ. Law of CEJ: distance from external surface to chamber wall is uniform around CEJ. Laws of symmetry: canal orifices show characteristic symmetry, with maxillary molars as an exception. Law of color change: chamber floor is darker than walls. The practical significance is to preserve sound tooth structure, maintain dimensional accuracy and select the procedure according to the clinical situation described in the source texts.

Project source(s): [Ingle]

31. Mousehole appearance

A small, round/ovoid radiolucent 'mousehole' defect is used descriptively for a localized perforation/defect or anatomical radiographic appearance; interpretation must be correlated with angulated radiographs/CBCT and clinical findings. The term is descriptive rather than a diagnosis by itself. Because similar radiographic appearances can arise from different anatomic or iatrogenic conditions, confirmation should be based on additional views and clinical findings.

Project source(s): [Endodontic imaging sources]

32. Morning glory appearance and significance

‘Morning-glory’ is a descriptive radiographic term used for a characteristic funnel-shaped/elongated canal or root morphology in selected developmental/anatomic anomalies. It signals unusual anatomy and the need for careful three-dimensional imaging and conservative instrumentation. The appearance is a warning that the root/canal system may not follow routine anatomy. Such cases benefit from careful radiographic assessment, magnification and conservative instrumentation to avoid transportation or perforation.

Project source(s): [Endodontic anatomy/imaging sources]

33. Law of CEJ

The distance from the external tooth surface to the pulp-chamber wall is equal around the circumference at the CEJ. The CEJ is therefore the most reliable landmark for locating the pulp chamber. This law is particularly useful when the clinical crown is heavily restored or rotated. Locating the CEJ provides a more dependable guide to the chamber than relying only on the external crown anatomy.

Project source(s): [Ingle]

34. Tests to confirm hue, value and chroma classification of apical periodontitis

The wording appears inconsistent: hue, value and chroma are color dimensions, not apical-periodontitis categories. For color, use standardized shade tabs/visual shade assessment under color-corrected light, with spectrophotometer/colorimeter when needed. For apical periodontitis, diagnosis relies on history, clinical pulp/periapical tests and radiographic/CBCT findings. This question appears to combine two unrelated classification systems. Hue, value and chroma belong to color science, whereas apical periodontitis is diagnosed from symptoms, pulp sensibility, percussion/palpation and periapical imaging.

Project source(s): [Summitt; Cohen]

35. PAI index

Periapical Index (PAI) is a 5-point radiographic scale for periapical status. Scores 1–2 are commonly grouped as healthy; 3–5 as diseased. It helps standardize radiographic assessment of apical periodontitis/healing. The scale improves consistency between observers and across follow-up examinations. A change in PAI score can therefore be used to describe radiographic healing or progression more systematically than an unstructured description.

Project source(s): [Cohen]

36. Necrobiosis

Necrobiosis refers to gradual degenerative cellular/tissue changes progressing toward necrosis rather than abrupt cell death. In pulp pathology it denotes progressive degenerative changes that may precede pulpal necrosis. The concept describes a progressive process rather than an instantaneous event. In pulpal disease, degenerative change may advance gradually before complete necrosis is established.

Project source(s): [Endodontic pathology sources]

37. Compressive strength of Biodentine and MTA

MTA has relatively low early compressive strength (about 40 MPa at 24 h) that increases to about 67 MPa by 21 days in the cited Cohen data. Biodentine is generally reported as having higher early and later compressive strength than conventional MTA, but values vary with test conditions/formulation. The reported values are time-dependent because these hydraulic cements continue to mature after placement. Direct comparison between products is meaningful only when the same specimen preparation, curing conditions and testing method are used.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Current Therapy]

38. Irrigants used in retreatments

Commonly use sodium hypochlorite for tissue dissolution/disinfection and EDTA for smear-layer/inorganic component removal. Additional agents such as chlorhexidine may be used for antimicrobial purposes but should not be mixed directly with sodium hypochlorite. Solvents are used selectively for gutta-percha removal. Retreatment often requires both disinfection and removal of remnants of previous filling materials. Sodium hypochlorite and EDTA have complementary functions, while solvents are used selectively for gutta-percha rather than as substitutes for irrigation.

Project source(s): [Cohen; Current Therapy]

39. Role of various vehicles in calcium hydroxide—indications/contraindications

Water/saline: simple, readily dissociating vehicle; useful for intracanal medication when rapid ion release is desired. Anesthetic solution: convenient carrier but not preferred for relying on anesthetic effect in the canal. Glycerin/polyethylene glycol: viscous vehicles improve handling and prolong paste retention but slow ion diffusion. Chlorhexidine: provides additional antimicrobial action, but compatibility and precipitation issues must be considered. Vehicle choice changes the rate at which calcium hydroxide dissociates and therefore influences ion availability and paste handling. Aqueous vehicles generally permit faster ionic release, whereas viscous vehicles prolong the medication's presence but slow diffusion.

Project source(s): [Cohen; calcium-hydroxide project sources]

40. Role of iodoform in calcium hydroxide paste

Iodoform is added mainly to improve radiopacity and provide additional antimicrobial/antiseptic action. It is especially used in pediatric/primary-tooth endodontic pastes; it is not responsible for the principal high-pH action, which comes from calcium hydroxide. The therapeutic high pH of the paste comes primarily from calcium hydroxide. Iodoform is mainly an adjunct that improves radiographic visibility and contributes antimicrobial activity; it should not be confused with the principal active component.

Project source(s): [Endodontic project sources]

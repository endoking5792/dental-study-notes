## 1. CORE STUDY FRAMEWORK: ADHESION AND DENTINE BONDING

This master framework is placed before the individual answers because the questions overlap substantially. Each examination answer is independently structured, but the same conceptual scaffold should be used during revision.

### Definition and objectives

Adhesion is the attachment between unlike surfaces produced by interfacial interactions. In restorative dentistry, the practical objectives are conservation of tooth structure, retention, marginal sealing and functional load transfer. Phillips’ Science of Dental Materials describes resin bonding as involving creation of microporosity followed by infiltration and polymerization of resin monomers, with possible chemical interaction when functional groups are present. Source: Phillips’ Science of Dental Materials, 12th ed., Chapter 12, “Bonding and Bonding Agents.”

### Why dentine is difficult

Dentine is hydrated, collagen-containing and traversed by fluid-filled tubules. It is covered by a smear layer after instrumentation, and its substrate varies with depth, sclerosis and caries status. Sturdevant describes dentine as approximately 50% mineral, 25% water and 25% organic material by volume in its discussion of adhesion. Source: Sturdevant’s Art & Science of Operative Dentistry, 5th ed., Chapter 5, “Fundamental Concepts of Enamel and Dentin Adhesion,” pp. 247-249.

### Smear layer

The smear layer consists of cutting debris and smear plugs that occlude dentinal tubules. It reduces permeability but can interfere with strong bonding. Etch-and-rinse systems remove it; self-etch systems generally modify, dissolve and incorporate it into the adhesive interface. Source: Sturdevant, 5th ed., Chapter 5; Ingle’s Endodontics, 7th ed., relevant sections on dentine etching and pulp response.

### Hybrid layer

Nakabayashi et al. (1982) demonstrated resin infiltration into acid-demineralized dentine and described the resulting interdiffusion structure as the hybrid layer. Source trail: Nakabayashi et al. (1982), as referenced in Sturdevant, 5th ed., Chapter 5, pp. 252-254; Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, 4th ed., “Adhesion to Enamel and Dentin.”

### Contemporary classification

| Strategy | Clinical sequence | Main advantage | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Etch-and-rinse | Etchant → rinse → primer → adhesive | Excellent enamel etching; established dentine bonding | Technique-sensitive moisture control; risk of over-etching |
| Self-etch | Acidic primer/adhesive simultaneously conditions and infiltrates | Fewer steps and less risk of overdrying | Enamel etching may be less effective |
| Universal/multimode | Self-etch, selective enamel etch or etch-and-rinse | Versatility and simplified inventory | Not equally optimal in every substrate/mode |

### Historical generations

| Generation | Typical concept | Core development |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Early resin systems | Weak dentine interaction |
| 2nd | Phosphate/chlorophosphate-type systems | Attempted chemical interaction with mineral; poor durability |
| 3rd | Smear-layer modification / partial conditioning | Acidic primers; improved but inconsistent performance |
| 4th | 3-step etch-and-rinse | Etch + primer + adhesive; hybridization |
| 5th | 2-step etch-and-rinse | Primer + adhesive combined |
| 6th | 2-step self-etch | Self-etch primer + adhesive |
| 7th | 1-step self-etch | All-in-one |
| Universal | Multimode | Self-etch / selective enamel etch / etch-and-rinse |

Generation terminology is a historical and somewhat artificial classification. Phillips and Seltzer/Bender emphasize that contemporary systems are better understood by mechanism and clinical steps. Source: Phillips, 12th ed., Chapter 12; Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp, chapter on dentine bonding.

### Major causes of interface degradation

Hydrolysis and water sorption of resin components.

Nanoleakage and water movement through incompletely infiltrated hybrid layers.

Degradation of exposed collagen by endogenous dentinal enzymes including MMPs and cysteine cathepsins.

Polymerization stress and cyclic fatigue.

Substrate variability, especially deep, sclerotic and caries-affected dentine.

Operator variables including isolation, moisture control, solvent evaporation, adhesive thickness and curing.

Chemical incompatibility between acidic adhesives and some self-cure or dual-cure resin systems.

### Recent direction

The major contemporary shift is from simply increasing immediate bond strength toward engineering a more durable interface. The 2025 Dental Materials review by Breschi et al. identifies improved infiltration and chemical interaction, residual-water removal, collagen reinforcement and inhibition of endogenous enzymes as important directions. citeturn2search3

Mild universal adhesives can show stable dentine performance, while selective enamel etching can improve enamel bonding. The evidence therefore supports substrate-specific use rather than a universal protocol applied indiscriminately. citeturn2search0

## 2. Discuss ‘Dentine Bonding’ and recent development.

Mark allocation: 25 marks

### STUDY MODE

Dentine bonding is the attachment of resin-based restorative or luting materials to dentine by micromechanical interlocking, chemical interaction, or both. The development of dentine bonding represents the solution of a fundamental materials problem: obtaining durable adhesion to a hydrated biological substrate while controlling the smear layer and preserving pulpal health.

#### Historical development

Early generations produced weak and unstable bonds because hydrophobic resins interacted poorly with hydrated dentine.

Fusayama et al. (1979) introduced the influential total-etch concept, in which enamel and dentine could be etched.

Nakabayashi et al. (1982) demonstrated resin infiltration of acid-demineralized dentine and the hybrid layer.

Fourth generation: three-step etch-and-rinse systems.

Fifth generation: two-step etch-and-rinse systems.

Sixth generation: two-step self-etch systems.

Seventh generation: one-step self-etch systems.

Universal adhesives: multimode systems capable of self-etch, selective enamel etch or etch-and-rinse application.

#### Dentine substrate

Dentine contains mineral, collagen and water and contains fluid-filled tubules. Deep dentine has greater tubule density and diameter and therefore less intertubular dentine available for resin infiltration. Source: Sturdevant, 5th ed., Chapter 5, pp. 247-250.

#### Mechanism

Conditioning modifies or removes the smear layer.

Demineralization exposes microporosity and, with etch-and-rinse systems, a collagen-rich zone.

Primer wets the substrate and transports monomers into dentine.

Adhesive resin polymerizes to create the hybrid layer.

Resin may penetrate dentinal tubules to form resin tags.

Functional monomers can additionally interact chemically with residual hydroxyapatite.

#### Current adhesive systems

| System | Protocol | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 4th generation | Etch → rinse → primer → adhesive | Established multistep approach |
| 5th generation | Etch → rinse → combined primer/adhesive | Simplified etch-and-rinse |
| 6th generation | Self-etch primer → adhesive | No separate phosphoric-acid dentine etch |
| 7th generation | One-step self-etch | Maximum simplification |
| Universal | Self-etch / selective enamel etch / etch-and-rinse | Multimode; often functional-monomer based |

#### Hybrid-layer degradation

The principal long-term challenge is degradation of the resin-dentine interface. Water can plasticize and hydrolyze resin components, while incompletely infiltrated collagen remains vulnerable to endogenous proteases. MMP inhibition, collagen crosslinking and remineralization have therefore become major research themes. Source: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; Phillips, 12th ed., Chapter 12.

#### Recent development

Universal/multimode adhesives containing functional monomers, especially 10-MDP.

HEMA-free and more hydrophobic two-step universal adhesive concepts.

Active adhesive application and prolonged application time.

Improved solvent evaporation and extended curing.

Additional hydrophobic resin coatings.

MMP-inhibitory and collagen-crosslinking strategies.

Crosslinker-modified etchants.

Antibacterial and quaternary-ammonium-containing adhesive approaches.

Experimental biomimetic remineralization.

Advanced analysis of water and monomer gradients in the hybrid layer.

Long-term clinical trials comparing application strategies.

Breschi et al. (2025) emphasize that dentine adhesion has improved but its fundamental durability problem remains unresolved. citeturn2search3

A 2026 Dental Materials randomized clinical trial with 96-month follow-up found no retention loss with the tested universal adhesive but reported differences in discoloration and survival between self-etch and etch-and-rinse strategies. This illustrates why long-term clinical outcomes should complement immediate bond-strength testing. citeturn2search1

#### Critical interpretation

The evolution of dentine bonding is therefore not simply a march from first to seventh generation. It is a progression from weak surface interaction, through controlled hybridization, toward multifunctional chemistry and interface stabilization. Simplification has improved usability, but it has not eliminated the biological and hydrolytic challenges of dentine.

## 3. Discuss recent advances in adhesive cement.

Mark allocation: 25 marks

### STUDY MODE

Adhesive cements are luting materials designed to provide retention and adhesion between tooth structure and indirect restorations. Their development parallels dentine bonding and restorative-surface treatment.

#### Evolution

Zinc phosphate and conventional acid-base cements relied primarily on preparation geometry and mechanical interlocking.

Polycarboxylate and glass-ionomer cements introduced stronger chemical interaction with tooth mineral.

Resin cements introduced high strength, low solubility and adhesive bonding potential.

Etch-and-rinse resin cementation became a high-bond-strength multistep approach.

Self-etch systems reduced separate phosphoric-acid conditioning.

Self-adhesive resin cements incorporated acidic functional monomers into the cement.

Universal resin cement/adhesive combinations expanded multimode and multisubstrate bonding.

#### Classification

| Cement | Tooth pretreatment | Major advantage | Major limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| GIC | Clean, slightly moist | Ionic interaction + fluoride release | Maturation and solubility |
| RMGI | Usually minimal | Simple handling | Not ideal for every weak/thin ceramic |
| Conventional resin cement | Etch/rinse + adhesive | High bond strength | Technique sensitive |
| Self-etch resin cement | Self-etch system | Simpler | Enamel bond may be inferior |
| Self-adhesive resin cement | Minimal | Very simple protocol | Substrate-dependent; weaker enamel interaction |
| Universal resin cement | Product-specific multimode | Broad compatibility | Chemistry and compatibility are product dependent |

#### Recent advances

Functional acidic monomers, especially phosphate chemistry and 10-MDP.

Universal adhesive-compatible resin cements.

Improved dual-cure systems for limited-light situations.

Improved optical properties and color stability.

Material-specific surface treatment for glass ceramics, zirconia and CAD/CAM resin-based materials.

Lower solubility and greater resistance to oral fluids.

Greater attention to water sorption, hydrolysis and interface degradation.

#### Critical appraisal

The principal weakness of the self-adhesive concept is that fewer steps do not necessarily mean stronger substrate conditioning. Craig’s Restorative Dental Materials reports that self-adhesive cements generally bond less strongly to enamel than systems involving phosphoric-acid etching and that their performance is substrate dependent. Source: Craig’s Restorative Dental Materials, 14th ed., resin-cement sections.

Summitt emphasizes that no cement can compensate indefinitely for inadequate preparation resistance and retention form. Adhesive cement is most valuable when adhesive retention is actually required. Source: Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, 4th ed., indirect restoration and luting sections.

#### Recent evidence

A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis of self-adhesive resin cements with CAD/CAM blocks found substrate-dependent adhesive strength, supporting material-specific surface treatment. citeturn0search14

A 2024 Dental Materials study found curing mode affected water sorption, solubility, color stability and flexural strength of resin composite cements, with light activation generally beneficial when adequate light reached the material. citeturn1search15

A 2024 randomized-trial meta-analysis compared self-adhesive resin cement with conventional cements using restoration failure as an outcome, reflecting the need to move beyond laboratory bond strength alone. citeturn1search5

## 7. Acid etching.

Mark allocation: 10 marks

### STUDY MODE

Acid etching is the controlled application of acid to tooth or restorative surfaces to alter surface morphology and chemistry and improve subsequent adhesion.

#### Enamel

Buonocore (1955) introduced the acid-etch concept. Phosphoric acid creates microporosities and increases surface energy. Resin penetrates these spaces and polymerizes to form resin microtags. Sturdevant describes Type I, II and III etching patterns.

#### Dentine

Phosphoric acid removes the smear layer and demineralizes superficial dentine, exposing collagen and increasing permeability temporarily. Adequate primer/adhesive infiltration is essential.

| Technique | Principle |
|---|---|
| Etch-and-rinse | Phosphoric acid → rinse → controlled moisture → primer → adhesive |
| Selective enamel etch | Phosphoric acid restricted to enamel; universal adhesive used on dentine |
| Self-etch | Acidic monomers simultaneously condition and infiltrate |

Current evidence supports selective enamel etching as a useful strategy for improving enamel bonding with mild universal adhesives. citeturn2search0

## 9. Newer generation of Dentin Bonding Agents.

Mark allocation: 10 marks

### STUDY MODE

The phrase “newer generation” is historically useful but scientifically imperfect because generation numbers are marketing-based. Modern classification is better based on etch strategy and number of steps.

#### Sixth generation

Two-step self-etch systems use an acidic primer followed by adhesive. Mild systems partially demineralize dentine and preserve residual hydroxyapatite, allowing possible chemical interaction with functional monomers.

#### Seventh generation

One-step self-etch systems combine conditioning, priming and bonding. They minimize clinical steps but may be relatively hydrophilic and water permeable.

#### Universal adhesives

Universal or multimode adhesives may be used in self-etch, selective enamel-etch or etch-and-rinse modes. Many contain 10-MDP and can bond to tooth structure and selected indirect restorative substrates.

|---|---|---|

| Functional chemistry | Variable | Often 10-MDP |

## 10. Describe in detail about the dentine bonding agents.

#### Fourth generation in detail

Etch enamel and dentine

Rinse

Maintain dentine in appropriate moist condition

Apply hydrophilic primer

Evaporate solvent

Apply hydrophobic adhesive resin

Light cure

Place restorative resin

The three-step etch-and-rinse approach remains an important reference system because it separates conditioning, priming and bonding. Source: Phillips, 12th ed., Chapter 12; Summitt, 4th ed., “Adhesion to Enamel and Dentin.”

#### Self-etch systems

Self-etch adhesives contain acidic monomers that simultaneously condition and infiltrate dentine. They modify rather than completely remove the smear layer. Mild self-etch systems preserve residual hydroxyapatite and can permit chemical interaction with functional monomers. Source: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; Summitt, 4th ed.

#### 10-MDP

10-MDP is a phosphate functional monomer widely used in contemporary universal adhesives. It can interact with calcium-containing tooth mineral and can contribute to bonding with zirconia through phosphate chemistry. The actual performance depends on formulation and surface treatment. Source: Summitt, 4th ed.; Craig, 14th ed.

#### Hybrid-layer degradation

Long-term failure can involve hydrolysis, nanoleakage and degradation of incompletely protected collagen by MMPs and cysteine cathepsins. Source: Phillips, 12th ed., Chapter 12; Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp.

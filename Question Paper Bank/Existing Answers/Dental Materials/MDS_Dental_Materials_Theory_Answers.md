## QUESTION 1

Marks: 10   Electrochemical corrosion of dental materials.

STUDY MODE

Electrochemical corrosion is wet corrosion in which an electrolyte permits anodic oxidation and cathodic reduction at coupled sites. In dentistry, saliva provides a conductive aqueous medium, while differences in electrode potential between metallic restorations or within an alloy can drive corrosion. Phillips et al. emphasize that the oral environment is particularly favorable for electrochemical action because it is warm, moist and subject to changes in temperature and pH. [1]

### Basic electrochemical mechanism

Anode: metal atoms lose electrons and enter solution as ions: M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻. The anodic region is the site of actual metal dissolution. [1]

Cathode: electrons consumed in a reduction reaction, commonly oxygen reduction or hydrogen-ion reduction depending on the local environment. [1]

Electrolyte: saliva, gingival crevicular fluid or another aqueous medium transports ions.

Electronic pathway: electrons move through the metallic conductor from anode to cathode.

The potential difference between anodic and cathodic sites establishes the driving force for the corrosion current.

Figure 1. Simplified electrochemical corrosion cell. The anode undergoes oxidation, while the cathode supports a reduction reaction.

### Important forms relevant to dental materials

| Type | Mechanism | Dental example / significance |
|---|---|---|
| Galvanic corrosion | Two dissimilar metals/alloys are electrically connected in an electrolyte; the less noble material tends to become anodic. | Amalgam adjacent to a gold restoration can form a galvanic couple; transient galvanic pain may occur. |
| Concentration-cell corrosion | Different concentrations of oxygen or ions create local potential differences. | Crevices, plaque deposits and marginal gaps may become anodic. |
| Pitting corrosion | Highly localized anodic dissolution produces pits. | Important for alloys with susceptible passive films. |
| Crevice corrosion | Restricted fluid exchange creates a chemically different microenvironment. | Marginal gaps, scratches and crevices favor localized attack. |
| Stress-corrosion / corrosion fatigue | Mechanical stress interacts with a corrosive environment. | Can accelerate degradation and fracture of susceptible metallic components. |

### Factors affecting corrosion

Alloy composition and phase distribution.

Electrode potential and nobility.

pH, chloride concentration, dissolved oxygen and sulfide-containing compounds.

Temperature fluctuations and biofilm/deposit accumulation.

Surface roughness, pits, scratches and crevices.

Contact between dissimilar metals.

Residual stress, cold work and microstructural heterogeneity.

### Corrosion resistance

Noble metals resist oxidation because of their positive electrode potentials, while chromium- and titanium-containing alloys can develop protective passive oxide films. [1] Limited corrosion of amalgam can paradoxically contribute to marginal sealing because corrosion products may partially occlude marginal gaps, but this does not make corrosion generally desirable. [1]

### Testing and control

Electrochemical methods include open-circuit potential, polarization resistance and potentiodynamic polarization.

Immersion or static-release tests evaluate ion release under controlled media.

Surface examination and mass-loss methods provide complementary information.

ISO 10271:2020 specifies corrosion test methods for metallic dental materials, including immersion, electrochemical and tarnish-related procedures. [7]

Clinically, use corrosion-resistant alloys, maintain smooth surfaces, avoid unnecessary dissimilar-metal couples, control plaque accumulation and preserve passivation.

### Recent advance

Grgur et al. evaluated noble dental alloys using ISO 10271-based electrochemical testing and demonstrated that corrosion behavior cannot be predicted reliably from noble-metal percentage alone; alloy chemistry and microstructure also influence electrochemical behavior. [8] This is important because 'noble' composition is a useful classification, not a complete surrogate for corrosion performance.

EXAM MODE

Definition: Electrochemical corrosion is the deterioration of a metal through coupled oxidation and reduction reactions in the presence of an electrolyte. In the oral cavity, saliva acts as the electrolyte and metallic restorations can function as electrochemical cells. [1]

Mechanism: 1) At the anode, M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ and the metal dissolves. 2) Electrons travel through the metal to the cathode. 3) A reduction reaction occurs at the cathode. 4) Saliva transports ions and completes the electrochemical circuit. [1]

Galvanic corrosion: dissimilar metals coupled in saliva.

Concentration-cell corrosion: differential oxygen/ion concentration, especially in crevices.

Pitting corrosion: localized breakdown and metal dissolution.

Crevice corrosion: attack in marginal gaps and restricted spaces.

Stress-corrosion/corrosion fatigue: interaction of corrosion with mechanical stress.

Factors: alloy composition, electrode potential, pH, chloride and sulfide ions, oxygen concentration, temperature, surface roughness, plaque deposits, residual stress and contact between dissimilar metals. [1]

Testing: visual assessment, immersion/mass-loss testing and electrochemical polarization techniques are used. ISO 10271:2020 is the current international standard for corrosion test methods for metallic dental materials. [7]

Prevention: select noble or passivating alloys, maintain polished surfaces, avoid unnecessary dissimilar-metal couples, minimize crevices, maintain oral hygiene and protect passive oxide films. [1]

Recent evidence: electrochemical testing of noble alloys confirms that corrosion resistance depends on composition and microstructure rather than noble-metal percentage alone. [8]

Conclusion: Electrochemical corrosion is clinically relevant because it can cause metal-ion release, surface degradation, galvanic symptoms and loss of mechanical integrity; systematic material selection and surface control are therefore essential.

Saliva = electrolyte.

Noble metals resist corrosion; Cr/Ti can passivate.

ISO 10271:2020 is the key current corrosion-testing standard.

## QUESTION 2

Marks: 10   Tarnish and Corrosion. (Describe)

STUDY MODE

Tarnish is primarily a surface phenomenon, whereas corrosion represents chemical or electrochemical deterioration of the metal. Phillips et al. define tarnish as surface discoloration or alteration of luster and describe oxides, sulfides and chlorides as common surface films. Tarnish may precede corrosion, although tarnish and corrosion are not synonymous. [1]

| Feature | Tarnish | Corrosion |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Surface discoloration or loss of luster. | Deterioration of metal by reaction with its environment. |
| Extent | Usually superficial. | May be superficial or involve substantial metal loss. |
| Main consequence | Esthetic change and possible early warning. | Ion release, surface loss, weakening and failure. |
| Products | Deposits or thin oxide/sulfide/chloride films. | Soluble ions and solid corrosion products. |
| Reversibility | Some deposits can be removed mechanically. | True corrosion requires prevention/control and may be irreversible. |
| Relationship | May be an early indicator. | May occur with or without obvious tarnish. |

### Causes of tarnish

Sulfur-containing food compounds and oral metabolites.

Oxidation and formation of oxide films.

Chloride-containing oral fluids.

Deposits of plaque, mucin and food debris.

Surface roughness and microstructural heterogeneity.

### Types of corrosion in dentistry

Dry/chemical corrosion.

Wet/electrochemical or galvanic corrosion.

Galvanic corrosion between dissimilar metals.

Concentration-cell or crevice corrosion.

Pitting corrosion.

Stress-corrosion and corrosion fatigue.

Microbiologically influenced corrosion.

Bhat et al. note that electrochemical corrosion is complex and multiple mechanisms may act simultaneously. Smooth, well-polished surfaces reduce sites for deposits and localized cells. [2]

### Clinical significance

Esthetic deterioration of metallic restorations.

Metal-ion release and possible local or systemic biological effects.

Galvanic pain when electrically dissimilar metals are coupled.

Surface roughening and plaque retention.

Loss of material and possible mechanical failure.

In amalgam, limited corrosion products may partly seal marginal gaps, although excessive corrosion remains undesirable. [1]

### Measurement and prevention

Visual scoring and color/tarnish assessment.

Electrochemical methods for corrosion behavior.

Immersion/coupon testing with measurement of mass or ion release.

Use corrosion-resistant alloys or passive alloys.

Polish and maintain smooth surfaces.

Avoid unnecessary dissimilar-metal contact.

Control plaque and oral deposits.

Current standards: ISO 10271:2020 provides standardized corrosion-test methods for metallic dental materials. [7] Contemporary electrochemical work also shows that the corrosion behavior of noble dental alloys varies with alloy composition and microstructure. [8]

EXAM MODE

Tarnish: a surface discoloration or alteration of luster caused by deposits or formation of thin films such as oxides, sulfides or chlorides. [1]

Corrosion: deterioration of a metal by chemical or electrochemical reaction with its environment. [1]

| Tarnish | Corrosion |
|---|---|
| Mainly superficial | May involve actual metal loss |
| Primarily esthetic | Esthetic + biological + mechanical consequences |
| May be due to deposits/films | May involve oxidation, dissolution and electrochemical cells |
| Can precede corrosion | Represents material degradation |

Types of corrosion: dry corrosion, electrochemical/galvanic corrosion, concentration-cell or crevice corrosion, pitting corrosion, stress-corrosion/corrosion fatigue and microbiologically influenced corrosion. [1,2]

Prevention: select corrosion-resistant alloys; maintain smooth, polished surfaces; avoid dissimilar-metal couples; prevent plaque and food deposits; preserve passivation of base-metal alloys. ISO 10271:2020 is the current international test standard for metallic dental-material corrosion. [7]

Conclusion: Tarnish is principally a surface change, while corrosion is deterioration of the metal itself; the two may be related but should never be used interchangeably.

Tarnish may be oxide/sulfide/chloride film.

Corrosion can cause ion release and mechanical failure.

Polishing and plaque control are important preventive measures.

## QUESTION 3

Marks: 25   Write the methods of testing the biocompatibility of dental materials. Discuss the effects of dental cements on the pulp tissue.

STUDY MODE

Biocompatibility is not a single material property. It is the biological response of a host to a material under a particular composition, site, duration and mode of exposure. Phillips et al. emphasize that no single test can establish complete biocompatibility; in vitro, animal and usage/clinical evaluations provide complementary information. [1]

### Factors determining the biological response

Chemical composition, leachable substances, degradation products and corrosion products.

Dose and concentration of released substances.

Site and type of tissue contact.

Duration of contact.

Surface characteristics and roughness.

Mechanical, thermal and chemical environment.

Patient factors and pre-existing tissue condition.

Depth of cavity and thickness/permeability of remaining dentin in pulp-related applications.

### Classical sequence of biocompatibility testing

Figure 2. Conceptual sequence of primary screening, secondary in vivo testing and usage/clinical evaluation. Modern standards use risk-based selection rather than assuming that every test is required for every material.

| Stage | Typical methods | Purpose | Major limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary / in vitro | Cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, cell viability, extract testing, hemolysis where relevant | Rapid screening and mechanistic assessment | Simplified biological environment |
| Secondary / in vivo | Implantation, irritation, sensitization, systemic toxicity and other tissue-response tests where indicated | Assess integrated biological responses | Animal-human extrapolation and ethical/cost issues |
| Usage / clinical | Material placed under intended or closely simulated clinical conditions; tissue response and performance assessed | Highest clinical relevance | Cost, duration, confounding factors and ethical constraints |

### In vitro tests

Direct-contact cytotoxicity: material or specimen contacts cultured cells.

Extract testing: cells are exposed to eluates from the material, allowing assessment of leachable components.

Cell viability/metabolic assays: MTT, WST-1 and related assays quantify metabolic activity.

Membrane integrity assays: LDH release and related methods detect cell injury.

Genotoxicity: DNA damage, chromosomal abnormalities or mutagenic effects may be evaluated.

Material characterization and ion/monomer release complement biological tests.

A recent systematic review of resin-based composites found that contemporary in vitro studies continue to demonstrate dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity, with oxidative stress and apoptosis among proposed mechanisms. The findings are important but remain predominantly laboratory evidence and should not be translated directly into clinical toxicity. [11]

### In vivo and usage testing

Implantation tests assess local tissue reaction around a material.

Mucosal/irritation and sensitization tests assess soft-tissue responses.

Systemic toxicity studies evaluate distant biological effects when justified.

Usage tests place the material in an anatomically and clinically relevant setting and may permit histologic assessment.

Clinical studies integrate symptoms, restoration performance, tissue response and longer-term outcomes.

### Current standards

ISO 10993-1:2025 is now the current general standard for biological evaluation of medical devices and explicitly integrates biological safety assessment into a risk-management framework. ISO 7405:2025 is the current dentistry-specific standard and is intended to be used with the ISO 10993 series. [9,10] Thus, the modern approach is not a rigid checklist but a risk-based selection of appropriate biological endpoints.

### Effects of dental cements on pulp tissue

The pulpodentin complex responds to restorations through the combined effects of remaining dentin thickness/permeability, bacterial leakage, operative trauma, thermal challenge and chemical components released from the restorative material. Seltzer and Bender emphasize that the pulp is biologically active and capable of repair, but its response depends strongly on the severity and persistence of the insult. [6]

| Cement/material | Potential pulpal effect | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate cement | Initially acidic; irritation depends on concentration, dentin thickness and sealing. | Use appropriate consistency and adequate dentin protection in deep cavities. |
| Zinc polycarboxylate cement | Generally less irritating than zinc phosphate because the polyacrylic acid is less diffusible. | Relatively favorable pulpal behavior. |
| Zinc oxide-eugenol | Eugenol has sedative/palliative effects and is relatively favorable to the pulp; however, it is not suitable beneath all resin systems because eugenol may interfere with polymerization. | Useful in selected temporary/protective applications. |
| Conventional GIC | Early acidity and ionic release can irritate very deep preparations, but chemical adhesion and fluoride release are advantageous. | Deep dentin may require protective management. |
| RMGI | Contains resin monomers such as HEMA; uncured or undercured material may release components with greater biological activity. | Adequate curing and careful use in deep cavities are important. |
| Resin cement/composite-related materials | Residual monomers and incomplete polymerization may contribute to cytotoxicity; microleakage can permit bacterial insult. | Bonding quality and curing are biologically important. |
| Calcium hydroxide | High pH, antibacterial action and stimulation of hard-tissue repair; mechanically weak and soluble. | Historically important for selected deep lesions and pulp capping. |
| Hydraulic calcium-silicate cements | Generally favorable biocompatibility and bioactivity; promote mineralized barrier formation and odontogenic responses. | Important contemporary materials for vital pulp therapy. |

Phillips et al. report that the relative chemical irritation of some conventional cements increases from polycarboxylate to zinc phosphate to GIC under the conditions discussed in the textbook. [1] This should not be treated as a universal ranking of clinical pulpal injury because the biological response is modified by dentin thickness, leakage, material formulation and exposure conditions.

Calcium-silicate cements represent an important contemporary shift. Emara et al. found broadly favorable biocompatibility and odontogenic/angiogenic responses among commercially available calcium-silicate cements, while also emphasizing heterogeneity and the inability to identify one universally superior cement. [12] Modern vital-pulp protocols therefore emphasize material sealing ability, biological compatibility and preservation of pulp vitality rather than reliance on a single 'best' liner.

### Factors controlling pulpal injury

Remaining dentin thickness and permeability.

Degree of pulpal inflammation before restoration.

Material toxicity and quantity of leachable components.

Polymerization/setting reaction and heat generated.

Marginal seal and bacterial microleakage.

Moisture contamination and manipulation.

Duration of exposure.

EXAM MODE

Introduction: Dental-material biocompatibility is context dependent. No single laboratory test is sufficient; evaluation proceeds from screening to increasingly biologically and clinically relevant testing. [1]

A. METHODS OF TESTING BIOCOMPATIBILITY

Primary in vitro tests: cytotoxicity by direct contact or extracts; cell viability/metabolic assays; membrane-integrity assays; genotoxicity and related mechanistic tests.

Secondary in vivo tests: implantation, irritation, sensitization, systemic toxicity and other tissue-response studies when indicated.

Usage/clinical tests: material is evaluated under intended or closely simulated clinical conditions, with clinical and, where possible, histologic assessment. [1]

Modern standards: ISO 10993-1:2025 provides the general risk-based framework for biological evaluation, while ISO 7405:2025 provides dentistry-specific biological test methods and is used with ISO 10993. [9,10]

B. EFFECTS OF DENTAL CEMENTS ON PULP

Zinc phosphate: low initial pH may irritate deep pulp; adequate dentin thickness and protective measures reduce risk.

Zinc polycarboxylate: generally less pulpal irritation than zinc phosphate.

Zinc oxide-eugenol: relatively favorable and sedative to the pulp, but eugenol can interfere with polymerization of resin materials.

GIC: early acidity can be irritating in very deep preparations; adhesion and fluoride release are advantages.

RMGI: HEMA and other resin components can increase biological concerns when incompletely cured.

Resin-based materials: residual monomers and inadequate polymerization can contribute to cytotoxicity; microleakage is a major indirect pathway of pulpal injury.

Calcium hydroxide: high pH, antibacterial activity and hard-tissue formation, but weak and soluble.

Calcium-silicate cements: generally favorable biocompatibility and bioactivity and important in contemporary vital pulp therapy. [1,6,12]

Key concept: pulpal injury is not determined by cement chemistry alone. It results from the interaction of material, remaining dentin, bacterial leakage, operative trauma, thermal challenge and pre-existing pulpal status. [6]

Conclusion: Biocompatibility testing is a staged, risk-based process, and contemporary evaluation increasingly integrates chemical characterization with biological testing. For cements, preservation of a healthy pulp depends on both material selection and a well-sealed, minimally traumatic restoration.

### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

ISO 10993-1:2025 + ISO 7405:2025 are current standards.

Remaining dentin thickness is a major determinant of pulpal response.

Microleakage can be more clinically important than direct material toxicity.

Hydraulic calcium-silicate cements are a major contemporary development.

## QUESTION 4

Marks: 10   Describe in vitro evaluation of hardness of dental restoratives.

STUDY MODE

Hardness is resistance to localized plastic deformation or indentation. For dental restoratives it is useful for assessing resistance to surface deformation, wear-related behavior and, for resin materials, indirectly reflecting aspects of polymerization and degree of conversion. Phillips et al. describe several hardness methods, with Knoop and Vickers being especially suitable for small regions and brittle materials. [1]

Figure 3. Principle of indentation hardness testing. A controlled load creates a small indentation whose geometry is measured after unloading.

### Methods

| Method | Indenter / measurement | Typical application |
|---|---|---|
| Brinell | Hardened steel/carbide ball; indentation diameter. | Metals and relatively large homogeneous specimens. |
| Rockwell | Ball or diamond indenter; penetration depth. | Industrial/macroscopic hardness testing. |
| Vickers | Square-based diamond pyramid; measure two diagonals. | Metals, ceramics and dental materials; suitable for brittle materials. |
| Knoop | Rhombohedral diamond pyramid; measure long diagonal. | Microhardness, thin specimens, small regions and brittle materials. |
| Shore / Barcol | Indentation depth or rebound-type principles depending on instrument. | Selected polymers and softer materials. |

### In vitro protocol for restorative resin/composite

Prepare standardized specimens in a mold of defined dimensions.

Standardize material batch, shade, filler composition, manipulation and curing protocol.

Finish and polish the test surface under controlled conditions.

Store specimens for a defined period, commonly with controlled temperature and medium.

Apply a calibrated Knoop or Vickers load for a standardized dwell time.

Measure the indentation dimensions using the microscope or instrument software.

Calculate hardness from applied load and indentation geometry.

Make multiple indentations at standardized distances to avoid interaction between indents.

Report load, dwell time, surface preparation, storage conditions and number of specimens.

Phillips et al. note that Knoop and Vickers are microhardness methods using small loads and shallow indentations, allowing localized measurement. [1] The choice of method must therefore match specimen thickness, heterogeneity and expected hardness.

### Contemporary methodological issue: indentation size effect

Roubickova et al. demonstrated in 2025 that hardness values of dental resin-based composites can vary with indentation load and dwell time. Knoop hardness was more susceptible to indentation size effect than Vickers hardness, and Vickers measurements were more stable at lower loads in their experimental setting. [13] Therefore, comparing hardness values from different studies without matching load, dwell time and protocol can be misleading.

### Clinical relevance

Higher surface hardness generally indicates greater resistance to indentation and may correlate with improved wear resistance, but hardness alone does not predict clinical longevity.

Hardness gradients can reveal inadequate light curing.

Surface hardness can change with aging, water sorption and degradation.

Depth-dependent hardness is useful for assessing cure effectiveness in resin-based restoratives.

EXAM MODE

Definition: Hardness is resistance to localized plastic deformation or indentation. [1]

Common tests: Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers, Knoop, Shore and Barcol. For dental restorative materials, Vickers and Knoop microhardness are particularly useful because their indentations are small and permit localized testing. [1]

Prepare standardized restorative specimens.

Cure according to a controlled protocol.

Finish and polish the test surface.

Condition specimens for standardized time and environment.

Apply a known Knoop or Vickers load for a fixed dwell time.

Measure indentation dimensions.

Calculate hardness and report mean ± variation from multiple indentations.

Knoop: rhombohedral diamond indenter; long diagonal measured. Vickers: square-based diamond pyramid; two diagonals measured. [1]

Recent advance: Roubickova et al. showed that indentation size effect and dwell time can materially alter measured hardness of resin composites; Vickers was less sensitive to load variation than Knoop in that study. [13]

Conclusion: A hardness value is meaningful only when the indenter, load, dwell time, specimen preparation and storage conditions are reported and standardized.

## QUESTION 5

Marks: 10   Osseointegration.

STUDY MODE

Osseointegration is the functional establishment of direct bone-to-implant contact without an intervening fibrous tissue layer, permitting a stable implant to transmit functional load to surrounding bone. In implant biomaterials, the interface is governed by implant material, surface chemistry/topography, surgical trauma, primary stability, host biology and loading conditions. [1,2]

Figure 4. Simplified biological sequence from implant placement to functional osseointegration.

### Biological sequence

Primary mechanical stability is established at insertion through intimate contact between implant and bone.

Blood wets the implant surface and forms a protein-rich provisional matrix.

Acute inflammatory and immune events initiate healing.

Mesenchymal cells migrate to the interface and osteogenic differentiation occurs.

New woven bone forms and subsequently remodels into more organized lamellar bone.

Secondary biological stability develops as bone matures and adapts to functional loading.

### Factors influencing osseointegration

| Factor | Desired characteristic | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Biocompatible, corrosion resistant, mechanically appropriate | Supports stable interface. |
| Surface topography | Controlled micro/nanotopography | Influences protein adsorption and cell attachment. |
| Surface chemistry | Favorable surface energy and oxide chemistry | Modulates biological interactions. |
| Hydrophilicity | Often favorable for early biological interactions | May improve initial wettability and cellular responses. |
| Primary stability | Adequate mechanical fixation | Reduces excessive micromotion. |
| Bone quality | Adequate viable bone | Determines healing capacity and stability. |
| Loading | Appropriate timing and magnitude | Excessive micromotion can compromise healing. |

### Surface modification

Machined and turned surfaces.

Sandblasted and acid-etched micro-rough surfaces.

Anodized or oxide-modified surfaces.

Hydrophilic modifications.

Calcium-phosphate and other bioactive coatings.

Nanotopography and nanotube architectures.

Antibacterial and biomolecule-based surface systems.

Recent evidence supports the biological importance of surface modification but requires a distinction between enhanced early osseointegration and proven long-term clinical superiority. Al Shayeb et al. reported improved bone-to-implant contact with bioactive surface modifications in a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis, but heterogeneity remained moderate and specific agents cannot be treated as universally superior. [14]

Liu et al. reviewed implant surface modification strategies and emphasized that many experimental modifications show promising laboratory outcomes, while fewer have translated into established clinical practice. [15]

EXAM MODE

Definition: Osseointegration is a direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the implant surface under functional loading, without an intervening fibrous tissue interface. [1,2]

Sequence: primary stability → blood/protein adsorption → inflammatory phase → osteogenic cell recruitment → woven bone formation → remodeling → secondary stability.

Factors: implant material, surface roughness/topography, surface chemistry, wettability, primary stability, bone quality, surgical trauma, micromotion, infection and loading.

Surface modifications include sandblasting/acid etching, anodization, hydrophilic treatments, calcium-phosphate coatings, nanostructuring and biomolecule-based coatings. [15]

Recent evidence: a 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis found improved bone-to-implant contact with bioactive surface modifications, although heterogeneity and limited long-term clinical evidence mean that laboratory superiority should not automatically be interpreted as universal clinical superiority. [14]

Conclusion: Osseointegration is a dynamic bone-healing process controlled by the implant surface-host interaction and mechanical environment, rather than a simple property of titanium alone.

### HIGH-YIELD EXAM POINTS

Primary stability is mechanical; secondary stability is biological.

Surface micro/nanotopography influences early healing.

Do not equate better BIC in laboratory studies with proven long-term clinical superiority.

## QUESTION 6

Marks: 10   Describe bonding to metals.

STUDY MODE

Bonding to metals in restorative dentistry is challenging because metallic surfaces are chemically different from tooth tissues and may contain passive oxide layers, contaminants and heterogeneous phases. Durable bonding usually requires a combination of surface conditioning, chemical coupling and an appropriate resin system. Craig describes airborne-particle abrasion as a common method because it produces a microretentive, high-energy surface. [3]

Figure 5. Contemporary sequence for bonding resin to a metallic substrate.

### Mechanisms

Micromechanical retention: surface roughening increases available area and creates undercuts.

Chemical interaction: functional monomers interact with metal or metal-oxide surfaces.

Silica-mediated bonding: tribochemical silica coating permits silane coupling to resin systems.

Oxide-mediated bonding: stable oxide layers can provide sites for phosphate-containing monomers.

Combination bonding: modern systems commonly rely on mechanical and chemical mechanisms together.

### Surface treatments

| Treatment | Principle | Comments |
|---|---|---|
| Alumina air abrasion | Roughens and cleans surface; increases surface energy. | Common first-line conditioning for many cast alloys. [3] |
| Electrolytic etching | Selective surface dissolution. | More useful for some base-metal alloys than noble alloys. [3] |
| Tin plating | Deposits tin-rich surface. | Historically used to improve bonding to noble alloys. [3] |
| Silica coating / tribochemical silica | Creates silica-rich surface. | Followed by silane for dimethacrylate resin systems. [3] |
| Functional primers | Chemical coupling to alloy/oxide surface. | 10-MDP is important for oxide-containing surfaces; sulfur-containing monomers are useful for noble-metal surfaces in appropriate systems. |

### Functional monomers

10-MDP is a phosphate functional monomer that is particularly useful for bonding to metal oxides and zirconia-type oxide surfaces. For noble metals, sulfur-containing monomers such as VBATDT or related chemistry can be used in metal primers. Craig notes that 10-MDP and 4-META can improve retention of cast alloy restorations, with stronger effects often observed for base-metal alloys than noble alloys. [3]

Recent evidence: Pereira et al. found in a systematic review that alumina airborne-particle abrasion combined with a 10-MDP-based primer increased metal-to-acrylic resin bond strength in the studied removable-partial-denture framework systems. [20] Shimizu and Takeuchi also emphasize combined mechanical conditioning and chemically functional primers for silver-based alloys, although silver-rich alloys have additional sulfurization and durability concerns. [17]

### Clinical principles

Identify the alloy before choosing a primer.

Clean and air abrade according to the material/manufacturer protocol.

Avoid contamination after conditioning.

Apply the correct functional primer where indicated.

Use a compatible resin cement or adhesive.

Control film thickness and polymerization.

Recognize that bond durability depends on hydrolytic stability, thermocycling and the alloy surface chemistry.

EXAM MODE

Bonding to metal depends on micromechanical retention, chemical interaction and, where used, silica-mediated coupling. [3]

Surface preparation: clean and air abrade with alumina to create a rough, high-energy surface.

Alternative conditioning: electrolytic etching for selected base-metal alloys; tin plating for selected noble alloys; tribochemical silica coating where indicated.

Primer: select a functional monomer compatible with the alloy. 10-MDP is important for oxide-rich/base-metal surfaces; sulfur-containing monomers can be used for noble metals.

Resin cement/adhesive: apply according to manufacturer protocol and polymerize adequately.

Finish and protect the interface from contamination and excessive mechanical/thermal stress.

Contemporary evidence supports alumina abrasion followed by a suitable functional primer. Pereira et al. reported the strongest evidence for Al₂O₃ abrasion plus 10-MDP primer in the specific metal-acrylic framework systems they reviewed. [20]

Conclusion: Durable metal bonding is substrate specific. Surface treatment must be matched to the alloy chemistry rather than applying one universal protocol.

## QUESTION 7

Marks: 10   Properties of noble metal alloys used in operative dentistry.

STUDY MODE

Noble dental alloys contain metals with high resistance to oxidation and corrosion. In the ADA compositional classification cited by Phillips, high-noble alloys contain at least 60 wt% noble metal with at least 40 wt% gold, noble alloys contain at least 25 wt% noble metal, and predominantly base-metal alloys contain less than 25 wt% noble metals. [1]

| Class | ADA compositional criterion | Key implication |
|---|---|---|
| High noble (HN) | ≥60 wt% noble metals and ≥40 wt% Au | Excellent corrosion resistance; widely used in demanding indirect applications. |
| Noble (N) | ≥25 wt% noble metals | Intermediate composition and corrosion behavior. |
| Predominantly base metal (PB) | <25 wt% noble metals | Relies on passivating elements such as Cr for corrosion resistance. |

### Important noble metals

Gold: highly corrosion resistant, ductile and workable; strengthened by alloying.

Palladium: increases strength and corrosion resistance and can modify color and melting behavior.

Platinum: highly noble and corrosion resistant; raises melting temperature and contributes to strength.

Rhodium, ruthenium, iridium and osmium are noble metals but are less commonly used as major constituents in routine dental casting alloys. [1]

### Desired properties in operative dentistry

High resistance to tarnish and corrosion.

Adequate strength and yield strength after alloying.

Good ductility and burnishability where clinically useful.

Good castability and dimensional accuracy.

Suitable modulus and resilience for the intended restoration.

Biological acceptability and low ion release.

Controlled melting range and compatibility with investment/casting procedures.

Acceptable color and surface finish.

Pure gold alone lacks adequate strength and stiffness for many restorative applications, so alloying is necessary. Copper, platinum-group metals and other elements can strengthen and modify hardness, thermal properties and microstructure. [1]

Recent electrochemical evidence indicates that noble-metal content alone does not completely predict corrosion resistance. Grgur et al. showed composition-dependent electrochemical differences among commercial noble dental alloys, reinforcing the need to consider microstructure and phase distribution. [8]

EXAM MODE

| Property | Clinical importance |

Recent evidence: electrochemical testing shows that corrosion resistance varies among noble alloys according to detailed composition and microstructure, so noble-metal percentage should not be treated as the sole predictor of performance. [8]

Conclusion: Noble alloys combine corrosion resistance with useful mechanical and casting properties, but their performance depends on the complete alloy composition and microstructure.

## QUESTION 8

Marks: 25   Effect of different restorative materials on pulp dentin complex.

STUDY MODE

The pulp and dentin should be considered a functional unit, the pulpodentin complex. The biological effect of a restoration is not determined solely by the intrinsic toxicity of the material. It reflects the interaction among pre-existing pulpal status, remaining dentin thickness and permeability, operative trauma, thermal/mechanical stimuli, material chemistry, polymerization/setting behavior and bacterial microleakage. [6]

### Determinants of pulpal response

Preoperative pulp condition.

Depth and extent of caries.

Remaining dentin thickness and tubular permeability.

Number and diameter of exposed dentinal tubules.

Heat, desiccation, vibration and pressure during preparation.

Acidity and leachable components of the restorative material.

Polymerization shrinkage and interfacial stress.

Marginal seal and bacterial leakage.

Duration of exposure.

### Restorative materials and pulpodentin response

| Material | Principal biological concern / benefit | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Amalgam | Not intrinsically highly toxic at clinically relevant exposure; thermal conductivity and marginal leakage can contribute to sensitivity. Corrosion products may partially seal margins. | Insulation and good adaptation are important. |
| Resin composite | Residual monomers from incompletely polymerized material may be cytotoxic; polymerization shrinkage and leakage can indirectly injure pulp. | Adequate curing, adhesive sealing and incremental/appropriate placement are important. |
| Conventional GIC | Early acidity can be irritating in very deep cavities; chemical adhesion and fluoride release are beneficial. | Use with attention to pulpal depth and moisture control. |
| RMGI | HEMA and other resin components may increase biological activity when incompletely cured. | Follow curing protocol; use judiciously in deep areas. |
| Zinc phosphate | Initial low pH can irritate deep pulp; dentin thickness reduces diffusion. | Protect deep preparations when indicated. |
| Zinc polycarboxylate | Generally less irritating than zinc phosphate. | Favorable pulpal profile. |
| ZOE | Eugenol has a sedative effect and is relatively favorable to pulp; can interfere with resin polymerization. | Useful in selected temporary/protective applications. |
| Calcium hydroxide | High pH, antibacterial action and stimulation of hard-tissue repair; weak/soluble. | Selective use in deep areas and pulp capping. |
| Hydraulic calcium-silicate cements | High biocompatibility and bioactivity; support mineralized barrier formation and pulp repair. | Important contemporary vital-pulp materials. |
| Adhesive systems | Acid etching itself is not necessarily the main cause of pulpal injury if adequately sealed; uncured monomers and inadequate bonding can be problematic. | Sealing quality and polymerization are central. |

### Mechanisms of pulpal injury

Thermal injury: heat from preparation, curing or removal of restorations can be transmitted through dentin.

Mechanical injury: vibration, pressure, dehydration and direct odontoblast-process injury.

Chemical injury: acids, monomers, solvents and leachable components.

Hydrodynamic injury: fluid movement within dentinal tubules can activate pulpal sensory mechanisms.

Bacterial injury: microleakage allows bacteria or their products to diffuse through dentin and is often a major determinant of persistent pulpal inflammation.

Seltzer and Bender describe the pulpodentin complex as an integrated biological unit and emphasize the role of dentin as a protective barrier. [6] Thus, increasing remaining dentin thickness generally reduces the effective dose of chemical and bacterial insults reaching the pulp.

### Polymerization shrinkage and pulpal effects

Resin composites contract during polymerization, producing shrinkage stress. If interfacial adhesion is exceeded, gaps may develop, increasing leakage and postoperative sensitivity. Summitt et al. describe incremental placement as one strategy for reducing the amount of material polymerized at one time and the associated stress. [4] Contemporary evidence, however, indicates that shrinkage stress is multifactorial and depends on volumetric shrinkage, viscoelastic behavior, polymerization kinetics, cavity configuration and boundary conditions. [18,19]

Magne et al. demonstrated in large MOD restorations that shrinkage-induced cuspal deformation remains clinically relevant even with contemporary short-fiber reinforced composites, reinforcing the importance of material selection and stress management in large adhesive restorations. [19]

### Vital pulp therapy and bioactive materials

Modern pulp preservation emphasizes conservative caries management, adequate sealing and biologically favorable materials. Calcium hydroxide remains historically important, while hydraulic calcium-silicate materials have become major contemporary agents. Emara et al. found generally favorable biocompatibility and odontogenic/angiogenic effects among commercially available calcium-silicate cements, but substantial methodological heterogeneity prevented identification of one universally superior material. [12]

Cohen and contemporary endodontic sources emphasize that material selection must be integrated with accurate pulpal diagnosis, contamination control and a definitive restoration that provides a durable coronal seal. [6]

EXAM MODE

Introduction: The pulp and dentin function as a pulpodentin complex. Pulpal response to restoration depends on remaining dentin, pulpal status, operative trauma, material chemistry, thermal/mechanical challenge and especially bacterial leakage. [6]

Factors: depth of cavity; remaining dentin thickness; dentinal permeability; pre-existing inflammation; heat and vibration; desiccation; material toxicity; polymerization shrinkage; marginal seal; and microleakage.

[6]

Conclusion: The best pulpal protection is achieved by accurate diagnosis, conservative preparation, preservation of dentin, minimally traumatic technique, appropriate material selection and durable sealing.

## QUESTION 9

Marks: 25   Dimensional changes in restorative materials.

STUDY MODE

Dimensional change refers to a change in length, volume or geometry of a restorative material during setting, polymerization, hydration, water sorption, thermal cycling or aging. Dimensional stability is clinically important because a restoration must maintain intimate adaptation to tooth structure despite changes in temperature, moisture and functional loading. [1,3]

Figure 6. Schematic representation of common dimensional-change mechanisms in restorative materials.

### Major mechanisms

| Mechanism | Material examples | Clinical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Setting contraction/expansion | Amalgam, cements | Marginal gap or excessive pressure if change is uncontrolled. |
| Polymerization shrinkage | Resin composites, resin cements, RMGI | Shrinkage stress, cuspal deflection, marginal gap and sensitivity. |
| Water sorption / hygroscopic expansion | Resin composites, ionomeric materials | May partly compensate shrinkage but can also cause swelling, hydrolytic effects and dimensional instability. |
| Thermal expansion/contraction | All restorative materials | Repeated mismatch with tooth can contribute to interfacial stress and leakage. |
| Post-cure changes | Resin-based materials | Continued conversion and shrinkage may occur after initial light exposure. |
| Delayed expansion | Zinc-containing amalgam contaminated with moisture | Large late expansion and possible postoperative complications. |

### 1. Amalgam

Modern amalgams commonly show an initial contraction followed by a limited expansion as the setting reaction progresses. Craig describes the early contraction as related to alloy-particle dissolution in mercury, followed by expansion associated mainly with formation and growth of the matrix phase. [3]

The dimensional change is measured as the change in length of a standardized cylindrical specimen between defined times after trituration. Craig reports that ANSI/ADA Specification No. 1 evaluates the 24-hour dimensional change, with an acceptable range of approximately −15 to +20 µm/cm for the amalgam products discussed. [3]

Bhat et al. describe factors increasing setting expansion such as higher mercury/alloy ratio, larger particles, spherical particles, slow mixing, prolonged trituration, low condensation pressure and moisture contamination. Moisture contamination of zinc-containing alloys can produce delayed expansion. [2]

### 2. Resin composite polymerization shrinkage

Polymerization converts monomers into a cross-linked polymer network with reduced free volume, producing volumetric contraction. Shrinkage becomes clinically significant when the material is bonded to cavity walls and contraction is constrained, producing polymerization stress. [4,18]

Higher monomer conversion can increase mechanical properties but may also increase shrinkage.

Filler loading generally reduces the resin fraction and can reduce volumetric shrinkage.

Elastic modulus, reaction kinetics and cavity configuration influence shrinkage stress.

The C-factor represents the ratio of bonded to unbonded surfaces and affects stress development.

Incremental placement reduces the bulk cured at one time and can reduce stress under appropriate conditions. [4]

Academy of Dental Materials guidance identifies polymerization shrinkage, shrinkage rate, shrinkage stress, hygroscopic expansion and thermal expansion as important properties for resin-composite evaluation. [16]

Taylor et al. showed that resin composite core materials continued to increase in measured polymerization shrinkage over 24 hours, with wide material-specific variation. [18] Magne et al. demonstrated that shrinkage-induced cuspal deformation remains relevant in large MOD restorations even with short-fiber reinforced composites. [19]

### 3. Water sorption and hygroscopic expansion

Water is preferentially absorbed by the resin matrix of resin composites. Swelling can partially reduce an existing marginal gap but does not recreate the original adhesive bond and is usually insufficient to eliminate polymerization shrinkage completely. [4]

### 4. Thermal dimensional change

The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) quantifies dimensional change per degree of temperature change. The restorative material should ideally have thermal behavior compatible with enamel and dentin. Large CTE mismatch produces repeated expansion and contraction at the interface during hot and cold exposures. [1,2]

| Material / tissue | Representative CTE cited in project sources |
|---|---|
| Enamel | ~11.4 ppm/°C [2] |
| Dentin | ~8.3 ppm/°C [2] |
| Composite resin | ~20–60 ppm/°C [2] |
| Silver amalgam | ~25 ppm/°C [2] |
| GIC | ~10–11 ppm/°C [2] |
| Ceramics / silicate cement | ~7–8.5 ppm/°C [2] |

These values are source-specific representative values rather than universal constants. Formulation, temperature range, filler content and measurement method can change the reported CTE, so numerical comparisons should be interpreted with the source and material formulation in mind.

### Measurement methods

Linear dimensional-change measurement with dial gauges or interferometric methods.

Digital image or non-contact optical measurement for resin shrinkage.

Mercury or bonded-disk dilatometry for volumetric shrinkage in research settings.

Digital image correlation and three-dimensional micro-CT approaches for spatial shrinkage analysis.

Thermomechanical analysis for temperature-dependent dimensional behavior.

Three-dimensional micro-CT methods can visualize volumetric change and shrinkage vectors within a cavity, although they are more complex and time intensive than conventional measurements. [16]

EXAM MODE

Definition: Dimensional change is alteration in the length, volume or geometry of a restorative material during setting and service.

Major causes: setting contraction/expansion, polymerization shrinkage, water sorption/hygroscopic expansion, thermal expansion and contraction, post-cure changes and delayed expansion.

A. Amalgam: initial contraction may be followed by expansion during matrix formation. Modern amalgams commonly show a small net contraction. Dimensional change is measured on a standardized cylindrical specimen between defined time points. ANSI/ADA Specification No. 1 gives an approximate acceptable range of −15 to +20 µm/cm at 24 hours for amalgam. [3]

Factors increasing expansion include high mercury/alloy ratio, larger particles, spherical particles, slow mixing, prolonged trituration, low condensation pressure and moisture contamination. Zinc-containing amalgam contaminated by moisture can undergo delayed expansion. [2,3]

B. Resin composite: polymerization shrinkage produces contraction; when bonded to cavity walls, constraint generates shrinkage stress. Consequences include marginal gap formation, cuspal deflection, postoperative sensitivity and possible microleakage. [4,18]

Factors: monomer chemistry, filler fraction, degree of conversion, polymerization kinetics, elastic modulus and C-factor.

Management: appropriate incremental placement, optimized curing, low-shrinkage materials and stress-reducing restorative designs.

C. Water sorption: swelling of the resin matrix may partly compensate for shrinkage but cannot be assumed to restore the original adhesive seal. [4]

D. Thermal changes: CTE mismatch between restoration and tooth produces repeated interfacial stress during temperature changes. Enamel is approximately 11.4 ppm/°C and dentin approximately 8.3 ppm/°C in the cited source. [2]

Contemporary evidence: the Academy of Dental Materials identifies shrinkage, shrinkage stress, hygroscopic expansion and thermal expansion as priority properties for resin-composite evaluation. [16] Recent studies demonstrate persistent material-specific shrinkage and clinically relevant cuspal deformation. [18,19]

Conclusion: Dimensional stability is a dynamic property. The clinically relevant variable is not simply the magnitude of dimensional change but how that change interacts with adhesion, tooth structure, thermal cycling and the oral environment.

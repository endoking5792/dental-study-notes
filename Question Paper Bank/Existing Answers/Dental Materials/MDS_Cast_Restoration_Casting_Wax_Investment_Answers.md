## QUESTION 1 - 25 MARKS

The biologic form and choice of materials for cast restoration

Mark allocation: 25

### STUDY MODE

A cast restoration should not be selected or shaped merely as a mechanically strong replacement for missing tooth structure. Its form must reproduce biologically compatible tooth morphology while preserving sound tissue, maintaining pulp and periodontal health, controlling occlusal loading, permitting hygiene, and providing adequate material thickness. Summitt et al. emphasize that cast restoration design should conserve tooth structure while protecting the remaining tooth from fracture and functional stresses [3].

#### 1. Meaning of biologic form

Biologic form means restoration morphology that is compatible with the tooth, pulp, periodontium, occlusion and oral environment while restoring the missing anatomy.

It includes correct axial and proximal contours, physiologic emergence profile, contact areas, embrasures, marginal adaptation, occlusal anatomy and appropriate cusp protection.

The restoration should reproduce rather than arbitrarily exaggerate natural anatomy. Overcontouring may increase plaque retention and gingival inflammation [3].

#### 2. Biologic objectives of a cast restoration

Preserve maximum sound tooth structure while removing unsupported or diseased tissue.

Protect weakened cusps and remaining tooth structure from functional and parafunctional fracture.

Preserve pulp vitality by avoiding unnecessary reduction, overheating and excessive desiccation.

Provide a smooth, well-adapted margin that minimizes plaque retention and microleakage.

Maintain proximal contact and embrasure form that permit dental hygiene and prevent food impaction.

Maintain physiologic occlusion, with contacts preferably on sound restorative material rather than directly on a margin.

Provide an emergence profile compatible with the periodontal tissues.

Permit sufficient material bulk without overcontouring.

#### 3. Form of cast restorations

Inlay: completely intracoronal; suitable for moderate intracoronal lesions when cusps remain adequately supported.

Onlay: intracoronal component plus cuspal coverage; indicated when cusps require protection.

Partial-coverage crown: combines intracoronal and extracoronal features.

Complete-coverage crown: covers the entire clinical crown when remaining structure is substantially compromised.

Choice should be based on the least destructive design that restores function and provides predictable fracture resistance [3].

#### 4. Biologic contour

Axial contour should reproduce the natural tooth rather than create a prominent artificial bulge.

Proximal emergence profiles of natural teeth are generally flat or concave, creating an open gingival embrasure that facilitates hygiene [3].

Overcontouring is often a consequence of inadequate tooth reduction because the technician must create material thickness somewhere; this is a preparation-design problem as much as a laboratory problem [3].

Where recession has exposed root surfaces, a flatter contour may better reproduce root anatomy.

#### 5. Margins and periodontal considerations

Whenever possible, margins should be supragingival because intracrevicular margins increase technical difficulty and may compromise periodontal health [3].

Where a subgingival/intracrevicular margin is unavoidable because of caries, fracture, existing restoration or other factors, tissue injury should be minimized.

Common cast-gold finish lines are knife edge, chamfer and shoulder. A chamfer is commonly preferred for cast gold because it is conservative and provides adequate space for metal without unnecessary overcontouring [3].

Bevels may improve the ability to burnish some gold margins, but excessive beveling can increase vertical marginal discrepancy and may risk encroachment on the epithelial attachment [3].

#### 6. Choice of restorative material

Cast gold alloys have high clinical longevity, excellent castability, corrosion resistance, ductility and wear compatibility with enamel. Long-term studies summarized by Summitt report high survival over decades [3].

Gold is especially useful in posterior teeth, heavy occlusal loading and parafunctional activity because of its combination of strength, ductility and adjustability [3].

Base-metal alloys such as Co-Cr and Ni-Cr provide high strength and stiffness and can be useful where thin sections or high rigidity are required, but their handling, melting temperature and biological considerations differ from noble alloys [1,2].

Ceramics are selected when esthetics is a dominant requirement. They offer tooth-colored appearance but are less forgiving of inadequate thickness, sharp internal geometry and tensile stress than ductile gold.

Indirect resin/composite materials can offer esthetics and repairability but generally have different wear, stiffness, polymerization and long-term behavior from cast alloys.

Thus material choice is not a competition in which one material is universally superior. It is a function of tooth structure, location, occlusion, esthetic demand, available clearance, bonding strategy, moisture control and required longevity.

#### 7. Modern interpretation

The classical cast-gold principle remains biologically relevant, but the material landscape has changed. Digital scanning, CAD design, additive pattern fabrication, milling and direct metal additive manufacturing can now bypass several conventional wax and casting steps [5-10].

Recent studies show that digitally fabricated patterns can be highly accurate, but the manufacturing route matters. Milled patterns generally showed higher trueness than printed crown patterns in one Journal of Dentistry study [5].

Digital workflows therefore do not eliminate biologic design principles. They shift the source of dimensional error from wax manipulation toward scanning, CAD compensation, printer or milling parameters, material shrinkage and post-processing [5-8].

### EXAM MODE

#### Introduction and definition

A cast restoration is an indirectly fabricated restoration produced from a pattern and designed to replace lost tooth structure while restoring biologic form, function and appearance. The biologically ideal restoration reproduces natural morphology without sacrificing sound tissue or compromising pulp, periodontal or occlusal health [3].

#### Biologic principles

Conservation of tooth structure.

Removal of unsupported or structurally compromised tooth tissue where necessary.

Protection of weakened cusps and prevention of fracture.

Preservation of pulp vitality through conservative preparation and thermal control.

Smooth, accurately adapted margins.

Physiologic proximal contacts and embrasures.

Natural axial contours and emergence profile.

Occlusal contacts positioned on restoration or tooth structure rather than restoration margins.

Margins preferably supragingival when clinically possible [3].

#### Choice of cast restorative material

| Material | Key advantages | Typical clinical emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Gold alloy | Excellent castability; ductile; corrosion resistant; burnishable; wear compatible with enamel | Posterior teeth; heavy function; bruxism; when longevity and adjustability are priorities |
| Co-Cr / Ni-Cr | High strength and stiffness; relatively thin sections possible | High-stress frameworks and situations requiring rigidity; technique sensitive because of high melting temperatures |
| Ceramic | Excellent esthetics; high hardness; brittle compared with gold | Esthetic zone; adequate thickness and adhesive protocol required |
| Indirect composite/resin | Esthetic; repairable; less stiff than metal/ceramic | Selected indirect esthetic restorations where stress and wear requirements are appropriate |

#### Clinical selection sequence

Assess caries, cracks, remaining cusp thickness and periodontal status.

Determine whether an intracoronal restoration can preserve adequate tooth strength.

If cusps are weakened, consider onlay or partial coverage rather than an inlay.

Select material according to stress, clearance, esthetics, bonding and opposing dentition.

Design contours and margins to reproduce biologic morphology.

Verify occlusion, proximal contact, cleansability and marginal integrity.

#### Conclusion

The biologic form of a cast restoration is more important than simply achieving maximum material thickness. The ideal design conserves and protects the tooth, respects pulp and periodontal tissues, reproduces natural contour and occlusion, and uses a material whose mechanical, biologic and esthetic properties fit the clinical situation [3].

## QUESTION 2 - 50 MARKS

Discuss the various methods of preventing, minimizing and compensating for casting shrinkage

Mark allocation: 50

### STUDY MODE

Dimensional accuracy in lost-wax casting depends on controlling dimensional changes at several stages: wax pattern formation, investment setting, investment thermal expansion, alloy solidification and cooling. Shama Bhat describes casting shrinkage as arising principally from wax-pattern shrinkage and alloy shrinkage, while Phillips emphasizes that the investment must be expanded sufficiently to compensate for the contraction of the casting alloy [1,2].

#### 1. Definition and sequence

Casting shrinkage is the net dimensional contraction that must be compensated so that the final casting reproduces the intended pattern and fits the prepared tooth.

Major dimensional changes occur during wax cooling, investment setting and heating, alloy solidification, and cooling of the solid alloy to oral temperature [1,2].

#### 2. Wax-pattern shrinkage

Inlay wax has a very high coefficient of thermal expansion compared with most dental materials. Phillips reports an average linear thermal expansion coefficient around 350 × 10^-6/K over the relevant temperature range, with substantial contraction when cooled from 37 °C toward room temperature [1].

Soft wax generally undergoes greater dimensional change and is more susceptible to deformation under investment expansion.

Residual stresses introduced during uneven heating, carving, molding or cooling can relax later and distort the pattern.

Prevention: use a suitable inlay wax, soften uniformly, avoid repeated heating, avoid unnecessary pressure, minimize temperature changes, use a die lubricant correctly, and invest as soon as practical [1,2].

Indirect wax pattern fabrication reduces the large mouth-to-room temperature excursion encountered by direct patterns.

#### 3. Alloy shrinkage

Alloy contraction occurs in stages: cooling of the liquid toward the liquidus, solidification, and thermal contraction of the solid alloy to room or oral temperature.

Liquid contraction and solidification contraction can be fed by a connected reservoir of molten alloy if the sprue system is correctly designed.

Solid-state thermal contraction cannot be eliminated and is the major dimensional change requiring mold compensation [1,2].

Phillips lists representative linear solidification shrinkage values of about 1.56%, 1.37% and 1.42% for Type I, II and III gold alloys, and about 2.30% for Type IV Ni-Cr and Co-Cr alloys [1]. These are representative rather than universal values.

#### 4. Investment setting expansion

Normal setting expansion enlarges the mold during setting in air.

Type I investments are intended to provide controlled setting expansion for partial compensation of casting contraction; Phillips reports a maximum setting expansion in air of 0.6% for Type I, with approximately 0.4% typical [1].

Setting expansion is influenced by water/powder ratio, mixing time, manipulation, binder chemistry, confinement and pattern geometry [1].

Excessive setting expansion can itself distort a wax pattern, particularly thin-walled MOD patterns.

#### 5. Hygroscopic expansion

Hygroscopic setting expansion occurs when a gypsum-containing investment sets in contact with water. It can be considerably greater than normal setting expansion [1].

Phillips reports that Type II investments should exhibit approximately 1.2% to 2.2% setting expansion in water [1].

Factors include silica content and particle size, hemihydrate form, water/powder ratio, mixing time, and the timing and conditions of water contact [1].

Advantages: useful for compensating shrinkage of low-fusing gold alloys.

Limitations: technique sensitivity, risk of nonuniform expansion and wax-pattern distortion.

#### 6. Thermal expansion of investment

High-heat casting relies principally on thermal expansion of the investment to enlarge the mold.

Quartz undergoes an alpha-to-beta inversion around 573 °C with associated expansion, while cristobalite undergoes its major transition over approximately 200-270 °C [1].

Cristobalite investments expand earlier and often more sharply than quartz-containing investments [1].

High-heat phosphate-bonded investments are used for high-fusing alloys and can be formulated to provide substantial thermal expansion [1].

#### 7. Ring liner and restriction control

Liners provide resilience and reduce rigid restriction of investment expansion.

Phillips notes that two liners can provide greater effective setting and thermal expansion than one liner [1].

Expansion is often greater longitudinally than radially in a rigid ring. A liner that is slightly shorter than the ring can reduce longitudinal restriction and promote more uniform expansion [1].

Overly thick investment around the pattern or poorly positioned patterns can alter expansion and gas escape.

#### 8. Water/powder and liquid/powder control

For gypsum investments, the water/powder ratio must be accurately controlled because increased liquid generally reduces setting and strength characteristics.

For phosphate investments, the concentration of special liquid can be adjusted to control expansion, but the manufacturer's prescribed range must be respected.

Shama Bhat emphasizes accurate measurement of powder and gauging liquid/water because expansion is a deliberate dimensional-control variable [2].

#### 9. Sprue and reservoir as shrinkage-control systems

Sprue diameter should approximate the thickest part of the pattern [1,2].

A reservoir or runner bar should remain molten longer than the restoration so it can feed metal during solidification.

The sprue should be attached to a bulky region and directed so that metal flows toward thinner sections.

An oversized sprue can create localized overheating and suck-back porosity, whereas an undersized or excessively long sprue may freeze prematurely and produce incomplete casting [1,2].

Chill or auxiliary sprues can act as cooling pins in bulky pontic areas [1].

#### 10. Die and pattern compensation

Type IV and Type V die stones can be selected according to required dimensional behavior. Type V stones have greater setting expansion and may be useful where greater casting compensation is needed [1,2].

Die spacers can create controlled cement space and can sometimes be used to create dimensional compensation, but they should not be treated as a substitute for proper casting compensation.

Pattern/die investing can reduce wax-pattern distortion by avoiding pattern removal before investment setting.

#### 11. Technique selection

Low-heat/hygroscopic technique: emphasizes setting and hygroscopic expansion of the investment.

High-heat/thermal technique: emphasizes thermal expansion of the investment.

Modern phosphate investments may permit rapid burnout or ringless systems, but these alter the thermal and mechanical conditions and therefore require manufacturer-specific control [1].

#### 12. Contemporary developments

Digital workflows can replace conventional hand-waxed patterns with milled or printed patterns. A 2022 Journal of Dentistry study found milled crown patterns had higher trueness than printed patterns, although the deviations were small enough that clinical fit was not necessarily different [5].

A 2023 Journal of Dentistry study similarly showed that CAD/CAM technique affected the trueness of fixed partial denture patterns used for casting or pressing [6].

Digital impressions and printed casts do not eliminate dimensional error. A 2025 Journal of Esthetic and Restorative Dentistry study found that digital impression accuracy varied by specimen geometry and that inlay specimens remained challenging for all tested technologies [7].

Direct metal additive manufacturing increasingly provides an alternative to conventional casting for selected frameworks. A 2024 systematic review found substantial heterogeneity and no simple universal accuracy advantage for conventional, milling or selective laser melting approaches [11].

### EXAM MODE

#### Introduction

Casting shrinkage is the dimensional contraction occurring during wax pattern cooling and, more importantly, during solidification and subsequent cooling of the cast alloy. Since the restoration must reproduce the prepared tooth accurately, shrinkage must be prevented where possible, minimized by technique and compensated by controlled mold expansion [1,2].

#### A. Prevention and minimization of wax-pattern shrinkage

Use dimensionally stable, appropriate inlay wax.

Uniformly soften and cool wax; avoid localized overheating.

Minimize repeated heating and manipulation.

Use a correct die lubricant without excessive thickness.

Carve with sharp instruments without pulling wax from margins.

Avoid unnecessary temperature changes and prolonged storage.

Invest the pattern promptly; if delay is unavoidable, store according to the wax manufacturer's instructions and minimize stress relaxation [1,2].

#### B. Control of alloy shrinkage by sprue-reservoir design

Sprue diameter approximately equals the thickest part of the pattern.

Attach sprue to a bulky area and direct flow toward thinner areas.

Use a reservoir/runner bar to provide molten metal during solidification.

Avoid excessively thin or long sprues because of premature freezing.

Avoid excessively large or short sprues that can create hot spots and suck-back porosity [1,2].

#### C. Compensation by investment expansion

1. Normal setting expansion.

2. Hygroscopic setting expansion.

3. Thermal expansion.

Setting/hygroscopic expansion is emphasized in low-heat/hygroscopic techniques.

Thermal expansion is emphasized in high-heat techniques.

Control water/powder or liquid/powder ratio, mixing time, liner configuration and confinement.

Use the investment appropriate for the alloy's melting range [1,2].

#### D. Hygroscopic method

Use a gypsum-bonded investment formulated for hygroscopic expansion.

Allow setting in contact with water under controlled conditions.

Control silica content, particle size, liquid ratio and timing of water exposure.

Useful particularly for lower-fusing gold alloys, but highly technique sensitive [1].

#### E. Thermal/high-heat method

Use a suitable high-heat investment.

Control burnout schedule and final mold temperature.

Quartz or cristobalite provides thermal expansion through crystalline transformations.

Phosphate-bonded investments are preferred for high-fusing alloys and metal-ceramic alloys [1,2].

#### F. Dimensional control of the ring

Use an appropriate liner and avoid rigid restriction of investment expansion.

Position pattern centrally with adequate investment around it.

Maintain appropriate investment thickness for gas escape.

Use ringless systems only with the prescribed investment and liquid because their expansion differs from conventional lined rings [1].

#### G. Die and CAD/CAM compensation

Type IV stone is the conventional high-strength low-expansion die material; Type V provides higher expansion when required [1].

Controlled die spacer can establish cement space but is not a substitute for correct mold compensation.

CAD/CAM milled or printed patterns reduce manual wax deformation but introduce scanner, software, machining/printing and post-processing variables [5,6].

#### Flowchart

Wax contraction → investment expansion → alloy solidification shrinkage → solid-state thermal contraction → final casting. The aim is to make the mold expansion approximately balance the net casting contraction without introducing nonuniform distortion.

#### Conclusion

Accurate casting is not achieved by one compensatory trick. It is a controlled dimensional-balance problem involving wax, investment, ring configuration, sprue-reservoir design, alloy temperature and cooling. Modern digital pattern fabrication reduces some wax-related errors but does not remove the fundamental need for dimensional compensation [1,2,5,6].

## QUESTION 3 - 25 MARKS

Discuss the causes and their management of distortion of Mesio-Occluso-Distal (M.O.D.) wax pattern

Mark allocation: 25

### STUDY MODE

Wax-pattern distortion is one of the most important causes of poor casting fit. Phillips identifies occluded air, physical deformation during molding/carving/removal, release of stresses trapped during cooling, excessive storage and temperature changes as major causes [1]. MOD patterns are particularly susceptible because the proximal walls are thin and can be displaced by investment expansion.

#### 1. Causes

Thermal contraction during cooling from working temperature.

Uneven softening or localized heating causing nonuniform contraction.

Residual stresses from molding, carving, pressure or cooling, followed by stress relaxation.

Elastic memory of wax causing partial recovery toward a previous shape.

Mechanical deformation during carving or finishing.

Distortion during removal from the die or tooth.

Excessive or poorly controlled die lubricant thickness.

Prolonged storage before investing.

Temperature fluctuations during storage or transport.

Investment setting and hygroscopic expansion acting nonuniformly on thin proximal walls.

Use of wax that is too soft.

Excessive investment manipulation or vibration.

Improper sprue attachment or a heavy sprue attached to a delicate pattern.

#### 2. Characteristic MOD mechanism

During investment setting, the expanding investment exerts outward forces on the proximal walls.

The gingival/proximal portions can move outward more readily than the thicker occlusal bar of wax.

Thin walls deform more readily than thick walls.

Greater investment setting expansion and softer wax increase the likelihood of displacement [1].

#### 3. Management during pattern fabrication

Use a suitable Type II inlay wax for indirect pattern construction.

Uniformly soften the wax and adapt it in controlled increments.

Overfill the preparation and carve rather than repeatedly adding small stressed fragments.

Use sharp carving instruments and avoid pulling wax from margins.

Use minimal, controlled lubricant.

Allow adequate cooling and avoid sudden temperature changes.

Do not repeatedly remelt the entire pattern.

#### 4. Removal and storage

Remove the pattern along its path of withdrawal.

For MOD patterns, use a staple-like wire or equivalent support so force is distributed near proximal areas; withdraw parallel to the axial walls [1].

Avoid touching the pattern unnecessarily because finger temperature can alter wax dimensions.

Invest immediately whenever possible.

If a delay is unavoidable, follow controlled low-temperature storage recommended for the wax; Shama Bhat describes cold water/refrigeration as a practical method for limiting stress-relaxation distortion [2].

#### 5. Investment-related management

Use an investment with controlled setting expansion appropriate for the pattern.

Avoid excessive expansion when thin MOD walls are present.

Control water/powder or liquid/powder ratio.

Minimize excessive vibration.

Maintain adequate investment support around the pattern.

Pattern/die investing can reduce distortion associated with removal and handling; Phillips notes this as a highly accurate approach [1].

#### 6. Modern digital alternative

CAD/CAM can generate milled wax or printed resin patterns directly from a digital design, reducing manual wax deformation.

However, digital patterns are not automatically more accurate. A Journal of Dentistry study found milled crown patterns had higher trueness than printed patterns, while a later study showed CAD/CAM manufacturing technique influenced FPD pattern trueness [5,6].

### EXAM MODE

#### Introduction

Distortion of an MOD wax pattern is a dimensional alteration that prevents the final casting from accurately reproducing the prepared cavity. Because wax is thermoplastic, highly thermally expansive and capable of stress relaxation, distortion may occur during fabrication, removal, storage, investing and investment setting [1].

#### Causes

Thermal contraction from working temperature to room temperature.

Uneven heating and cooling.

Residual stresses and stress relaxation.

Elastic memory of wax.

Mechanical deformation during molding, carving and removal.

Prolonged storage and temperature fluctuations.

Excessively soft wax.

Excessive die lubricant.

Investment setting/hygroscopic expansion, especially of thin proximal walls.

Excessive vibration or manipulation.

Heavy or incorrectly attached sprue.

#### Why MOD patterns are vulnerable

The proximal walls are relatively thin. During investment setting, expanding investment can push the proximal walls outward while the thicker occlusal portion resists movement. Thus gingival/proximal dimensions may become enlarged or displaced. Phillips notes that distortion increases as pattern thickness decreases and with increasing effective setting expansion [1].

#### Management

Use appropriate Type II inlay wax.

Uniformly soften and adapt wax in layers.

Carve with sharp instruments without pulling margins.

Use minimal separating medium.

Cool uniformly and avoid abrupt temperature changes.

Remove along the path of withdrawal; support MOD patterns during removal.

Invest immediately whenever possible.

Use controlled investment expansion and accurate powder/liquid ratio.

Avoid excessive vibration.

Consider pattern/die investing for maximum accuracy.

Use digital milled/printed patterns when indicated, while controlling digital manufacturing parameters [5,6].

#### Conclusion

The best management of MOD wax-pattern distortion is prevention rather than correction. A stable wax, controlled thermal history, atraumatic removal, prompt investing and controlled investment expansion are the fundamental measures [1,2].

## QUESTION 4 - 10 MARKS

Causes of defective casting

Mark allocation: 10

### STUDY MODE

This short-answer topic is best understood by linking the material property or casting variable to its effect on dimensional accuracy, surface quality and clinical fit.

#### Definition

A defective casting is one that fails to reproduce the intended pattern accurately or possesses unacceptable surface, internal or dimensional defects [1].

#### Classification

Distortion

Surface roughness and irregularities

Porosity

Incomplete or missing detail [1].

#### Major causes

Distorted wax pattern due to manipulation, stress relaxation, thermal changes or investment expansion.

Poor investment manipulation, bubbles, inadequate wetting or excessive vibration.

Incomplete wax elimination, leaving carbon or residue and increasing back pressure.

Incorrect sprue diameter, length or attachment.

Insufficient alloy quantity, incomplete melting, incorrect alloy temperature or inadequate casting force.

Premature solidification caused by low alloy or mold temperature.

Gas absorption, turbulent casting or oxidizing flame.

Investment permeability that is insufficient for adequate venting.

Improper alloy reuse or contamination.

#### Management

Control wax fabrication and invest promptly.

Use correct investment and manufacturer-recommended powder/liquid ratio.

Ensure complete wetting and vacuum mixing where indicated.

Use correct sprue-reservoir design.

Eliminate wax completely.

Use correct alloy and casting temperature and adequate casting force.

Use a reducing flame or controlled atmosphere appropriate to the alloy.

Inspect the casting under magnification and section or radiograph when internal porosity is suspected [1,2].

#### Conclusion

Most casting defects are process-control failures rather than unavoidable events. A systematic diagnosis should move backward from the defect to pattern, investment, burnout, sprue, alloy and casting variables.

Premature solidification caused by low alloy or mold temperature.

Gas absorption, turbulent casting or oxidizing flame.

Investment permeability that is insufficient for adequate venting.

Improper alloy reuse or contamination.

Control wax fabrication and invest promptly.

Use correct investment and manufacturer-recommended powder/liquid ratio.

Ensure complete wetting and vacuum mixing where indicated.

Use correct sprue-reservoir design.

Use correct alloy and casting temperature and adequate casting force.

#### Definition

#### Types

Centrifugal casting machines

Induction/resistance melting with centrifugal casting

Direct-current arc melting machines

#### Centrifugal principle

A spring or motor generates rotational motion; molten alloy is driven into the mold by centrifugal force. It is widely used in dental casting.

#### Induction melting

An alternating magnetic field induces electrical currents in the alloy, rapidly heating it. Phillips notes that induction equipment is particularly useful for many base-metal applications [1].

An electric arc between the alloy and a water-cooled tungsten electrode produces very high temperatures and very rapid melting. Overheating is a major risk [1].

The mold may be evacuated to facilitate filling and pressure can then force molten alloy into the cavity. Titanium and titanium alloys require specialized inert-gas casting systems [1].

#### Selection

Machine choice depends on alloy melting range, reactivity, required casting force, mold system and laboratory workflow. There is no universal machine that is best for every alloy [1].

## QUESTION 8 - 25 MARKS

Define die. What are the materials used. Describe electroformed dies.

Mark allocation: 25

### STUDY MODE

A die is a positive replica of a prepared tooth or other structure on which an indirect restoration is fabricated. Accuracy of the die is fundamental because every subsequent wax, CAD, investment and casting step reproduces the geometry inherited from the die [1,2].

#### 1. Definition and requirements

A die is a positive reproduction of a prepared tooth, generally used as the working surface for fabrication of an indirect restoration.

An ideal die should reproduce fine detail accurately, remain dimensionally stable, possess adequate hardness and abrasion resistance, tolerate carving and waxing procedures, and be compatible with the impression material [1].

#### 2. Die materials

Type IV dental stone: high-strength, low-expansion material and the routine choice for many indirect restorations.

Type V dental stone: high-strength, higher-expansion stone; useful where increased dimensional compensation is desirable [1,2].

Resin dies: epoxy, acrylic and polyester systems can provide useful hardness and dimensional behavior, but compatibility with impression materials is product-specific [1].

Electroformed metal dies: copper or silver can be deposited electrochemically on an impression surface to produce a hard, abrasion-resistant working surface [1].

Die-investment combinations: a compatible refractory die and investment can be used to reduce pattern distortion and improve casting accuracy [1].

#### 3. Electroformed dies: principle

The impression surface is first made electrically conductive by metallization.

A thin conductive metal layer is deposited on the impression surface.

The impression is placed in an electroforming bath containing the desired metal ions.

With direct current, metal is deposited onto the conductive impression surface, building a coherent metal shell.

The shell is strengthened and backed with a supporting die material.

The completed metal-covered die is separated from the impression and used for pattern fabrication [1].

#### 4. Advantages of electroformed dies

High surface hardness and abrasion resistance.

Good dimensional detail reproduction.

Very fine line detail can be reproduced; Phillips reports reproduction of lines approximately 4 μm or less under appropriate conditions [1].

Useful where repeated waxing and manipulation would damage softer die surfaces.

#### 5. Limitations

Technique sensitive.

Requires specialized electroforming equipment and solutions.

Potential dimensional change can occur during impression and electroforming steps.

Compatibility is dependent on the specific impression material and electroforming system.

More cumbersome than Type IV stone for routine work.

The term 'silver-plated die' is technically imprecise because the process is electroforming rather than simple surface plating of a conventional stone die [1].

#### 6. Contemporary relevance

Digital workflows increasingly generate virtual dies and printed working models, but the same principle remains: the working replica must be dimensionally accurate and stable.

Recent work in Journal of Esthetic and Restorative Dentistry and Journal of Prosthetic Dentistry shows that printed casts can exhibit technology-, material- and geometry-dependent deviations, so digital fabrication does not remove the need for die accuracy [7-9].

## QUESTION 14 - 10 MARKS

Flow properties of inlay wax

Mark allocation: 10

### STUDY MODE

This short-answer topic is best understood by linking the material property or casting variable to its effect on dimensional accuracy, surface quality and clinical fit.

#### Definition

Flow is the time-dependent deformation of wax under a specified load and temperature, commonly expressed as the percentage reduction in height of a standardized specimen under defined conditions [1].

#### Importance

Permits adaptation to cavity walls.

Affects carving and marginal reproduction.

Influences dimensional stability during investing.

Excessive flow increases deformation risk.

Insufficient flow can impair adaptation and increase stress during carving.

#### Temperature effect

Flow increases markedly as temperature rises. Wax becomes much more plastic near and above its transition/softening range [1,2].

#### Type I vs Type II

Phillips reports lower permissible flow for harder Type I wax at lower temperatures and greater flow allowances at higher temperatures; Type II is formulated for easier indirect manipulation [1].

#### Clinical control

Use the correct wax for direct or indirect technique.

Avoid overheating.

Use uniform heating.

Do not handle patterns at elevated temperature.

Invest promptly after final adjustment [1,2].

#### Evidence

Ito et al., as discussed in Phillips, found that casting shrinkage decreased as wax flow increased under the conditions studied, illustrating that wax flow affects dimensional behavior but should not be interpreted as 'more flow is always better' [1].

## QUESTION 15 - 10 MARKS

Divestment material

Mark allocation: 10

### STUDY MODE

This short-answer topic is best understood by linking the material property or casting variable to its effect on dimensional accuracy, surface quality and clinical fit.

#### Definition

A divestment material or divesting medium is used to remove or facilitate removal of the investment material from the casting after solidification.

#### Requirements

Should facilitate investment breakdown without damaging the casting.

Should not chemically attack the alloy.

Should minimize surface contamination.

Should permit efficient recovery and finishing.

#### Methods

Mechanical fracture of investment.

Water quenching where appropriate for compatible noble-alloy/gypsum systems.

Ultrasonic cleaning.

Sandblasting with suitable abrasive for appropriate alloys/investments.

Commercial divestment solutions where indicated [1,2].

#### Gypsum investment

Quenching a hot gypsum investment can make it friable and facilitate removal, while noble-metal castings may remain in a favorable annealed condition for finishing [1].

#### Phosphate investment

Phosphate investments are harder and more difficult to remove. Mechanical methods, ultrasonic cleaning and suitable abrasive procedures are commonly required [1,2].

#### Safety

Aggressive chemical dissolution of silica, especially hydrofluoric acid, carries serious hazards and should not be treated as a routine chairside procedure [1].

## Recent advances: evidence notes for viva and long essays

The contemporary literature does not justify claiming that digital manufacturing has simply made conventional casting obsolete. Instead, the workflow is becoming hybrid: digital scanning and CAD design may generate milled or printed patterns for conventional casting, or may directly drive additive metal manufacturing. Accuracy remains dependent on the entire manufacturing chain.

Çakmak et al. [5] found that manufacturing technique affected crown-pattern trueness, with milled patterns showing higher trueness than printed patterns in the tested system.

Donmez et al. [6] demonstrated that CAD/CAM manufacturing technique influenced the trueness of fixed partial denture patterns used for casting or pressing.

Piao et al. [7] showed that digital impression accuracy varies with the geometry of the tested specimen and that inlay geometry remains challenging for digital workflows.

Parize et al. [8] found that abutment geometry influences the accuracy of casts obtained by digital and analog workflows, emphasizing that digital does not mean geometry-independent.

Park et al. [9] demonstrated that different 3D-printing technologies produce different dimensional deviations in dental casts.

Kouhi et al. [10] reviewed recent additive-manufacturing developments across polymers, metals, ceramics and patient-specific devices.

Revilla-León et al. [11] found high heterogeneity in studies comparing conventional, milling and additive manufacturing of Co-Cr frameworks, arguing against a simplistic universal accuracy advantage.

Matsumura et al. [12] demonstrated a contemporary titanium casting approach using calcium aluminate-bonded magnesia investment and argon pressure casting, illustrating how investment chemistry and inert-gas casting remain important for reactive alloys.

## Examiner-oriented synthesis

The most defensible way to approach these questions is to think of casting as a dimensional-control system rather than a sequence of isolated laboratory steps. Wax creates the positive form; the die defines the geometry; the investment reproduces and compensates the geometry; the sprue-reservoir system feeds the casting; the burnout stage must create a clean mold; and the casting machine must deliver molten alloy with sufficient temperature and force while minimizing gas incorporation and turbulence. Most defects can therefore be traced backward through this chain.

## STUDY MODE

### Definition and normal anatomy

A proximal contact is the area of proximal height of contour of one tooth that touches the adjacent tooth in the same arch. Immediately after eruption it is essentially a point, but physiologic tooth movement and wear convert it into a small contact area. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 34-35, “contact point becomes an area”] [Certain]

The contact is located in the incisal third in the anterior teeth and progressively more cervically toward the posterior teeth, where it lies near the junction of the occlusal and middle thirds or within the middle third. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 35, “incisal third”] [Certain]

### Importance

Prevents food impaction and protects the proximal periodontal tissues from repeated wedging of food. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 35, “prevents food impaction”] [Certain]

Permits the interdental papilla to occupy the gingival embrasure and supports physiologic periodontal health. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 35, “interdental papillae filling”] [Certain]

Maintains proximal tooth position and contributes to stability and integrity of the dental arch. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 35, “maintains the integrity”] [Certain]

Provides appropriate embrasure form for escape of food during mastication and reduces harmful interproximal stress. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 36, “escape of food from the occlusal surfaces”] [Certain]

Contributes to correct anatomic form and self-cleansing contours of the restored tooth. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 34-37, “more self-cleansing”] [Certain]

An open contact may permit food stagnation, tooth movement, periodontal inflammation and caries risk in the proximal region. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 35, “food impaction between the teeth”] [Certain]

### Requirements of an effective contact

The contact should be anatomically positioned and sufficiently firm to resist food impaction without producing an excessively tight, non-cleansable interproximal area. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 34-36, “contact and embrasure forms”] [Likely]

The proximal surface should be convex, with balanced facial, lingual, occlusal or incisal, and gingival embrasures. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 35-36, “four embrasures”] [Certain]

The gingival aspect should be free from overhangs and should permit a physiologic interdental papilla. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 34-35, “proper proximal surfaces”] [Certain]

After restoration, floss should pass through the contact with controlled resistance rather than being impossible to pass or excessively loose. [RECALL] [Likely]

### Methods of obtaining an effective contact point

#### 1. Pre-wedging

Pre-wedging before preparation produces temporary separation of the adjacent teeth. This separation compensates for matrix thickness and facilitates a firm contact after removal of the wedge. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 580-581, “preoperative wedging”] [Certain]

In posterior composite restorations, pre-wedging is particularly important because composite cannot be condensed laterally against a matrix in the same way as amalgam. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 588, “almost totally dependent on the contour”] [Certain]

#### 2. Correct matrix adaptation

The matrix must contact the adjacent tooth at the intended contact area before composite placement. A matrix that is separated from the adjacent tooth produces a flat or open contact even when the restoration is otherwise well adapted. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 588, “absolute contact with”] [Certain]

The matrix should be burnished or otherwise contoured to reproduce the natural proximal convexity rather than being left as a flat sheet. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 527, “altered (burnished)”] [Certain]

#### 3. Wedge application

The wedge stabilizes the matrix, separates the teeth and limits gingival extrusion of restorative material. Its position and shape must be selected according to proximal anatomy and gingival depth because an inappropriate wedge can distort the matrix. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 519, 542-543, “separate the teeth”] [Certain]

#### 4. Tofflemire matrix

A Tofflemire matrix can be used for two-surface or larger Class II restorations. Adequate wedging is essential because the circumferential band introduces matrix thickness between the prepared tooth and adjacent tooth. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 588, “A Tofflemire matrix can be used”] [Certain]

#### 5. Customized compound-supported metal matrix

A customized, compound-supported metal matrix is useful for a two-surface posterior composite restoration because only one matrix thickness needs to be compensated for by the wedge, while the matrix can be held closely against the adjacent tooth. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 589, “excellent matrix for a two-surface”] [Certain]

#### 6. Sectional matrix with separation ring

Sectional matrix systems use a contoured matrix with a separation ring. The ring stabilizes the matrix and provides additional tooth separation, facilitating development of a convex proximal contact. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 589, “additional tooth separation”] [Certain]

The system is especially useful when the proximal preparation is relatively wide, although careful positioning is required to avoid matrix deformation. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 589, “matrix band”] [Certain]

#### 7. Polyester strip matrix for anterior teeth

For anterior Class III and IV restorations, a thin polyester strip is commonly used. Because the natural proximal surface is convex, the strip should be contoured before placement so that the composite is moulded into the required contact and proximal contour. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 542, “properly contoured polyester strip”] [Certain]

#### 8. Incremental composite placement and matrix support

In proximal boxes, composite is placed incrementally and the matrix can be held against the adjacent tooth while the proximal increment is cured. This helps maintain matrix adaptation during polymerization. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 597, “holding the matrix against the adjacent tooth”] [Certain]

#### 9. Preservation of natural contact

When a small anterior proximal lesion can be treated without sacrificing the natural contact, preservation of the contact is desirable because recreating a contact with composite is more technically demanding. [SRC: Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, “maintain natural tooth contact”] [Likely]

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

The textbook position is that matrix contour, matrix-tooth adaptation and tooth separation are the central determinants of proximal contact development in direct composite restorations. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 588, “dependent on the contour and position”] [Certain]

The rationale is strong because direct composite does not provide the same lateral condensation effect as amalgam, making the matrix the principal mould for proximal anatomy. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 588, “difficult to obtain good proximal contacts”] [Certain]

## STUDY MODE

### Evolution to the present position

Anterior restorative dentistry has moved from nonadhesive tooth-colored materials such as silicate cement and acrylic resin toward adhesive resin composites and ceramic partial-coverage restorations. Sturdevant notes that composite has largely replaced earlier tooth-colored materials because of improved bonding and physical properties. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 500, “completely replaced silicate cement and acrylic resin”] [Certain]

The modern philosophy is minimally invasive: preserve sound enamel, remove only defective tissue, use adhesive retention rather than unnecessary mechanical extension, and select the least invasive restoration that can provide predictable function and appearance. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 5-6, “more conservative approach”] [Certain]

### 1. Direct resin composite: present mainstay

Direct resin composite is the material of choice for most Class III and Class IV restorations and for esthetically prominent Class V lesions. Its position is based on tooth-colored appearance, adhesive bonding, conservative preparation and the ability to repair or modify the restoration intraorally. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 529-530, “almost all Class III and IV”] [Certain]

Contemporary composites include microfilled, hybrid, flowable, packable and nanofill or related nanostructured systems, with different balances of polishability, strength, viscosity and wear resistance. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 501-502, “flowable, packable, and nanofill”] [Certain]

The clinical technique is highly dependent on moisture control, shade selection before dehydration, adhesive protocol, matrix contour, incremental placement, anatomic layering and finishing. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 529, 541-545, “shade must be selected”] [Certain]

### 2. Conservative preparation philosophy

For small and moderate anterior lesions, modified preparations are generally preferred because the outline is dictated by the defect and unnecessary mechanical retention is avoided. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 538, “most used type of Class III”] [Certain]

Lingual access is preferred when it permits preservation of facial enamel and improves esthetics, although facial access is justified when the lesion or previous restoration is facial or when access from the lingual is destructive. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 530-531, “facial enamel is conserved”] [Certain]

Enamel beveling can improve blending of the restoration with the tooth and increases available enamel surface for bonding, but bevels are avoided where thin composite would be exposed to heavy occlusal contact or where cementum rather than enamel forms the margin. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 535-536, “45 degrees”] [Certain]

### 3. Esthetic layering and optical control

Anterior composite is no longer treated simply as a single opaque filling. Contemporary technique uses different opacity and translucency characteristics to reproduce dentin, enamel, cervical chroma, incisal translucency and internal characterization. [SRC: Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, Chapter 10, “opaque, cervical, body, and translucent shades”] [Certain]

Shade selection should be performed before dehydration because dehydrated teeth become lighter and can lead to an incorrect shade choice. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 529, “before the tooth dehydrates”] [Certain]

### 4. Direct composite veneers and diastema closure

Direct composite can correct discoloration, shape abnormalities, enamel defects and selected diastemata with little or no tooth reduction. [SRC: Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, Chapter 10, “no tooth structure has to be removed”] [Certain]

For diastema closure, contour is critical, especially at the gingival embrasure, and larger spaces may require a combined restorative and orthodontic approach rather than simply adding excessive composite. [SRC: Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry, Chapter 10, “proper tooth contours”] [Certain]

### 5. Indirect ceramic veneers and partial coverage

Ceramic veneers provide high esthetic potential and can be used when greater color stability, surface characteristics or resistance to staining are required. Their success is strongly influenced by preservation of enamel and by the quality of adhesive bonding. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, Chapter 15, “restricted entirely to enamel”] [Certain]

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis found that ceramic veneers bonded predominantly to enamel had approximately 99% survival and success in the included evidence, whereas severe dentin exposure was associated with lower outcomes. The evidence is observational and heterogeneous, so these figures should not be interpreted as a guarantee for an individual patient. [LIT: Al-Nuaimi et al., 2024, doi: 10.1016/j.prosdent.2023.10.031] [Certain]

A 2023 EPA consensus systematic review found high 5-year survival for both anterior full-coverage and partial-coverage restorations, while partial-coverage restorations showed fewer biological complications. [LIT: Hjerppe et al., 2023, doi: 10.1922/EJPRD_2582HJERPPE25] [Certain]

### 6. Anterior restorations in worn dentition

Direct composite has become important for localized anterior tooth wear because it can add lost structure with limited preparation and can be repaired when technical complications occur. [LIT: Rajarajan et al., 2023, doi: 10.3390/dj11110255] [Certain]

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that anterior composite restorations are a reasonable short- to medium-term option for localized tooth wear, while long-term management requires monitoring and possible repair or replacement. [LIT: Aziz and Locke, 2024, doi: 10.1922/EJPRD_2576Aziz12] [Certain]

### 7. Limitations and failure modes

Composite remains technique sensitive because adhesive failure, moisture contamination, polymerization shrinkage, marginal staining, wear, fracture and contour deterioration can compromise results. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 505-507, “effectiveness of generating the bond”] [Certain]

Esthetic failure may occur through color mismatch, marginal staining, surface roughness, loss of anatomy or inappropriate translucency. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 500-502, “discoloration and loss of contour”] [Certain]

Large restorations under high functional stress may require stronger indirect or partial-coverage alternatives rather than indefinite extension of direct composite. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 603-604, “large defects”] [Certain]

Root-surface margins remain challenging because isolation, bonding and polymerization shrinkage are less favorable than when enamel margins are available. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 529-530, “V-shaped gap”] [Certain]

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

The older textbook position that composite is the principal direct material for anterior Class III and IV restorations remains consistent with contemporary evidence-based guidance. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, pp. 529-530, “almost all Class III and IV”] [Certain]

A 2024 evidence-based guideline supports direct composite for anterior and posterior permanent-tooth indications, including cavity Classes I-V, cusp replacement and tooth-shape correction, provided appropriate technique and contamination control are used. [LIT: Wolff et al., 2024, doi: 10.3290/j.jad.b5748881] [Certain]

However, longevity evidence for anterior composite is heterogeneous, particularly for tooth-wear applications, so it is safer to describe direct composite as a conservative and repairable option rather than universally the longest-lasting option. [LIT: Rajarajan et al., 2023, doi: 10.3390/dj11110255] [Certain]

The present status can therefore be summarized as a hierarchy of conservative choices: preventive or nonrestorative management where possible, direct composite for most small to moderate anterior defects, adhesive veneers or partial-coverage ceramics for selected esthetic or structural indications, and full coverage only when remaining tooth structure or functional requirements make it the more predictable option. ### Prevention

Use water or air-water cooling during high-speed cutting. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 29, “air-water spray”] [Certain]

Use light, controlled and intermittent cutting pressure. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 309, “minimal pressure”] [Certain]

Do not desiccate dentin unnecessarily. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry, p. 29, “should not be dehydrated”] [Certain]

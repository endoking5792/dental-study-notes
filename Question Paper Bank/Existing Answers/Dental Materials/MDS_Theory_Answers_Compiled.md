## 3. STUDY MODE

Definition. Dentinal hypersensitivity is a short, sharp dental pain arising from exposed dentin in response to thermal, evaporative, tactile, osmotic or chemical stimuli that cannot be explained by another dental defect or disease. [RECALL] [Certain]

Clinical significance. The condition is most often encountered where dentin or root dentin has become exposed, particularly in cervical areas affected by gingival recession, abrasion, erosion, attrition or periodontal treatment. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “dentin hypersensitivity is a common clinical condition”] [Certain] The clinical problem is not simply the presence of an exposed dentin surface. Patency of dentinal tubules and the hydraulic conductance of dentin are important determinants of symptom intensity. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “greater numbers of patent dentinal tubules”] [Certain]

Etiologic factors. The important predisposing factors include gingival recession with root exposure, periodontal instrumentation or surgery, toothbrush abrasion, erosion from dietary or endogenous acids, attrition and noncarious cervical lesions. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “Identified risk factors for dentinal hypersensitivity”] [Certain] Iatrogenic exposure may follow tooth preparation, finishing, polishing or inadequate sealing of freshly cut dentin. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “seal and desensitize dentin surfaces”] [Likely]

Predisposing factors should be divided into factors that expose dentin and factors that keep the tubules patent. This distinction matters because management is directed either at removing the cause, reducing fluid movement through tubules, or reducing neural responsiveness. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “reducing the permeability of dentinal tubules”] [Certain]

### Mechanism of dentinal hypersensitivity

Hydrodynamic theory. The most widely accepted explanation is Brännström’s hydrodynamic theory. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “predominant hypothesis for dentinal hypersensitivity”] [Certain] When dentinal tubules are open, external stimuli cause movement of dentinal fluid. This rapid fluid movement mechanically distorts structures associated with pulpal sensory nerve endings and produces pain. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “hydrodynamic activation of pulpal nerves”] [Certain]

Cold, air blast and tactile stimuli can produce rapid fluid movement. Evaporation and osmotic stimuli can also alter fluid movement. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “fluid flow is a major stimulus”] [Likely] The pain is characteristically rapid in onset and short in duration, unlike the lingering spontaneous pain more typical of inflammatory pulpal disease. [RECALL] [Likely]

Anatomic basis. Dentinal tubules are more numerous and larger toward the pulp, making deep dentin more permeable than superficial dentin. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “Permeability is not uniform throughout the tooth”] [Certain] Exposure of dentin removes the external seal provided by enamel or cementum and creates a fluid-filled pathway toward the pulp. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “tubules become fluid-filled channels”] [Certain]

Modern refinement. The hydrodynamic theory remains clinically useful, but Seltzer and Bender note that odontoblasts also have sensory functions, so the traditional model involving only fluid movement and nerve endings may require modification. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “odontoblasts also play important roles”] [Certain] This refinement does not remove the practical importance of reducing dentinal fluid movement. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “occlusion of tubules reduces dentin permeability”] [Certain]

### Factors modifying sensitivity

1. Tubule patency and number: hypersensitive dentin tends to have more patent tubules and greater mean tubule diameter than nonsensitive dentin. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “greater numbers of patent dentinal tubules”] [Certain]

2. Remaining dentin thickness: increasing dentin thickness increases the diffusion distance and reduces permeability. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “deep dentin is more permeable”] [Certain]

3. Smear layer and surface deposits: the smear layer and other intratubular deposits reduce permeability by partially or completely occluding tubules. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “smear plugs greatly reduce the permeability”] [Certain]

4. Sclerosis and tertiary dentin: natural mineral deposition can reduce tubular patency and permeability and therefore tends to reduce sensitivity. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “tubules become progressively occluded”] [Certain]

5. Pulpal status: sensitivity must not be assumed to be dentinal hypersensitivity until pulpal and structural disease have been excluded. [RECALL] [Certain]

### Clinical features and diagnosis

History. Ask about the site, duration, precipitating stimulus, intensity, whether the pain is localized, whether it lingers after removal of the stimulus, and whether there is spontaneous or nocturnal pain. [RECALL] [Certain]

Clinical examination. Inspect for gingival recession, exposed root surfaces, cervical abrasion, erosion, attrition, abfraction-like defects, caries, defective restorations, cracks and marginal leakage. [RECALL] [Certain]

Stimulus testing. Air blast is a practical clinical stimulus, and tactile stimulation can be performed with an explorer. Cold testing may be used when pulpal diagnosis is also required. [RECALL] [Certain]

Differential diagnosis. The clinician should exclude dental caries, defective restorations, cracked tooth, traumatic occlusion, reversible or irreversible pulpitis, periodontal pathology and other sources of odontogenic pain before labeling a tooth as dentinally hypersensitive. [RECALL] [Certain]

Pain quality is useful. Dentinal hypersensitivity is usually stimulus dependent and brief, whereas lingering, spontaneous or poorly localized pain should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis. [RECALL] [Likely]

### Management: principles

The management sequence is: identify and eliminate the cause, control plaque and traumatic habits, reduce the permeability of exposed tubules, reduce neural excitability when appropriate, restore structurally defective surfaces when indicated, and reassess the response. [RECALL] [Certain]

The central biological objective is reduction of dentinal fluid movement. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “interventions that reduce fluid flow”] [Certain] The second objective is reduction of neural responsiveness. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “reducing the sensitivity of dentinal neurons”] [Certain]

### 1. Etiologic and preventive management

• Correct toothbrushing technique and reduce excessive brushing force when abrasion is suspected. [RECALL] [Certain]

• Control erosive dietary or gastric acid exposure when relevant and avoid unnecessary acidic challenges. [RECALL] [Likely]

• Manage gingival inflammation and use atraumatic periodontal instrumentation. [RECALL] [Certain]

• Review occlusal factors when cervical defects or symptoms suggest an occlusal contribution. [RECALL] [Likely]

• Avoid unnecessary removal of dentin during restorative procedures. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “preservation of tooth structure”] [Certain]

• Maintain adequate isolation and provide an effective restoration seal when dentin has been cut. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “seal and desensitize dentin surfaces”] [Certain]

### 2. Home-use desensitizing agents

Potassium-containing dentifrices. Potassium nitrate and related potassium salts are intended to reduce excitability of sensory nerves rather than simply plug the tubules. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “Potassium-containing dentifrices have been found”] [Certain] Seltzer and Bender summarize multiple randomized clinical trials supporting an effect of potassium-containing dentifrices, while also noting methodological variation among studies. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “variations in the methodology or design”] [Certain]

Fluoride-containing preparations. Sodium fluoride, stannous fluoride and fluoride-releasing preparations can contribute to desensitization by promoting mineral deposition and tubular occlusion. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “fluoride-releasing resins or varnishes”] [Likely]

Other dentifrice approaches include formulations containing strontium salts and other tubule-occluding or nerve-modulating agents. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “strontium acetate, potassium nitrate and fluoride”] [Certain]

Home therapy is appropriate for mild or generalized sensitivity, provided the diagnosis is secure and the patient is reviewed if symptoms persist. [RECALL] [Certain]

### 3. Professional topical treatment

Fluoride varnishes and other professionally applied fluoride preparations may reduce dentin permeability by encouraging surface or intratubular mineral deposition. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “block fluid flow”] [Likely]

Resin sealing. Dentin bonding agents or low-viscosity resins can seal exposed tubules and reduce permeability. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “dentin-bonding agents may be used”] [Certain] Seltzer and Bender report clinical and experimental evidence that resin application can reduce sensitivity, although the durability depends on persistence of resin tags and the treatment environment. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “resin tags still blocking their dentinal tubules”] [Certain]

Other tubule-occluding approaches described in the source include Gluma-type materials, oxalate salts, cyanoacrylate, fluoride-releasing resins or varnishes and burnishing. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “Examples include the application of materials”] [Certain]

The main limitations are variable duration, recurrence if tubules reopen, and the need to balance occlusion with tissue compatibility. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “longevity of effective blockage”] [Certain]

### 4. Restorative management

Restoration is indicated when sensitivity is associated with a significant cervical defect, loss of tooth structure, esthetic concern, or failure of conservative measures. [RECALL] [Certain]

Adhesive restorative materials can seal exposed dentin and restore lost contour. Glass ionomer materials may be useful in selected cervical lesions because they can bond to tooth structure and function in areas where preparation is minimal. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “Glass-ionomer materials form chemical bonds”] [Certain]

Resin composite can be selected when esthetics, adhesion and adequate isolation are favorable. [RECALL] [Certain]

The restorative interface must be sealed effectively because microleakage and bacterial penetration can maintain pulpal irritation. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Restorations must seal the preparation adequately”] [Certain]

### 5. Periodontal and surgical management

When hypersensitivity is directly related to gingival recession, periodontal management may include root coverage procedures in appropriately selected cases. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “coronally positioned flap”] [Certain]

Surgical management should not be the first response to every sensitive cervical surface. The cause, periodontal condition, defect morphology and response to conservative therapy should guide selection. [RECALL] [Certain]

### 6. Laser therapy

Lasers have been investigated for dentinal hypersensitivity. Proposed mechanisms include tubule occlusion by melting or resolidification of dentin or smear material and possible reduction of neuronal activity. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “lasers may occlude dentinal tubules”] [Certain]

Laser therapy should be regarded as an adjunctive professional option rather than an automatic first-line treatment because the literature contains variation in protocols and comparative effectiveness. [SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “few studies directly compare efficacy”] [Certain]

### Treatment algorithm

[DIAGRAM: Management of dentinal hypersensitivity | Diagnose and exclude pulpal/structural disease → identify etiologic factor → preventive control → home desensitizing agent → professional tubule occlusion/resin seal if persistent → restorative or periodontal treatment when indicated → review | why it earns marks: shows logical clinical decision-making and prevents premature treatment]

[SRC: Seltzer and Bender’s Dental Pulp; phrase: “difficult to determine which”] [Certain]

## 3. STUDY MODE

Retentive pins are auxiliary mechanical retention devices used mainly when inadequate remaining tooth structure prevents adequate retention of a direct restorative material, especially in complex amalgam restorations. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “pin retention is used more frequently”] [Certain]

The three classical types are cemented pins, friction-locked pins and self-threading pins. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Three types of pins”] [Certain]

1. Cemented pins. A pinhole is prepared slightly larger than the pin, and the pin is retained with a dental luting cement. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “They are cemented into pinholes”] [Certain] Retention in dentin is the lowest of the three types. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “cemented pin is the least retentive”] [Certain] They produce less insertion stress than self-threading pins but require cement and are now used less frequently. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Neither the cemented nor friction-locked pins”] [Certain]

2. Friction-locked pins. The pinhole is prepared slightly smaller than the pin diameter, and the pin is tapped into the dentin. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “pins are tapped into place”] [Certain] Retention is produced by elastic deformation and resiliency of dentin. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “retained by the resiliency of the dentin”] [Certain] It is more retentive than the cemented type but less retentive than the self-threading type. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “friction-locked pin is intermediate”] [Certain]

3. Self-threading pins. The pin is threaded into a pinhole of smaller diameter, allowing its threads to engage dentin as it is inserted. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “threads engage the dentin”] [Certain] This is the most retentive of the three types. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “self-threading pin is the most retentive”] [Certain] Its disadvantage is that insertion can generate dentinal stress and craze lines, particularly with larger diameter pins. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Vertical and horizontal stresses can be generated”] [Certain]

Clinical note. Pins should be used sparingly because excessive pin placement can weaken tooth structure or restoration and can risk pulpal penetration or external perforation. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “fewest pins possible should be used”] [Certain] Slot retention may be an alternative in some complex restorations and may create fewer dentinal microfracture risks, although it requires removal of more tooth structure. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Slots are less likely to create microfractures”] [Certain]

Definition. A matrix is a temporary artificial wall used to replace missing tooth structure during placement and contouring of a direct restorative material. [RECALL] [Certain] It is particularly important when a proximal wall is absent because it confines the material, provides a contouring surface and helps establish the proximal contact and embrasure. ### Applications

1. Class II restorations. The matrix replaces the missing proximal wall and allows formation of the proximal contact, contour and embrasure. [SRC: Summitt’s Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “development of an adequate contact”] [Certain]

2. Class III restorations. Strip matrices can confine composite or amalgam at proximal anterior defects and can be supported with compound when required. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Compound matrix ready for insertion”] [Certain]

3. Class V restorations. Most Class V restorations do not require a matrix, but short strips may be used to confine material when the preparation morphology makes condensation difficult. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Most Class V amalgam restorations”] [Certain]

5. Complex coronal defects during endodontic treatment. Matrix barriers can help reconstruct missing walls and permit a preendodontic build-up when conventional band application is difficult. [SRC: Ingle’s Endodontics 7; phrase: “floating sectional matrix held in position”] [Certain]

### Principles of matrix application

## 3. STUDY MODE

Rake angle is an important geometric feature of a bur blade and is related to the relationship between the rake face and the direction of cutting. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “rake angle is the most important design”] [Certain]

1. Positive rake angle. The rake face is oriented to favor cutting, producing a sharper effective cutting action and reducing cutting forces. [SRC: Ingle’s Endodontics 7; phrase: “positive rake angle cut dentin very effectively”] [Certain] Positive rake can increase cutting efficiency but can reduce edge strength and tool life. [SRC: Science of Dental Materials; phrase: “shorter life and also higher temperature rise”] [Certain]

2. Zero or neutral rake angle. The rake angle is approximately zero, producing an intermediate cutting behavior compared with positive and negative configurations. [SRC: Ingle’s Endodontics 7; phrase: “positive, negative, or neutral”] [Certain] Science of Dental Materials describes zero rake as having lower efficiency than positive rake. [SRC: Science of Dental Materials; phrase: “Zero rake angled bur has lower efficiency”] [Certain]

3. Negative rake angle. The rake face lies ahead of the radius from the cutting edge toward the axis of the bur. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “A rake angle is said to be negative”] [Certain] Negative rake reduces cutting efficiency but reinforces the cutting edge, increases resistance to fracture and may improve tool life. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “negative rake angle minimizes fractures”] [Certain]

Clinical relevance. Dental carbide burs commonly have slight negative rake angles. [SRC: Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry; phrase: “Carbide burs normally have blades”] [Certain] Negative rake is useful in dentistry because it provides controlled cutting and a durable edge rather than maximizing aggressive penetration.

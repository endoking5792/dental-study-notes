## QUESTION 1 - "Luting cements." - 10 marks

### STUDY MODE

Luting cements are materials used to fill the microscopic space between an indirect restoration and the prepared tooth, thereby assisting retention, sealing the interface and distributing functional stresses. The classical luting concept was primarily mechanical retention, whereas contemporary adhesive cements can additionally provide chemical and micromechanical bonding to tooth structure and restorative substrates.[1,2]

Figure 1. Schematic of the luting interface. The cement fills microscopic surface irregularities and contributes to retention and interfacial sealing.[1,2]

#### Classification

| Class | Examples | Key clinical feature |
|---|---|---|
| Acid-base cements | Zinc phosphate, zinc polycarboxylate, conventional GIC | Primarily luting by mechanical interlocking; GIC and polycarboxylate can chemically interact with tooth structure. |
| Resin-modified acid-base cements | RMGIC | Improved strength and handling compared with conventional GIC; useful for selected indirect restorations. |
| Resin-based cements | Adhesive, self-adhesive, universal resin cements | Low solubility, high bond strength and esthetic versatility; technique sensitivity varies with system. |
| Temporary cements | ZOE and noneugenol formulations | Retrievable cementation; eugenol-containing products can interfere with resin polymerization. |

#### Requirements of an ideal luting cement

Low film thickness and adequate flow under seating pressure to permit complete seating of the restoration.

Adequate working time and clinically appropriate setting time.

High compressive and flexural strength, adequate fracture toughness and fatigue resistance.

Low solubility and resistance to oral-fluid disintegration.

Dimensional stability and low setting contraction.

Good adhesion or adequate mechanical retention according to the indication.

Biocompatibility with pulp and periodontal tissues.

Radiopacity for postoperative diagnosis.

Acceptable esthetics, particularly for translucent ceramic restorations.

Predictable handling, cleanup and excess-cement removal.

#### Major luting cements

| Cement | Principal advantages | Major limitations / clinical caution |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | Long clinical record, good compressive strength, low film thickness, predictable manipulation. | No chemical adhesion; initially acidic; soluble in oral fluids; retention depends strongly on preparation geometry.[1,2] |
| Zinc polycarboxylate | Chemical interaction with calcium of enamel/dentin; relatively biocompatible. | Lower mechanical properties and higher viscosity than zinc phosphate; retention advantage is limited in ideal preparations.[1,2] |
| GIC | Chemical adhesion, fluoride release, useful translucency and reasonable mechanical properties. | Moisture sensitivity during early setting; acid-base reaction; lower strength than resin cement.[1,2] |
| RMGIC | Improved handling and early strength compared with conventional GIC. | Water sorption and expansion related to resin component; indications depend on restoration and substrate.[1,2] |
| Resin cement | High bond strength, low solubility, high mechanical performance and excellent esthetic potential. | Technique sensitive; polymerization, surface treatment and moisture control are critical.[1,2,10,11] |

#### Clinical selection principle

No luting cement can compensate indefinitely for an inadequate preparation design. The cement becomes especially important when the restoration or preparation has limited conventional retention, when adhesion is required for a minimally retentive ceramic restoration, or when the restorative material requires a specific bonding protocol.[2]

#### Recent advances

Current resin-cement development has moved toward simplified adhesive workflows. A 2023 review proposed distinguishing adhesive resin cements, self-adhesive resin cements and newer 'universal' resin cement systems that can be used in either adhesive or self-adhesive modes depending on the clinical situation.[10] A 2024 systematic review of nine randomized clinical trials found no significant pooled difference in failure rates between self-adhesive resin cements and conventional cements for indirect single-tooth restorations, supporting their use in appropriate cases while not implying that every substrate can be treated identically.[11]

| Examiner takeaway: The best answer is not a catalogue of cements. Start with the definition and purpose, classify them, state ideal requirements, give one or two key properties of each major class, then finish with material-selection principles and contemporary adhesive cements. |

#### Clinical significance

Zinc phosphate provides reliable mechanical retention but does not chemically bond to tooth structure. GIC and polycarboxylate provide chemical interaction with tooth calcium. Modern practice increasingly uses resin-based systems for adhesive ceramic and other minimally retentive restorations. ## QUESTION 2 - "Describe the importance of physical properties of dental cements in clinical practice." - 25 marks

### STUDY MODE

The weakest way to answer this question is to list physical properties without linking each property to a clinical consequence. The scoring value comes from showing the chain: property -> material behavior -> clinical effect -> material-selection implication.[1,2]

Dental cements are used as luting agents, bases, liners, temporary restoratives and endodontic materials. Their performance is therefore governed by a combination of rheological, mechanical, thermal, dimensional, optical and surface-related properties. No single property predicts clinical success; the properties interact with preparation design, restoration geometry, substrate, manipulation and the oral environment.[1,2,3]

#### 1. Flow and viscosity

Viscosity represents resistance to flow. For luting cements, sufficiently low viscosity and favorable rheology permit the cement to spread into microscopic irregularities and allow complete seating of the restoration. Excessive viscosity can prevent complete seating, increase cement-film thickness and alter occlusion. Conversely, excessive flow without adequate body may make handling and excess control difficult.[1,2]

Resin cements can exhibit thixotropic behavior, becoming more flowable under shear during seating. The practical goal is not simply 'lowest viscosity' but adequate flow under the actual seating force and geometry of the restoration.[1]

#### 2. Film thickness

Film thickness is critical because an excessively thick cement layer can prevent complete seating, alter restoration position and potentially increase interfacial stresses. Phillips describes a luting-cement film thickness requirement of less than 25 micrometres under the relevant standard definition.[1] Clinically, the final cement space also depends on restoration fit, seating force, restoration geometry and the rheology of the cement.[1,2]

Importantly, film thickness is not synonymous with cement thickness. Cement thickness is the actual interfacial space and can be influenced by the fit of the restoration and seating conditions.[1]

#### 3. Working time and setting time

Adequate working time permits mixing, loading, seating and initial stabilization before viscosity rises excessively. Setting time determines when the restoration becomes sufficiently stable for cleanup and function. A material that sets too quickly may lead to incomplete seating or excess cement being trapped at the margin, whereas a material that sets too slowly prolongs isolation and cleanup and may expose the interface to contamination.[1,2]

#### 4. Mechanical strength

Compressive strength is important because cement films experience compressive loading beneath restorations. Flexural strength and fracture toughness become important when the cement is exposed at margins or when tensile and shear stresses develop. Fatigue behavior is especially relevant because the clinical environment subjects cements to repeated rather than single static loading.[2]

A cement with high static strength but poor fatigue resistance may perform less predictably than expected from a simple compressive-strength value. Therefore, mechanical testing should be interpreted in the context of restoration design and loading conditions.[2,8]

#### 5. Elastic modulus

Elastic modulus describes the stiffness of the cement. A stiffer cement deforms less under a given stress and can influence stress transfer between the restoration and tooth. However, 'higher modulus is always better' is an oversimplification because stress distribution depends on the modulus of the restoration, tooth substrate, cement thickness and interface.[1,2]

#### 6. Fracture toughness

Fracture toughness is resistance to unstable crack propagation. It becomes particularly relevant for brittle cement systems and cement lines subjected to repeated tensile and shear stresses. A cement with inadequate fracture toughness can crack even when its compressive strength appears acceptable.[2]

#### 7. Solubility and disintegration

Luting cement is exposed to saliva, gingival crevicular fluid and changes in pH. High solubility or disintegration can enlarge marginal defects, expose the interface and promote plaque retention, recurrent caries or loss of retention. This property is therefore particularly important at exposed margins and in areas of moisture challenge.[1,2,7]

The low solubility of resin cements is a major advantage over conventional water-soluble acid-base cements, although resin systems have their own problems such as water sorption, hydrolytic degradation and polymerization-related stresses.[1,2]

#### 8. Dimensional stability and polymerization shrinkage

Setting contraction or polymerization shrinkage can generate interfacial stresses. If shrinkage exceeds the ability of the bonded interface to accommodate the stress, marginal gaps or debonding may result. Conversely, undesirable expansion or water uptake can create stress at restoration interfaces. Dimensional stability must therefore be considered over both the setting period and long-term water exposure.[1,2]

#### 9. Thermal properties

Thermal conductivity determines the rate at which heat is transferred toward the pulp. Metallic restorations conduct heat efficiently, whereas cement bases and nonmetallic restorative materials generally provide greater thermal insulation. In deep cavities, the combined effect of remaining dentin thickness and the thermal properties of the restorative/cement system influences pulpal protection.[1,2]

#### 10. Electrical properties

Electrical resistivity and dielectric behavior are relevant to the ability of materials to insulate against galvanic or electrical stimuli. Their practical significance is greater in certain metallic-restoration situations than in modern adhesive ceramic workflows.[1,2]

#### 11. Water sorption

Water sorption may plasticize resin matrices, alter dimensions, facilitate hydrolytic degradation and change mechanical properties. In resin-modified glass ionomers, water uptake can contribute to hygroscopic expansion. Thus, water interaction can be beneficial in some acid-base systems but detrimental when excessive or uncontrolled.[1,2]

#### 12. Radiopacity

Radiopacity allows the cement line to be distinguished from tooth structure and recurrent caries on radiographs. It also assists detection of voids, excess cement and marginal defects. Adequate radiopacity is particularly valuable in posterior proximal margins and endodontic materials.[1]

#### 13. Optical properties and color stability

For translucent ceramics and veneers, the color, translucency and refractive behavior of the cement can influence the final restoration. Resin cements are therefore available in multiple shades and levels of translucency. Color stability is especially important for esthetic indirect restorations because degradation or staining of the cement may become clinically visible.[1,10]

#### 14. Biocompatibility

Although biocompatibility is not purely a physical property, it must be integrated into cement selection. Luting cements may contact substantial areas of dentin, and early acidity or residual monomer can contribute to pulpal irritation. The risk is influenced by remaining dentin thickness, permeability, cement chemistry and clinical isolation.[2]

#### 15. Adhesion and interfacial behavior

Adhesion is not simply a bulk physical property, but interfacial behavior determines whether the cement can resist displacement and seal the tooth-restoration interface. Resin cements can achieve strong adhesion when the tooth and restorative surfaces are appropriately conditioned. Self-adhesive systems simplify the workflow but may show substrate-dependent differences in bond performance.[1,2,10,11]

| Property | Why it matters clinically | Failure if inadequate |

#### Contemporary evidence

A classic review of luting agents emphasized that material selection must consider biocompatibility, microleakage, strength, solubility, water sorption, adhesion, setting stresses, wear, color stability, radiopacity, film thickness, viscosity and working/setting times rather than any single test result.[7] A later study of contemporary luting agents likewise concluded that mechanical testing must be interpreted together with solubility, film thickness, biocompatibility and handling.[8]

Recent evidence supports a more nuanced view of self-adhesive resin cements. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials found no significant pooled difference in failure rates compared with conventional cements for single-tooth indirect restorations, but this does not establish universal equivalence across all substrates, restoration types or adhesive protocols.[11]

| Critical synthesis: Physical properties are clinically meaningful only when linked to the restoration, tooth substrate, cement thickness, loading and environment. The ideal cement therefore represents a balance of properties rather than the maximum value of any single property. |

### EXAM MODE

Introduction: The clinical performance of a dental cement depends on its interaction with the tooth, restoration, oral environment and loading conditions. Important properties include flow, film thickness, setting characteristics, mechanical properties, solubility, dimensional stability, thermal behavior, radiopacity, optical behavior and interfacial adhesion.[1,2]

#### 1. Flow and viscosity

Adequate flow under seating pressure allows complete seating and intimate adaptation. Excessive viscosity may increase cement-film thickness and prevent complete seating.[1,2]

#### 2. Film thickness

Low film thickness is essential for accurate seating and marginal adaptation. Phillips gives <25 micrometres as the standard requirement for luting-cement film thickness under the specified test.[1]

#### 3. Working and setting time

Adequate working time allows manipulation and seating; controlled setting time permits stabilization and excess removal without prolonged contamination.[1,2]

#### 4. Mechanical properties

Compressive and flexural strength resist functional loading, while fracture toughness and fatigue resistance influence resistance to crack growth and long-term failure.[2]

#### 5. Solubility

Low solubility prevents washout and marginal deterioration in saliva and gingival fluids, reducing the risk of leakage and recurrent caries.[1,2]

#### 6. Dimensional stability

Low setting contraction and controlled water uptake minimize interfacial gaps and stress development.[1,2]

#### 7. Thermal properties

Low thermal conductivity helps reduce thermal transfer toward the pulp, especially when remaining dentin is limited.[1,2]

#### 8. Radiopacity

Allows identification of cement, excess, voids and marginal defects radiographically.[1]

#### 9. Optical properties

Shade and translucency influence the appearance of translucent ceramics and veneers; color stability is therefore important for esthetic restorations.[1,10]

#### 10. Adhesion and biocompatibility

Strong adhesion improves retention and sealing, whereas low pulpal irritation is essential when dentin is thin or permeable.[1,2]

#### Conclusion

Thus, physical properties determine whether a cement can be mixed, seated, set, resist loading, maintain its marginal integrity and remain biologically acceptable. Cement selection should be based on the clinical indication rather than on a single 'best' physical property.[1,2]

## QUESTION 3 - "Composition and properties of resin cements." - 10 marks

### STUDY MODE

Resin cements are polymer-based luting materials designed to bond indirect restorations to tooth structure and, depending on the formulation and surface treatment, to ceramics, composites, metals and zirconia. Their chemistry is related to resin composites but is modified to obtain low film thickness, suitable flow, controlled polymerization and high bond performance.[1,2]

Figure 2. Original schematic of the major functional components of resin cements.[1,2]

#### Composition

| Component | Examples / role |
|---|---|
| Resin matrix | Dimethacrylate monomers such as Bis-GMA and UDMA; diluent monomers such as TEGDMA or related low-viscosity dimethacrylates regulate viscosity.[1,2] |
| Inorganic fillers | Silica, glass and radiopaque fillers; improve mechanical properties, radiopacity and handling.[1,2] |
| Coupling agent | Silane couples inorganic filler to the resin matrix when appropriate.[2] |
| Initiator / activator | Photoinitiator for light cure; chemical redox system for self cure; both for dual cure.[1,2] |
| Functional acidic monomers | Phosphate or carboxylate-containing monomers such as 10-MDP in contemporary systems provide chemical interaction with tooth or restorative substrates.[1,10] |
| Other additives | Inhibitors, pigments, stabilizers, radiopacifiers and viscosity modifiers. |

#### Classification

According to curing mode: light-cure, chemical-cure and dual-cure.

According to adhesive strategy: conventional adhesive resin cements, self-adhesive resin cements and newer universal resin-cement systems.

Light-cured esthetic/veneer cements are particularly useful where adequate light transmission through the restoration is possible.

#### Properties

High bond strength to appropriately treated tooth and restorative surfaces.

Low solubility compared with conventional acid-base luting cements.

High compressive and flexural strength and favorable fracture resistance.

Low film thickness can be achieved when the cement is properly formulated and the restoration is adequately fitted.

Excellent esthetic potential with different shades and translucencies.

Dual-cure systems provide chemical polymerization where light transmission is reduced.

Polymerization shrinkage and shrinkage stress can develop, making adhesive protocol and restoration fit important.

Residual monomer and inadequate conversion can influence biocompatibility and mechanical performance.

Technique sensitivity is greater for multi-step adhesive systems than for simplified self-adhesive systems.

#### Contemporary advances

A 2023 review proposed a contemporary classification separating adhesive, self-adhesive and universal resin cement concepts. It emphasized that newer universal systems can be used in self-adhesive or adhesive modes and may incorporate functional acidic monomers and silane-related chemistry, but additional priming can still be required for specific substrates.[10]

Dental Materials research published in 2024 also demonstrated that the photoinitiator system and filler load can influence the physicochemical and mechanical behavior of light-cured resin cements through lithium disilicate, with ceramic shade and thickness affecting performance. This supports the clinical principle that curing cannot be considered independently of restoration thickness and translucency.[12]

### EXAM MODE

## QUESTION 4 - "Super EBA." - 10 marks

### STUDY MODE

Super EBA is a reinforced zinc oxide-eugenol type cement developed as a root-end filling material. Its historical importance lies in combining the handling characteristics of zinc oxide-eugenol cement with improved strength and reduced solubility by modifying the powder and replacing a substantial proportion of eugenol with ethoxybenzoic acid.[5,13]

Figure 3. Original schematic of the commonly described Super EBA powder-liquid composition. Reported formulations vary slightly between descriptions; the classic formulation is approximately 60% zinc oxide, 34% alumina and 6% natural resin in the powder, with approximately 62.5% ortho-ethoxybenzoic acid and 37.5% eugenol in the liquid.[13]

#### Composition

| Component | Classic formulation / role |
|---|---|
| Powder: zinc oxide | ~60%; principal reactive oxide component. |
| Powder: alumina | ~34%; reinforces the set cement and improves mechanical properties. |
| Powder: natural resin | ~6%; contributes to handling and cement properties. |
| Liquid: ortho-ethoxybenzoic acid | ~62.5%; replaces much of the eugenol and improves mechanical behavior. |
| Liquid: eugenol | ~37.5%; participates in zinc-eugenolate formation and contributes to antimicrobial/biologic behavior, but is also responsible for tissue-irritation concerns. |

#### Setting reaction

The setting process is based on the reaction of zinc oxide with the organic liquid system to form a zinc-organic matrix, conventionally described as zinc-eugenolate modified by ethoxybenzoic acid. Moisture accelerates the setting reaction of zinc oxide-eugenol systems.[5,13]

#### Properties

Good handling and plasticity, allowing placement into a prepared root-end cavity.

Improved compressive strength compared with conventional zinc oxide-eugenol materials.

Better resistance to disintegration than conventional ZOE.

Reasonable adaptation to dentin under suitable microsurgical conditions.

Radiopacity is adequate in commercial formulations.

Good historical clinical performance in endodontic microsurgery.

Not truly adhesive in the modern chemical-bonding sense; adaptation is predominantly mechanical.

Eugenol-related cytotoxicity remains a biological limitation.

It has largely been superseded for many indications by hydraulic calcium silicate materials because of their sealing and biologic advantages.

#### Indications

Root-end filling during endodontic microsurgery.

Selected repair/root-end applications when a suitable handling and ZOE-type material is specifically indicated.

#### Advantages

Easy manipulation

Good plasticity

Adequate working/setting behavior

Higher strength than conventional ZOE

Good historical sealing performance

Better than amalgam in several biological and clinical comparisons

#### Disadvantages

Eugenol-related cytotoxicity

No true modern adhesive bond to dentin

Not bioactive in the same manner as calcium silicate materials

Historical evidence base is less relevant to contemporary microsurgical calcium-silicate practice

#### Contemporary evidence and position

A 2014 retrospective clinical study of microsurgery using SuperEBA as the root-end filling material reported a 93.1% healing rate at 2 years in 101 treated teeth, supporting its clinical effectiveness in experienced microsurgical treatment, although the retrospective design limits causal inference.[14]

A 2022 systematic review and network meta-analysis found MTA to rank highest overall among root-end filling materials at 12 months, while sensitivity analyses still associated SuperEBA with higher success than gutta-percha. This places SuperEBA as a historically effective material but not the contemporary biologic benchmark.[15]

Experimental work has repeatedly shown more favorable periapical tissue response and cementum regeneration with MTA than with SuperEBA, while SuperEBA remained superior to amalgam in several comparisons.[16] Ingle's Endodontics similarly describes MTA as having superior sealing and marginal adaptation compared with SuperEBA.[5]

## QUESTION 5 - "Calcium hydroxide - formulations and mechanism of action." - 25 marks

### STUDY MODE

Calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)2] is a high-pH, alkaline material used extensively in operative dentistry and endodontics as a liner, pulp-capping material and intracanal medicament. Its clinical importance comes primarily from hydroxyl-ion release, antimicrobial activity and its ability to create an environment conducive to hard-tissue repair. However, its high solubility and lack of an intrinsic seal are important limitations.[1,3,5]

#### Basic chemistry

Calcium hydroxide is sparingly soluble in water and dissociates into calcium ions and hydroxyl ions: Ca(OH)2 -> Ca2+ + 2OH-. The high concentration of hydroxyl ions produces an alkaline environment, with pure calcium hydroxide described in endodontic sources as having a pH around 12.5.[5]

Figure 4. Original schematic of the major mechanisms attributed to calcium hydroxide. The reparative response is not explained by calcium ions alone; antimicrobial control, alkaline injury and release of dentin-derived bioactive molecules are important concepts.[1,3,5]

### Formulations

#### A. Chemically setting calcium hydroxide liners

Traditional two-paste systems contain a base paste and catalyst paste. A typical base paste may contain calcium tungstate, tribasic calcium phosphate and zinc oxide suspended in a glycol salicylate vehicle. The catalyst paste may contain calcium hydroxide, zinc oxide and zinc stearate in a resinous salicylate-based liquid. Calcium hydroxide reacts with salicylate to form an amorphous calcium disalicylate matrix, while radiopaque fillers such as calcium tungstate or barium-containing materials contribute radiopacity.[2]

#### B. Light-cured calcium hydroxide liners

Light-cured formulations disperse calcium hydroxide and radiopaque fillers such as barium sulfate in a urethane dimethacrylate resin matrix. They offer improved handling, rapid curing and substantially lower solubility than conventional self-cured calcium hydroxide liners.[2,3]

#### C. Aqueous calcium hydroxide pastes

For endodontic use, calcium hydroxide may be supplied as powder mixed with a vehicle to form a paste. Vehicles can be aqueous, viscous or oily. The vehicle affects ion release, handling, diffusion and the rate at which the paste is removed or replaced.[5]

#### D. Premixed commercial calcium hydroxide pastes

Premixed products may contain calcium hydroxide with water or proprietary aqueous/viscous vehicles and radiopaque additives. Ingle describes Calasept as a premixed formulation containing calcium hydroxide with calcium chloride, sodium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, potassium chloride and sterile water.[5]

### Mechanism of action

#### 1. Antibacterial action

The principal antimicrobial effect is attributed to hydroxyl ions and the resulting high pH. The alkaline environment damages bacterial cytoplasmic membranes, denatures proteins and interferes with essential enzymatic activity. This is clinically relevant for intracanal disinfection and for reducing the bacterial challenge to an injured pulp.[3,5]

#### 2. Neutralization of acidic products

The alkaline material can neutralize acids and reduce the effects of acidic bacterial products. This is one reason calcium hydroxide has historically been used beneath restorations and in deep carious lesions.[1,2]

#### 3. Superficial tissue injury and hard-tissue repair

Direct application to exposed pulp can produce a superficial zone of coagulative necrosis because of the high pH. In the absence of persistent microbial contamination, the underlying tissue can undergo healing with hard-tissue barrier formation.[5]

#### 4. Release of dentin-derived bioactive molecules

An important contemporary interpretation is that calcium hydroxide does not simply 'stimulate' reparative dentin by irritation. Its alkaline action can solubilize matrix-associated bioactive molecules, including growth factors such as TGF-beta1 and bone morphogenetic proteins, which can participate in the reparative response.[3]

#### 5. Effects on mineralization-related enzymes

High pH and calcium availability have been associated experimentally with increased activity of alkaline phosphatase and calcium-dependent pyrophosphatase, processes relevant to mineralization. However, evidence that calcium ions from the dressing directly become the calcium source of the dentin bridge is weak; radiolabeled calcium studies do not establish that exogenous calcium from calcium hydroxide is directly incorporated into newly formed tissue.[5]

#### 6. Intracanal antimicrobial effect

As an intracanal medicament, calcium hydroxide provides a high-pH environment that can act against many endodontic microorganisms. Its effect is not identical against all species, and dentinal buffering and limited penetration into some anatomical niches constrain its antimicrobial performance.[5]

#### 7. Effect on necrotic pulp and apical barrier formation

Traditional apexification with calcium hydroxide can induce formation of a mineralized apical barrier in immature necrotic teeth. However, treatment duration is variable and can extend over months, requiring repeated medicament replacement. Ingle highlights variability, multiple visits, reinfection risk and possible weakening of dentin as important limitations of prolonged calcium hydroxide apexification.[5]

#### Calcium hydroxide versus modern calcium silicate materials

The important contemporary shift is from viewing calcium hydroxide as the universal biologic standard to recognizing the importance of both biologic activity and sealing ability. Hydraulic calcium silicate cements also generate calcium hydroxide during hydration, but they set into a solid mass and can provide a much better physical seal.[3,5]

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis found higher success with MTA than calcium hydroxide for direct pulp capping, while comparisons between MTA and tricalcium silicate cements did not show a significant difference.[17] A 2021 meta-analysis likewise found calcium silicate materials more predictable than calcium hydroxide, although the authors noted substantial risk of bias in the underlying studies.[18]

More recently, a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of five randomized controlled trials involving 552 teeth found hydraulic calcium silicate cements had significantly higher clinical and radiographic success than calcium hydroxide for direct pulp capping, with an odds ratio of 2.68 and moderate certainty of evidence.[19] This supports a contemporary preference for hydraulic calcium silicate materials in many direct pulp-capping situations, while calcium hydroxide remains relevant because of its antimicrobial and biologic effects and its extensive evidence base.

#### Clinical implications

For deep operative lesions, calcium hydroxide should be used selectively rather than routinely. Contemporary operative dentistry emphasizes preservation of sound dentin and use of a stronger overlying base or adhesive restorative system where required. The high solubility and weak mechanical properties of conventional calcium hydroxide liners make them unsuitable as large unsupported bases.[2,3]

When calcium hydroxide is used in a deep preparation or exposure, it should be confined to the area where its biologic effect is required and protected by an appropriate overlying material. Summitt's text notes that high solubility can result in material loss and contamination of subsequent bonding procedures, whereas light-cured products have improved physical properties and reduced solubility.[3]

#### Recent advances

Modern evidence increasingly favors hydraulic calcium silicate cements over calcium hydroxide for direct pulp capping because they combine high-pH biologic activity with a set material that provides a better seal. The 2025 International Endodontic Journal meta-analysis reported significantly greater success with hydraulic calcium silicate cements than calcium hydroxide, with moderate certainty of evidence.[19]

#### Conclusion

Calcium hydroxide remains a biologically active and important dental material, but its mechanism should not be reduced to 'calcium stimulates dentin.' Its major actions are hydroxyl-ion-mediated alkalinity, antimicrobial activity, superficial controlled injury and release of dentin-derived bioactive signals. Its major weakness is the absence of a durable intrinsic seal, which explains the movement toward hydraulic calcium silicate materials for many contemporary vital pulp therapies.[3,5,19]

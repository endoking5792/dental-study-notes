## QUESTION 1 - 25 MARKS

“Merits and demerits of elastomers as impression material for cast restorations.”

### QUESTION-FIRST INTERPRETATION

This is a merits-and-demerits question, so the answer should establish what elastomers are, classify the major groups, explain why their material properties make them suitable for indirect cast restorations, and then critically identify the limitations that can compromise accuracy. A material-by-material comparison earns more marks than an undifferentiated list.

### STUDY MODE

#### Definition and classification

Elastomeric impression materials are synthetic polymer-based impression materials that set by chemical cross-linking to form an elastic, rubber-like network capable of deformation followed by recovery. The major groups are polysulfide, condensation silicone, addition silicone, and polyether. Hydrocolloids are elastic impression materials but are not classified as elastomers in this terminology (Anusavice et al., 2013).

Figure 1. Original examination-oriented classification of elastomeric impression materials, based on the classification framework in Anusavice et al. (2013).

#### Why elastomers are suitable for cast restorations

A definitive impression for an inlay, onlay, crown or fixed prosthesis must reproduce preparation margins, proximal surfaces and undercuts while remaining dimensionally reliable until a cast or die is produced. Elastic recovery allows an elastomer to pass over intraoral undercuts without fracture, while adequate tear resistance helps preserve thin marginal and interproximal areas. The set impression must also remain sufficiently stable during disinfection, storage and cast production (Anusavice et al., 2013; Sakaguchi et al., 2019).

#### Important material properties

| Property | Clinical significance | General comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic recovery | Allows removal over undercuts with minimal permanent deformation. | Addition silicone is generally best; polysulfide is least favorable among the four. |
| Tear strength | Resists tearing at thin margins, interproximal areas and sulcular extensions. | All elastomers markedly outperform hydrocolloids; values vary by consistency. |
| Dimensional stability | Controls change after removal and during storage/pouring. | Addition silicone is generally most stable; condensation silicone and polysulfide are more time sensitive. |
| Wettability | Controls adaptation to moist tooth surfaces and gypsum wetting. | Polyether is intrinsically hydrophilic; hydrophilized PVS improves wetting but does not eliminate field-control requirements. |
| Viscosity/rheology | Determines flow around margins and containment in tray. | Low body records detail; high body supports the impression; shear thinning aids injection. |
| Stiffness | Influences removal from undercuts and cast separation. | Polyether is relatively stiff; polysulfide is more flexible. |
| Working/setting time | Must permit mixing, loading, injection and seating before elastic properties develop. | Material and temperature dependent; manufacturer instructions govern clinical timing. |
| Disinfection compatibility | Must permit microbial control without loss of accuracy or surface detail. | Short, validated exposure is generally preferable; prolonged exposure is risky for hydrophilic materials. |

#### Merits

High accuracy and excellent surface detail reproduction when correctly manipulated.

Elasticity permits reproduction of undercuts and removal from the mouth without the fracture associated with rigid materials.

High tear resistance compared with hydrocolloids, particularly valuable at thin finish-line areas.

Multiple viscosities permit a rational combination of low-viscosity detail material with a more highly filled tray material.

Good dimensional stability is a major advantage of addition silicones and many modern polyethers.

Good elastic recovery reduces permanent distortion during removal, with addition silicone generally demonstrating the most favorable recovery.

Custom trays can produce a controlled and relatively uniform impression-material thickness, reducing dimensional change and improving accuracy, particularly for polysulfide.

Automix and dynamic-mix systems improve proportioning, homogeneity and reduce entrapped air compared with manual mixing.

Modern hydrophilized PVS formulations improve wettability and gypsum handling relative to conventional hydrophobic silicones.

Polyether has intrinsic hydrophilicity and useful detail reproduction in the presence of limited moisture, although clinical isolation remains important.

Elastomeric impressions can be used for gypsum and, with appropriate material selection, epoxy dies and other laboratory replicas.

Addition silicone and polyether impressions can permit more than one cast or die when handling and storage are appropriate (Anusavice et al., 2013; Sakaguchi et al., 2019).

#### Demerits

Elastomers are technique sensitive. Tray selection, adhesive, tissue displacement, moisture control, mixing, seating, setting time, removal and disinfection can all introduce error.

Silicones are intrinsically hydrophobic to varying degrees. Hydrophilized PVS improves wetting but should not be treated as a substitute for field control.

Polyether is relatively stiff. Removal from pronounced undercuts may require greater force, and removal from the cast may fracture weak die segments.

Polysulfide has comparatively slow elastic recovery and greater permanent deformation, and its water by-product contributes to dimensional change.

Condensation silicone releases alcohol as a by-product, producing clinically relevant shrinkage if the impression is not handled promptly.

Addition silicone may release hydrogen gas from secondary reactions. Modern hydrogen scavengers have reduced this problem, but gypsum surface defects can still occur if inappropriate pouring is performed.

Prolonged immersion in disinfectants may alter hydrophilic PVS or polyether through surfactant leaching or water uptake.

Poor tray adhesion can cause impression-to-tray separation and localized distortion.

Late seating of the tray after the material has begun to develop elasticity can produce elastic rebound and an inaccurate, short or narrow die.

Material cost is generally higher than alginate, and elastomeric systems require more material, accessories and operator control.

Latex or sulfur-containing contamination can inhibit polymerization of addition silicone.

#### Material-wise critical comparison

| Material | Principal strengths | Principal weaknesses | Best exam interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polysulfide | Flexible; good tear resistance; relatively easy removal from undercuts. | Water by-product; greater dimensional change; slower recovery; unpleasant odor/taste; manual mixing common. | Useful but largely displaced by PVS and polyether for definitive fixed prosthodontic impressions. |
| Condensation silicone | Good elasticity and detail; useful putty-wash technique. | Alcohol by-product causes shrinkage; hydrophobic; more time sensitive. | Historically important; inferior dimensional stability to addition silicone. |
| Addition silicone (PVS/VPS) | Excellent elastic recovery, dimensional stability, detail, handling; automix widely available. | Hydrophobic chemistry; moisture sensitivity; sulfur inhibition; hydrogen-gas issue addressed by scavengers. | Usually the preferred conventional elastomer for many definitive fixed prosthodontic impressions. |
| Polyether | Excellent wettability, detail reproduction and dimensional stability; hydrophilic. | High stiffness, difficult removal from undercuts, moisture/disinfectant uptake if exposure is prolonged, unpleasant taste in some formulations. | Excellent where moisture control is difficult, but case selection and removal forces matter. |

#### Recent advances

Recent development has focused on reducing the historic trade-off between silicone dimensional stability and polyether wettability. Experimental hydrophilic VPS formulations using modified cross-linking and surfactant strategies have been investigated to improve water absorption behavior and performance during prolonged exposure to aqueous media (Din et al., 2021). Contemporary elastomer research also compares newer generations of PVS, polyether and vinyl-polyether-silicone hybrids using digital measurements of flow, tear strength and hydrophilicity, showing that material performance remains formulation dependent rather than determined solely by the material family (Huettig et al., 2021; Singer et al., 2022).

The larger technological change is the emergence of intraoral scanning, which removes the physical impression-material and cast-production stages. Current evidence does not support the simplistic statement that digital impressions are always more accurate. A 2025 overview of systematic reviews found broadly comparable accuracy and marginal/internal adaptation for many tooth-supported fixed restorations, while conventional impressions retained an advantage in complete-arch fixed prostheses in the reviewed evidence; the methodological quality of many underlying reviews was low (Kaitatzidou et al., 2025).

#### Conclusion

Elastomers remain high-performance definitive impression materials because they combine fine detail reproduction, elasticity, tear resistance and clinically useful dimensional stability. Their principal weakness is not an inherent inability to produce accurate casts but their sensitivity to clinical technique and material-specific behavior. In contemporary practice, addition silicone and polyether dominate conventional elastomeric impression making, while digital scanning increasingly provides an alternative rather than an automatic replacement.

## QUESTION 2 - 25 MARKS

“Discuss critically about Elastomers as impression material for cast restorations.”

### QUESTION-FIRST INTERPRETATION

“Critically” changes the answer from a catalogue of properties into an appraisal. The answer must connect material science to clinical performance, identify limitations and conflicting evidence, compare alternatives, and end with a defensible contemporary position.

### STUDY MODE

#### Conceptual basis

The purpose of a definitive impression is not merely to reproduce a negative image. It must convert the clinical preparation into a stable laboratory replica from which a restoration can be fabricated. Accuracy therefore depends on a chain: tissue exposure and moisture control → material flow and wetting → polymerization → elastic recovery → dimensional stability → disinfection and storage → cast or die production. Failure at any link can negate the favorable intrinsic properties of the elastomer (Anusavice et al., 2013; Sakaguchi et al., 2019).

Figure 2. Original error map showing how clinical and material variables propagate into impression inaccuracy.

#### Critical appraisal by property

| Property | Why it matters | Critical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Determines the geometric fidelity of the negative replica. | Excellent material accuracy does not rescue poor tissue management or tray movement. |
| Elastic recovery | Controls distortion during removal. | High recovery is especially important in undercut-rich dentitions; addition silicone is generally most favorable. |
| Dimensional stability | Determines how long the impression remains usable. | PVS is the benchmark among conventional elastomers, but storage, disinfection and temperature still matter. |
| Wettability | Influences adaptation to moisture and gypsum. | Polyether has intrinsic hydrophilicity; hydrophilized PVS improves wetting, but moisture remains a clinical threat. |
| Viscoelasticity | Makes the material time and strain-rate dependent. | Rapid removal reduces time under deformation and limits permanent set. |
| Stiffness | Improves resistance to deformation but complicates removal. | Polyether demonstrates the classic trade-off between rigidity and removal difficulty. |
| Tear strength | Protects thin marginal regions. | A high value is desirable, but tear strength must be interpreted with sulcus geometry and material thickness. |
| Rheology | Determines flow during syringe injection and tray seating. | Shear thinning supports syringe delivery and monophase approaches; time lag and setting kinetics remain relevant. |

#### Where the classical claims remain strong

Elastic materials are fundamentally better suited than rigid materials for dentate impressions because they can flex around undercuts and recover after removal.

Addition silicone provides an unusually favorable combination of elastic recovery, low permanent deformation and dimensional stability.

Polyether provides strong wettability and detail reproduction and is useful where small amounts of moisture are difficult to eliminate.

Automixing reduces operator-dependent proportioning and mixing variability and generally decreases entrapped air.

Custom trays can reduce the bulk of impression material and make its thickness more uniform.

#### Where classical teaching needs qualification

“Hydrophilic PVS” should not be interpreted as “moisture proof.” Studies summarized in operative dentistry texts show reduced detail reproduction under moisture, and current formulations remain clinically dependent on field control (Roberson et al., 2006; Hilton et al., 2013).

“Dimensionally stable” does not mean dimensionally immutable. Polymerization shrinkage, thermal contraction, viscoelastic recovery, water or disinfectant uptake and plastic deformation remain potential sources of change (Anusavice et al., 2013).

Polyether’s hydrophilicity is a major advantage during impression making, but its stiffness can make removal from severe undercuts and separation from the cast more difficult.

Putty-wash is not automatically more accurate than a well-controlled simultaneous multiple-mix technique. Inadequate wash space or compression of partially set putty can generate distortion.

Digital impressions should not be presented as universally superior. Evidence differs by arch length, indication, scanner, operator, implant geometry and outcome measure.

#### Evidence and contemporary interpretation

A 2021 Dental Materials study explored experimental hydrophilic VPS formulations using a modified cross-linking system and a novel surfactant, illustrating the continuing materials-science goal of improving water interaction without sacrificing the dimensional behavior for which VPS is valued (Din et al., 2021).

A 2024 Journal of Esthetic and Restorative Dentistry in-vitro comparison found that, in a full-arch implant model, conventional A-silicone and polyether demonstrated statistically superior precision to the digital scanners tested, while A-silicone and polyether did not differ significantly from each other. This is important because it directly challenges the assumption that digital acquisition has already made conventional elastomers obsolete for every indication (Palantza et al., 2024).

Conversely, a 2025 overview of systematic reviews found no significant difference in many tooth-supported fixed-restoration accuracy outcomes between digital scans and conventional impressions, but conventional impressions performed better for complete-arch fixed dental prostheses in the reviewed literature. The authors also emphasized that the methodological confidence of most systematic reviews was critically low, so the evidence does not justify an absolute digital-versus-conventional winner (Kaitatzidou et al., 2025).

#### Clinical selection: an evidence-informed position

| Clinical situation | Reasonable conventional choice | Critical reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Single crown / short-span fixed prosthesis | PVS or polyether | Both can be highly accurate if tissue and moisture are controlled. |
| Subgingival margin with controlled tissue | PVS or polyether | The limiting factor is usually sulcular access and hemostasis rather than brand selection. |
| Moisture-prone field | Polyether may offer a wettability advantage | Still requires isolation; hydrophilicity is not permission to ignore blood or saliva. |
| Severe undercuts | Flexible PVS generally easier than stiff polyether | Material stiffness and removal path should be considered before impression making. |
| Multiple-unit / complete-arch conventional impression | PVS or polyether with rigid tray | Tray stability, material thickness and technique become increasingly important. |
| Digital workflow | IOS may be advantageous for appropriate cases | Reduced material-handling error and rapid feedback are advantages, but accuracy is case dependent. |

#### Conclusion

The critical position is that elastomers are not simply “accurate materials.” They are accurate when their chemistry and viscoelastic behavior are matched to a controlled clinical technique. PVS offers the strongest overall conventional combination of dimensional stability and elastic recovery, polyether offers exceptional wettability with greater stiffness, and older polysulfide and condensation silicone systems retain historical and selected clinical relevance but are disadvantaged by time-dependent dimensional change. Digital scanning is now a genuine alternative for many fixed-restorative indications, but current evidence does not support abandoning conventional elastomers indiscriminately.

Suggested answer architecture: 25 marks

Introduction

Elastomeric impression materials are elastic synthetic polymers used to reproduce intraoral structures for indirect restorations. Their performance should be judged by the complete impression system, not by material chemistry alone.

Critical appraisal of important properties

1. Elastic recovery: essential for removal over undercuts; addition silicone generally shows the most favorable recovery.
2. Tear strength: important at thin margins and interproximal areas; elastomers generally outperform hydrocolloids.
3. Dimensional stability: addition silicone is particularly favorable; condensation silicone and polysulfide are more time dependent because of their setting by-products.
4. Hydrophilicity: polyether has intrinsic hydrophilicity; modern PVS can be hydrophilized, but clinical moisture control is still necessary.
5. Stiffness: polyether provides useful rigidity but may complicate removal from severe undercuts.
6. Detail reproduction: all major elastomers can reproduce fine detail when tray design, wash space, tissue management and manipulation are appropriate.
7. Working and setting behavior: even a high-performance material can fail if the tray is seated late or moved during polymerization.

Critical discussion of the four groups

Polysulfide: historically important because of high tear strength and useful working time, but slow recovery and dimensional change limit its current use.
Condensation silicone: easy handling and good elasticity, but alcohol loss makes it less dimensionally stable than addition silicone.
Addition silicone/PVS: usually the preferred conventional elastomer because of excellent detail reproduction, elastic recovery and dimensional stability; hydrophobicity and polymerization inhibition remain relevant limitations.
Polyether: useful hydrophilicity and detail reproduction, but stiffness can be a clinical disadvantage in undercuts and during cast removal.

Why the material alone does not determine accuracy

Accuracy is controlled by a chain of events: rigid tray + effective tray adhesive + adequate tissue displacement + moisture control + correct material volume and wash space + timely seating + complete polymerization + controlled removal + compatible disinfection + appropriate pouring. Failure at any major step can outweigh the intrinsic advantage of the selected material (Anusavice et al., 2013; Sakaguchi et al., 2019).

Evidence and contemporary interpretation

Recent studies show that flowability, tear strength and hydrophilicity vary substantially among individual commercial elastomers, so material-family labels should not be treated as complete predictors of clinical behavior (Huettig et al., 2021; Singer et al., 2022). Conventional elastomers also remain competitive with digital scanning in some clinical situations, particularly extensive complete-arch workflows, so a categorical claim that digital impressions are always superior is not justified (Palantza et al., 2024; Kaitatzidou et al., 2025).

Clinical selection

For most definitive fixed prosthodontic impressions, PVS is a strong general-purpose choice when isolation and tissue management are controlled. Polyether is attractive when wettability is important, but undercuts and stiffness must be considered. Condensation silicone and polysulfide have more restricted contemporary roles because of dimensional and handling limitations.

Conclusion

No elastomer is universally superior. PVS generally provides the best balance of accuracy and dimensional stability, polyether offers useful wetting behavior with greater stiffness, and older materials retain specific advantages but carry greater limitations. Clinical technique remains a major determinant of final cast accuracy.

## QUESTION 3 - 10 MARKS

“Rubber base impression materials in conservative dentistry.”

### QUESTION-FIRST INTERPRETATION

For 10 marks, prioritize definition, classification, composition, setting reaction, properties, manipulation, uses, advantages and disadvantages. Avoid excessive history.

### STUDY MODE

#### Definition

Rubber base impression materials are elastomeric impression materials that form a cross-linked rubber-like network after chemical setting. The traditional rubber base group includes polysulfide, while the broader elastomeric impression category includes polysulfide, condensation silicone, addition silicone and polyether (Anusavice et al., 2013).

#### Classification

Polysulfide, historically termed Thiokol or rubber base.

Silicones: condensation silicone and addition silicone/polyvinyl siloxane.

Polyether.

#### Composition and setting

| Material | Principal chemistry / components | By-product |
|---|---|---|
| Polysulfide | Multifunctional mercaptan polymer + filler + plasticizer; reactor paste contains lead dioxide and modifiers. | Water |
| Condensation silicone | Hydroxyl-terminated polydimethylsiloxane + alkyl silicate + catalyst. | Ethyl alcohol |
| Addition silicone / PVS | Divinyl polysiloxane + polymethylhydrosiloxane + platinum catalyst. | No stoichiometric condensation by-product; hydrogen may arise as a secondary reaction |
| Polyether | Polyether prepolymer with aziridine end groups + initiator/sulfonate ester. | No major volatile condensation by-product in the classic system |

#### Desirable properties

High elastic recovery and low permanent deformation.

High tear resistance at thin margins.

Adequate working time and clinically acceptable setting time.

High detail reproduction.

Dimensional stability until cast or die production.

Controlled viscosity and shear thinning for syringe delivery.

Compatibility with gypsum and appropriate disinfectants.

Biocompatibility and acceptable patient handling characteristics.

#### Advantages

Excellent reproduction of fine detail and undercuts.

Elastic recovery permits removal from dentate arches.

Several viscosities permit multiple clinical techniques.

Modern PVS systems offer excellent dimensional stability and automixing.

Polyether offers high wettability and good detail reproduction.

#### Disadvantages

Technique sensitive.

Moisture can reduce detail reproduction, especially with silicone systems.

Polyether is relatively stiff and may be difficult to remove from undercuts.

Polysulfide and condensation silicone undergo greater dimensional change because of by-product loss.

Disinfection and storage can alter hydrophilic materials if exposure is excessive.

Addition silicone can be inhibited by sulfur-containing contamination.

#### Clinical uses in conservative dentistry

Definitive impressions for cast inlays and onlays.

Final impressions for crowns and fixed partial dentures.

Impressions for complex indirect restorations requiring high detail.

Diagnostic impressions where a dimensionally stable elastomer is preferred over alginate.

Occlusal and bite-registration applications with appropriate formulations.

#### Recent development

Current elastomer research is directed toward improving the water interaction of VPS while preserving dimensional stability. Hydrophilic experimental VPS formulations and vinyl-polyether-silicone hybrids represent attempts to combine the dimensional advantages of silicone with the wetting behavior of polyether (Din et al., 2021; Huettig et al., 2021).

Digital intraoral scanning is increasingly used for indirect restorative workflows and eliminates physical impression material and cast-production stages in appropriate cases. Current evidence, however, remains indication dependent rather than universally favoring digital methods (Palantza et al., 2024; Kaitatzidou et al., 2025).

#### Conclusion

Rubber base and other elastomeric impression materials remain important conventional materials because their elasticity, detail reproduction and dimensional behavior make them suitable for cast restorations. Modern practice is dominated by PVS and polyether, while newer formulations and digital workflows are addressing the limitations of conventional impression making.

## QUESTION 4 - 25 MARKS

“Discuss critically ‘impression procedures for cast restorations.’”

### QUESTION-FIRST INTERPRETATION

The command word is critical and the topic is procedural. Therefore the answer must focus on sequential clinical technique, rationale for each step, sources of error, material selection and contemporary alternatives.

### STUDY MODE

#### Requirements of a definitive impression

Elastic after setting so it can be removed over undercuts.

Adequate tear strength.

Accurate dimensional reproduction and dimensional stability.

Fine detail reproduction at margins and adjacent teeth.

Working and setting times compatible with the clinical sequence.

Biocompatibility.

Ability to undergo disinfection without clinically relevant distortion.

Compatibility with the intended cast or die material.

Figure 3. Original examination-oriented workflow for a definitive elastomeric impression for cast restorations.

#### 1. Preoperative assessment

Assess the preparation, finish-line location, undercuts, adjacent teeth, occlusion and extent of the restoration.

Determine whether a quadrant or full-arch impression is required. Full-arch casts are particularly useful when multiple posterior teeth or a larger portion of the occlusion is being restored (Roberson et al., 2006).

Evaluate periodontal tissues before final impression making. Inflamed or bleeding tissues compromise the impression more fundamentally than minor differences between otherwise acceptable elastomeric materials.

#### 2. Tray selection

The tray must be rigid enough to resist flexure during seating and removal. Custom trays reduce the amount and variability of impression material and therefore can reduce polymerization-related dimensional change. Anusavice et al. describe approximately 2 to 3 mm of controlled material thickness as a useful clinical principle; Sturdevant similarly emphasizes adequate, uniform spacing and rigid tray support (Anusavice et al., 2013; Roberson et al., 2006).

Select a tray that provides adequate extension without impinging on tissues.

Prefer a rigid stock tray or a custom tray when indicated.

Apply the material-specific tray adhesive uniformly and allow the solvent to evaporate before loading.

#### 3. Tissue management and gingival retraction

The impression can only reproduce surfaces that are accessible, clean and controlled. Subgingival margins require lateral displacement of the gingiva and control of sulcular fluid and hemorrhage. Retraction cord is a common method; a single cord may suffice for margins at or slightly above the gingival crest, while a double-cord approach can be used when greater sulcular access and moisture control are required (Anusavice et al., 2013; Roberson et al., 2006).

Achieve profound local anesthesia when appropriate to reduce discomfort and salivary flow.

Use cotton rolls, suction and appropriate cheek/tongue retraction.

Select cord diameter according to sulcus depth and tissue thickness.

Use hemostatic agents judiciously and remove residues that may interfere with the impression material.

Do not begin material mixing until the field is clinically acceptable.

#### 4. Material selection

| Technique | Material arrangement | Key rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple-mix / dual viscosity | Light body around preparation + heavy body in tray | Light body supplies flow/detail; heavy body supports the impression and limits bulk shrinkage. |
| Putty-wash | Putty support + light-body wash | Putty can act as an intraoral custom tray; requires controlled wash space and seating. |
| Monophase | One medium-viscosity material for tray and syringe | Uses shear thinning to permit flow during injection while retaining body in the tray. |

#### 5. Mixing

Elastomers may be hand mixed, statically mixed or dynamically mechanically mixed. Static automixing improves proportioning and homogeneity and reduces air incorporation because the material is blended within the mixing tip rather than spatulated in open air (Anusavice et al., 2013).

Use the manufacturer-supplied matched components.

Do not arbitrarily alter the base-to-catalyst ratio to manipulate setting time.

Keep the dispensing tip embedded in the material when filling the tray to reduce air entrapment.

Observe the material's working time from the start of mixing.

#### 6. Syringe and tray loading

For the multiple-mix technique, the heavy-bodied material is loaded into the adhesive-coated tray while the light-bodied material is injected around the preparation. The syringe tip should remain close to the surface and the preparation should be filled progressively to reduce void formation. The tray is then seated before either material loses its clinically appropriate working time (Roberson et al., 2006).

Inject around the preparation and into the opened sulcus.

Keep the tip in contact with the material being deposited to reduce air entrapment.

Cover adjacent occlusal anatomy with light-body material where indicated.

Seat the tray once and maintain stable pressure without rocking.

#### 7. Seating and polymerization

The impression should remain completely immobile during setting. A critical error is seating after the material has begun to develop substantial elasticity. Compression of partially set material creates elastic rebound after removal and can produce a short, narrow die (Anusavice et al., 2013).

Use a controlled, single seating movement.

Do not repeatedly lift and reseat the tray.

Follow the manufacturer's setting time under the actual intraoral conditions.

An additional short curing interval can be beneficial where clinically appropriate, but the manufacturer's instructions take priority.

#### 8. Removal

After adequate polymerization, first break the peripheral seal if necessary and then remove the impression rapidly in the direction of the planned path of withdrawal. Elastomers are viscoelastic, so prolonged deformation increases permanent set. A quick, firm removal minimizes the duration of stress and therefore reduces distortion (Anusavice et al., 2013).

Do not remove an incompletely set impression.

Break the seal rather than slowly rocking the tray.

Remove with a rapid, controlled snap when the anatomy permits.

Inspect immediately under good illumination and magnification.

#### 9. Inspection and rejection criteria

Continuous, sharp finish-line reproduction.

No voids or tears at critical margins.

No tray show-through where it indicates inadequate material support.

Complete reproduction of preparation and relevant adjacent surfaces.

Stable tray-material bond.

No visible distortion, folds or pull marks.

A defective critical-area impression should generally be remade rather than repaired. Accurate reseating of a partially relieved impression is difficult and can introduce new dimensional error (Anusavice et al., 2013).

#### 10. Disinfection, storage and pouring

After removal, the impression should be rinsed and disinfected with a compatible method for the material. Disinfection exposure must be long enough for the intended antimicrobial effect but not unnecessarily prolonged. PVS and polyether generally tolerate appropriate short exposure, whereas prolonged exposure can affect hydrophilic systems through water uptake or surfactant leaching. Material-specific storage and pouring recommendations should therefore be followed (Anusavice et al., 2013; AlZain, 2020).

#### 11. Cast and die production

The definitive impression is poured with an appropriate high-strength gypsum or other compatible die material. Low-viscosity initial increments should flow into the fine details with minimal air entrapment. For removable dies, the working cast may be produced by a two-pour or split-cast technique depending on the laboratory system (Roberson et al., 2006).

#### Critical appraisal of conventional impression procedures

| Step | Major error | Mechanism of failure | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tray | Flexure | Global distortion during seating | Rigid, well-fitting tray |
| Adhesive | Poor bond | Tray-material separation | Correct material-specific adhesive |
| Tissue | Blood/saliva | Poor wetting and detail loss | Retraction + hemostasis + isolation |
| Mixing | Air/proportion error | Voids or altered setting | Automix where available |
| Injection | Tip too far from surface | Air entrapment | Inject continuously with tip close to surface |
| Seating | Late or rocking seating | Elastic rebound / distortion | Seat within working time and stabilize |
| Removal | Slow teasing | Viscoelastic permanent deformation | Rapid controlled snap |
| Disinfection | Overexposure | Water uptake / surfactant leaching | Validated short exposure |
| Pouring | Delayed or poor stone flow | Dimensional error / bubbles | Prompt, controlled pouring |

#### Recent advances

Automix systems have reduced proportioning and mixing variability, while newer PVS formulations have incorporated surfactants and hydrogen scavengers to address historical hydrophobicity and hydrogen-bubble problems. Research continues to optimize the balance between wettability, flow, tear strength and dimensional stability (Anusavice et al., 2013; Din et al., 2021; Huettig et al., 2021).

Digital impressions are the major contemporary alternative because they replace the material-mixing, setting, removal, disinfection and cast-production stages. A 2024 JERD study nevertheless found conventional A-silicone and polyether more precise than the digital scanners tested in a full-arch implant model, while a 2025 overview found broadly comparable outcomes for many tooth-supported fixed restorations but a possible conventional advantage for complete-arch fixed prostheses (Palantza et al., 2024; Kaitatzidou et al., 2025).

#### Conclusion

A cast restoration is only as accurate as the weakest step in the impression chain. The clinician should therefore select the material and technique according to the preparation, tissue condition, undercuts, arch extent and laboratory workflow, rather than treating the impression material as the sole determinant of accuracy.

EXAM MODE

Suggested answer architecture: 25 marks

Introduction

A definitive impression for a cast restoration is a precision procedure in which the preparation, finish line, adjacent teeth and relevant occlusal surfaces are accurately reproduced for fabrication of the working cast or die. The material is only one component of the accuracy chain.

Requirements

Elasticity; adequate tear strength; accurate surface detail reproduction; dimensional accuracy and stability; acceptable working and setting characteristics; biocompatibility; compatibility with disinfection and cast materials.

Clinical procedure

1. Prepare and evaluate the tooth and soft tissues.
2. Select a rigid stock or custom tray with adequate extension and uniform material space.
3. Apply the correct tray adhesive and allow it to dry.
4. Control gingival tissues, especially for subgingival margins, using appropriate retraction and hemorrhage control.
5. Achieve moisture control before mixing and syringing.
6. Select an appropriate elastomer and viscosity combination.
7. Load the tray while the syringe material is prepared.
8. Syringe low-viscosity material around the preparation, maintaining the tip in contact with the material and avoiding air entrapment.
9. Seat the tray immediately within working time and maintain it immobile until polymerization.
10. Remove with a rapid, controlled movement along the path of withdrawal after adequate setting.
11. Inspect the impression under good illumination and magnification.
12. Rinse and disinfect with a compatible validated protocol.
13. Pour promptly when required by the material and produce the working cast/die using appropriate gypsum and laboratory technique.

Critical technique points

Tray rigidity prevents flexure. Adhesive prevents impression separation from the tray. Adequate wash space prevents excessive distortion or voids. Tissue displacement exposes the finish line. Correct sequencing prevents contamination and loss of working time. Rapid removal minimizes permanent deformation. Defects at critical margins generally justify remaking the impression rather than attempting repair.

Common techniques

Multiple-mix technique: heavy or medium body in the tray with light body around the preparation.
Putty-wash technique: putty provides bulk support and a controlled wash space provides detail reproduction.
Monophase technique: one medium-viscosity material is used for both tray loading and syringing when indicated.

Critical appraisal and errors

Major errors include flexible trays, poor adhesive, inadequate tissue retraction, moisture contamination, insufficient wash space, late seating, tray movement during setting, slow or rocking removal, and failure to inspect the impression. A high-performance material cannot compensate for failure in these procedural steps.

Recent advances

Automix systems improve proportioning and reduce manual mixing variability. Hydrophilized PVS formulations improve wetting, while digital scanning removes several conventional impression stages. Nevertheless, conventional elastomers remain clinically relevant and are not universally inferior to digital scanning (Din et al., 2021; Palantza et al., 2024; Kaitatzidou et al., 2025).

Conclusion

The impression procedure should be understood as a controlled sequence from tissue preparation to cast production. The major determinants of success are a rigid adhesive-supported tray, accurate tissue management, moisture control, correct timing, complete polymerization, controlled removal and appropriate disinfection and pouring.

## QUESTION 5 - 10 MARKS

“Disinfection of impression materials.”

### QUESTION-FIRST INTERPRETATION

For 10 marks, define the problem, state the sequence, classify methods, emphasize elastomer-specific precautions, and finish with the central balance between infection control and dimensional accuracy.

### STUDY MODE

#### Definition and rationale

Disinfection of an impression is the controlled reduction or elimination of pathogenic microorganisms on the impression after removal from the mouth, with the aim of preventing transmission to dental personnel and the laboratory. The challenge is that the impression is itself a precision device: the disinfectant and exposure conditions must not produce clinically important dimensional change, surface-detail loss or alteration of wettability (Sakaguchi et al., 2019; AlZain, 2020).

#### Basic sequence

Figure 4. Original examination-oriented disinfection sequence for impressions.

Remove the impression from the mouth.

Rinse thoroughly under running water to remove saliva, blood and debris.

Shake off excess water without damaging delicate areas.

Apply or immerse in a compatible, validated disinfectant according to manufacturer instructions and the required contact time.

After the required exposure, rinse the impression.

Pour the impression promptly when required by the material, or store under the material-specific conditions.

#### Methods

| Method | Advantages | Limitations / precautions |
|---|---|---|
| Immersion | Uniform contact with disinfectant; reliable coverage. | Hydrophilic materials may absorb water; prolonged exposure can distort some materials. |
| Spray | Uses less disinfectant; may be suitable where immersion is contraindicated. | Coverage may be incomplete; must be performed according to validated protocol. |
| Other physical methods | Investigated include microwave, steam, UV, ozone and electrolyzed water. | Suitability is material and protocol dependent; not a universal replacement for validated chemical disinfection. |

#### Elastomer-specific considerations

| Material | General textbook guidance | Critical point |
|---|---|---|
| Polysulfide and silicone | Short immersion in compatible disinfectants is generally acceptable. | Avoid unnecessarily prolonged exposure; pour as soon as practical. |
| Addition silicone / PVS | Good resistance to short appropriate disinfection; hydrophilic versions need caution with prolonged immersion. | Surfactant leaching can reduce surface hydrophilicity. |
| Polyether | Immerse with caution and use short exposure. | Pronounced hydrophilicity favors water uptake; prolonged exposure can cause dimensional change. |

#### Evidence

The contemporary systematic review and meta-analysis by AlZain evaluated 70 relevant articles and found heterogeneous results across disinfection methods and impression materials. Some studies reported significant changes in dimensional stability, wettability or surface roughness, whereas others found minor or no clinically important effects. The review therefore supports material-specific protocols and standardized research rather than a universal claim that one disinfectant or one method is optimal for all impression materials (AlZain, 2020).

The classic textbook guidance remains clinically useful: condensation silicone, addition silicone and polysulfide can tolerate appropriate short exposure, whereas polyether requires particular caution because of its water affinity. Prolonged immersion may also leach surfactant from hydrophilized PVS and reduce its wettability (Anusavice et al., 2013).

#### Recent practical principle

The most defensible contemporary approach is not “disinfect as long as possible.” It is “use the shortest validated exposure that achieves the required disinfection, using a product and protocol compatible with the specific impression material.” Manufacturer instructions and institutional infection-control policy should take precedence over generic timing statements.

#### Conclusion

Impression disinfection is mandatory for safe transfer of impressions to the laboratory, but it must be performed without compromising the precision of the impression. Rinse first, use a compatible validated disinfectant for the required contact time, rinse again, and pour or store according to the material's dimensional-stability requirements.

Suggested answer architecture: 10 marks

Definition and objectives

Disinfection of an impression is the controlled reduction or elimination of pathogenic microorganisms after removal from the mouth, performed to prevent cross-infection without producing clinically important dimensional or surface changes.

Sequence

1. Remove impression.
2. Rinse thoroughly to remove saliva, blood and debris.
3. Remove excess water without deforming delicate areas.
4. Apply or immerse in a compatible validated disinfectant for the specified contact time.
5. Rinse after disinfection when indicated.
6. Pour promptly or store according to the material manufacturer's instructions.

Methods

Chemical disinfection: immersion or spray using a compatible disinfectant.
Other investigated methods include microwave irradiation, steam, ultraviolet radiation, ozone and electrolyzed oxidizing water, but their effects are material and protocol dependent (AlZain, 2020).

Elastomer-specific precautions

Polysulfide and silicone materials can generally tolerate appropriate short disinfection exposure. Polyether is more susceptible to water uptake and therefore requires particular caution with prolonged aqueous exposure. Hydrophilized PVS may lose surfactant and wettability during prolonged immersion (Anusavice et al., 2013).

Critical point

There is no single universal immersion time that should be applied to every impression material. The disinfectant, concentration, contact time, application method and storage conditions must be compatible with the specific material and manufacturer instructions (AlZain, 2020).

Conclusion

The aim is to achieve adequate infection control while preserving impression accuracy. The examination sequence is: rinse, disinfect with a compatible validated protocol, rinse when indicated, then pour or store according to material-specific dimensional-stability requirements.

### MASTER EXAM SYNTHESIS

For any elastomer question, organize the answer around the sequence: material class → chemistry/setting → property → clinical implication → limitation → technique → contemporary evidence. This prevents the common problem of listing isolated material facts without explaining why they matter clinically.

The single most important critical concept is that impression accuracy is not a property of the material alone. Tray rigidity, tray adhesion, tissue displacement, moisture control, working time, seating, polymerization, removal, disinfection, storage and cast production interact to determine the final die. Modern materials reduce some sources of error, but they do not eliminate the need for disciplined clinical technique.

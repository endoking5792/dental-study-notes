## STUDY MODE (lean, 10 marks)
Most microleakage tests are in vitro. They imitate the oral environment, for example by thermocycling, but cannot reproduce the dynamic pulpo-dentinal complex or plaque (Sikri, 2020, p.444). The textbook groups the methods as follows (Sikri, 2020, p.444):

1. **Penetration:** dye (the commonest; subjective depth scoring; errors from dye particle size, binding to carious dentin and colour instability), chemical tracers (silver precipitate; both reagents must penetrate), radioactive isotopes (very small tracer, subjective and costly), neutron activation (cannot locate leaks), bacterial penetration (qualitative; misses gaps under 0.5–1 µm) (Sikri, 2020, p.445–446).
2. **Air pressure:** bubbles released at the margin under compressed air; tooth preserved; not in vivo (Sikri, 2020, p.446).
3. **Fluid filtration:** water forced through the interface; volume read from an air bubble in a capillary tube; no tracer; pressure hard to standardise (Sikri, 2020, p.446).
4. **Electrochemical:** current across the interface; not for metal restorations; not in vivo (Sikri, 2020, p.447).
5. **Microscopy:** SEM (direct view; preparation artefacts), fluorescent microscopy (UV excitation of dye), optical coherence tomography (gap quantification) (Sikri, 2020, p.447).
6. **Miscellaneous:** artificial caries, pain perception, reverse diffusion (time-dependent), 3D or CBCT analysis, laser microspectral analysis, resin infiltration (Sikri, 2020, p.447–448).

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** Microleakage is tested by methods that show whether a path exists, how much fluid crosses it, or what the interface looks like. Nearly all are in vitro (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

**1. Penetration methods.** Dye penetration is the commonest and uses methylene blue, India ink, fluorescein or similar. Teeth are immersed, sectioned and scored by depth, but scoring is subjective and dye errors are common (Sikri, 2020, p.445). Chemical tracers use a silver precipitate. Radioactive isotopes detect very small leaks but need safety measures. Neutron activation cannot locate leaks. Bacterial penetration is qualitative (Sikri, 2020, p.445–446).

**2. Pressure methods.** The air-pressure test shows bubbles at the margin and keeps the tooth intact but is not used in vivo. The fluid-filtration method measures volume through the gap and needs no tracer (Sikri, 2020, p.446).

**3. Electrochemical.** Current across the interface; unsuitable for metal restorations (Sikri, 2020, p.447).

**4. Microscopy.** SEM gives direct views, FL microscopy uses fluorescent dyes, OCT quantifies gap margins (Sikri, 2020, p.447).

**5. Other methods.** Artificial caries, pain perception, reverse diffusion, 3D/CBCT analysis (Sikri, 2020, p.447–448).

**Methodological review.** Taylor and Lynch (1992, full text) note that electrochemical testing destroys tooth substance and that autoradiographic results are compromised by subjective scoring (full text). They also document wide methodological variation across dye studies (PDF p.5). Kidd (1976, full text) lists artificial caries and scanning electron microscopy among established methods (PDF p.1).

**6. Interpretation.** No single method suits every question. Penetration methods show whether leakage occurs; pressure and fluid methods measure flow; microscopy shows structure. In vitro results rank materials but do not replace clinical follow-up (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Most methods are in vitro; the pulpo-dentinal complex cannot be reproduced (Sikri, 2020, p.444).
- Dye: commonest, subjective scoring, dye-specific errors (Sikri, 2020, p.445).
- Fluid filtration: no tracer (Sikri, 2020, p.446).
- Electrochemical: not for metal (Sikri, 2020, p.447).

## STUDY MODE

### What marginal integrity means
Marginal integrity is the continuity of the seal between a restorative material and the tooth at the cavosurface and internal margins. It is the clinical counterpart of microleakage: a material with good marginal integrity presents a gap too narrow for bacteria, fluids or ions to cross (Sikri, 2020, p.435). No restorative material bonds chemically to tooth structure to form a perfect seal under thermal and mechanical cycling, so every restoration carries a residual gap, at least about 10 µm even with liners or adhesives (Sikri, 2020, p.435). Summitt's makes the same point in clinical terms: a completely closed margin is unattainable, so the aim is a margin the patient can clean and maintain, and 100 µm is the smallest ledge a clinician can detect (Summitt's, PDF p.956).

### Why the gap forms: three material properties
Sikri names three properties that control gap formation (Sikri, 2020, p.436). Other properties (creep, elasticity, fatigue, solubility) contribute less.
1. **Coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE).** Materials that expand and contract differently from enamel (11.4 ×10⁻⁶/°C) and dentin (8.3 ×10⁻⁶/°C) open and close the gap with temperature. Silver amalgam (25.0) and composites (20–25) differ far more than glass-ionomer (11.0) or gold (14.0) (Sikri, 2020, p.436, Table 22.1).
2. **Polymerization shrinkage.** Polymers lose volume as monomer forms chains. Shrinkage pulls material from the wall. Acrylic heat-cure shrinks about 7%; conventional composite 1.5–2.0%; microfilled 1.3–1.5% (Sikri, 2020, p.436, Table 22.2).
3. **Adhesion.** Wetting, surface energy, water and smear layer, and composition all control adhesion (Sikri, 2020, p.436–437).

### Factors that decide whether bacteria get through
Gap size (10–50 µm; even 10 µm admits oral organisms), host defence (dentinal fluid pressure, tubule sclerosis, plasma proteins, smear plugs) and material quality (fluoride release from glass-ionomer; corrosion products from amalgam) (Sikri, 2020, p.438).

### Material by material
- **Amalgam.** A freshly condensed restoration leaves a 10–15 µm gap. It "self-seals" as tin oxide and chloride corrosion products fill the interface, and high-copper alloys corrode more slowly, so they leak for longer (Sikri, 2020, p.438). Amalgam's net dimensional change is small (±0.2% by volume), and ADA Specification No. 1 allows 20 µm/cm (Sikri, 2020, p.438). Spherical alloys leak more than lathe-cut or admixed alloys, and creep below 3% is considered acceptable (Sikri, 2020, p.438–439).
- **Glass-ionomer.** Chemical adhesion through carboxyl–calcium bonds, CTE close to tooth, fluoride release. Its drawbacks are moisture sensitivity for the first 24 hours and desiccation crazing. Resin-modified GIC has sometimes leaked more than chemically cured GIC, because light-cured setting shrinks faster (Sikri, 2020, p.439–440).
- **Composite.** Shrinkage (1.67–5.68% volumetric) and CTE mismatch break the dentin bond first. Enamel bonding gives near-nil leakage. See Q1 for the full composite analysis (Sikri, 2020, p.440–443). Peutzfeldt et al (2024, full text) found dentin bond strength and marginal gap differed significantly between adhesive–composite combinations, with weak clinical correlation (Q1, Claim E). Kidd (1976, full text) reported that an early composite formed a good initial seal that leaked more after temperature cycling (Kidd, 1976, full text).
- **Gold.** Cohesive and malleable, so it burnishes into the margin. Mat and powdered golds are porous; cohesive foil should cover them (Sikri, 2020, p.443).
- **Cast restorations.** Gaps of 10–160 µm are reported, so a luting cement must seal the interface. Gaps over 0.1 mm should not be burnished. Remake the casting instead (Sikri, 2020, p.444).
- **Porcelain.** Bonded with resin cement after acid etching and silanation. The silane bond weakens by hydrolysis within a year. Leakage is most likely at the cement lute and the inlay–tooth interface (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

### Manipulation and operator factors
Poor packing, condensation and insertion increase dimensional change and marginal misfit. Failure to isolate during composite insertion, unburnished cast margins, and surface contamination all open the gap (Sikri, 2020, p.437).

### Prevention in brief
Good adhesion (etch and bond, or a fluid intermediate layer to fill irregularities); material choice matched to the site; conservative design; correct manipulation; delayed finishing; liners and sealants where needed (Sikri, 2020, p.437–444). Detail for composite is in Q1.

### Critical evidence synthesis (textbook-only; literature retrieval pending)
**Claim A: "The smear layer should be left or removed."** The textbook presents both positions. One holds that the intact smear layer blocks bacteria; Pashley (1991), as cited, reports an 86% drop in dentin permeability with the smear layer present. The other holds that the smear layer carries bacteria and that removal improves adaptation. The textbook's own resolution is that the smear layer blocks bacteria but not their products, so a synthetic, sterile, inert smear layer is preferred (Sikri, 2020, p.437).
- EVIDENCE: a permeability figure (86%, Pashley, cited) and a statement that removal improved adaptation.
- INTERPRETATION: the question is unresolved. The textbook itself withholds a verdict.
- LIMITATION: the Pashley figure is secondhand; the primary study is not read. Keep it [RECALL]-tagged until verified.

**Claim B: "Amalgam becomes self-sealing with time."** The textbook says the gap closes as corrosion products accumulate, and that high-copper alloys leak for longer (Sikri, 2020, p.438).
- EVIDENCE: a mechanism (textbook), plus Kidd (1976, review, full text): freshly packed amalgam leaks, and the leakage falls as fillings age; cavity varnish or liners reduce initial leakage (PDF pp.1, 5) [LIT]. No quantified time course is given.
- INTERPRETATION: plausible and consistent with clinical teaching on amalgam margins.
- LIMITATION: labelled [Likely]. A time course needs a primary longitudinal source.

**Claim C: "Resin-modified GIC leaks more than chemically cured GIC."** The textbook reports this and gives a mechanism (rapid light-induced setting contraction), with the water-expansion offset as a partial counterweight (Sikri, 2020, p.440).
- EVIDENCE: a stated finding, no data given.
- INTERPRETATION: a clear, mechanistically explained claim; no contrary view in the chapter.
- LIMITATION: no effect size. Needs a primary study before quantification.

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** Marginal integrity is the continuity of the seal at the restoration margin. No restorative material bonds chemically to tooth structure to form a perfect seal, so every restoration keeps a gap of at least about 10 µm (Sikri, 2020, p.435). The clinical question is whether that gap admits bacteria, fluids and ions.

**1. Properties that open the gap**
- *Thermal expansion.* Mismatch with tooth (11.4 ×10⁻⁶/°C) causes the gap to open and close with temperature. Amalgam (25) and composite (20–25) mismatch far more than glass-ionomer (11) or gold (14) (Sikri, 2020, p.436).
- *Polymerization shrinkage.* Acrylic heat-cure about 7%, conventional composite 1.5–2%. Shrinkage stress breaks the adhesive bond (Sikri, 2020, p.436).
- *Adhesion.* Wetting, surface energy, water and smear layer, and surface roughness determine it (Sikri, 2020, p.436–437).

**2. Factors controlling bacterial entry.** Gap size (10–50 µm), host defences (dentinal fluid, tubule sclerosis, smear plugs) and material quality (fluoride release, corrosion products) (Sikri, 2020, p.438).

**3. Material behaviour**
- *Amalgam* leaves a 10–15 µm gap when fresh, then self-seals with corrosion products. High-copper alloys corrode more slowly and leak for longer (Sikri, 2020, p.438).
- *Glass-ionomer* bonds chemically, has CTE close to tooth, and releases fluoride. It needs moisture control for 24 hours; desiccation causes crazing. Resin-modified types have leaked more than chemically cured types (Sikri, 2020, p.439–440).
- *Composite* leaks mainly at the dentin bond, through shrinkage and CTE mismatch. Enamel bonding gives near-nil leakage (Sikri, 2020, p.440).
- *Gold* adapts by burnishing and is insoluble; cohesive foil seals better than mat or powdered gold (Sikri, 2020, p.443).
- *Cast restorations* leave 10–160 µm gaps; luting cement must seal them; gaps over 0.1 mm mean remaking (Sikri, 2020, p.444).
- *Porcelain* depends on etch, silane and resin cement; the silane bond weakens over a year (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

**4. Operator and manipulation.** Poor packing, condensation and isolation, unburnished margins and surface contamination all open the gap (Sikri, 2020, p.437).

**5. Prevention.** Bond where possible; choose material for the site; conservative design; correct manipulation; delayed finishing; liners or sealants where the margin is at risk (Sikri, 2020, p.437–444).

**6. Interpretation.** Marginal integrity is a balance between material properties and operator control. No material gives a permanent seal. The aim is a gap that bacteria cannot use, and a restoration that the clinician checks and maintains.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Marginal integrity = continuity of the seal; a residual gap of about 10 µm is always present (Sikri, 2020, p.435).
- Three properties: CTE, polymerization shrinkage, adhesion (Sikri, 2020, p.436).
- Bacteria pass a 10 µm gap; the gap is 10–50 µm in practice (Sikri, 2020, p.438).
- Amalgam self-seals with corrosion products; high-copper leaks longer (Sikri, 2020, p.438).
- GIC: moisture for 24 hours; RMGI leaks more than chemical GIC (Sikri, 2020, p.439–440).
- Composite: dentin bond fails first (Sikri, 2020, p.440).
- Cast gap over 0.1 mm: remake, do not burnish (Sikri, 2020, p.444).
- Smear layer: unresolved; keep as a stated disagreement (Sikri, 2020, p.437).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Treating marginal integrity as "no gap". A residual gap is always present; the question is whether it admits bacteria.
2. Quoting amalgam's dimensional change without the unit (ADA 20 µm/cm, not %).
3. Saying glass-ionomer "seals perfectly". It needs 24 hours of moisture protection.
4. Presenting the smear-layer question as settled. The textbook does not settle it.
5. Ignoring the operator. Packing, isolation and finishing are part of marginal integrity.

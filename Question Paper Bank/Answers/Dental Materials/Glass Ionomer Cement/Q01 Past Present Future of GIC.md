## STUDY MODE

### S1. The design problem GIC was invented to solve [basic science]
Every direct restorative has to answer three questions: does it stay on the tooth, does it seal the margin against bacteria, and does it survive load? Before GIC the two acid-base cements each answered half of that. Silicate cement released fluoride and was translucent, and recurrent caries around it was unusually rare, but it dissolved in saliva and had poor strength [SRC 1]. Zinc polycarboxylate (developed in the 1960s) was the first cement with real adhesion to tooth, but its low tensile strength meant the adhesion did not last [SRC 2] and it was too soluble and weak to restore teeth [SRC 3]. Replacing the zinc oxide of polycarboxylate with the ion-leachable glass of silicate cement gave a stronger, less soluble, more translucent material [SRC 4]. That is why GIC is described as combining the adhesion of polycarboxylate with the fluoride release of silicate [SRC 5]. The whole later history of GIC is a series of attempts to keep those two strengths while fixing its two weaknesses, brittleness and water sensitivity.

### S2. PAST: how the material developed

**The dates are not agreed.** Bhat and van Noort give Wilson and Kent, 1972 [SRC 6] [SRC 7]; Powers also gives 1972 [SRC 8]; a DCNA review says 1969 [SRC 9]; Craig's 14th edition says "the 1960s" on one page but "the 1970s" on another [SRC 10] [SRC 11]. The sensible reading is that discovery and first publication fall between 1969 and 1972, and that the 1972 date used in Indian textbooks is the safe one to write. [Likely] First clinical use is attributed to McLean and Wilson in 1975, and the first product was ASPA (aluminosilicate polyacrylate) cement with a citric acid conditioner [SRC 6].

**Why the early cements failed, and what fixed them** (this chain is a good model for a "past" answer):
- The original liquid, a 50% polyacrylic acid solution, gelled within months, probably by hydrogen bonding between chains; copolymers (itaconic, maleic acid) prevent this by steric hindrance [SRC 12] [SRC 13].
- Early formulations used high-fluoride, opaque glasses to get a fast set; tartaric acid let manufacturers use lower-fluoride, more translucent glasses and sharpened the set [SRC 14] [SRC 15].
- Water-hardening ("anhydrous") cements, with freeze-dried polyacid blended in the powder, first appeared commercially in 1981 [SRC 16].
- Early ASPA restorations failed mainly because they were left unsealed, so they took up water or dried out and cracked; properly sealed replacements lasted at least eight years [SRC 17]. Handling, not chemistry, was the lesson.
- Wilson and McLean's 1988 classification (Type I luting, II restorative, III lining/base) is the one still used in Mount's texts [SRC 18].
- Resin-modified GIC (RMGIC) arose in the 1980s, when water-soluble polymer or polymerisable resin was added to the liquid [SRC 19]; dates quoted range from the late 1980s [SRC 20] to the early 1990s [SRC 8].
- High-viscosity GICs (Fuji VIII, IX) arrived in the 1990s for minimal-preparation restorations [SRC 21]; compomers were introduced from about 1995 [SRC 8] and are not glass ionomers [SRC 22]; nano-ionomers (RMGIC with nanomers and nanoclusters) have been available since 2007 [SRC 23].

### S3. PRESENT: chemistry, mechanism and why it behaves as it does

**Composition.** A powder of ion-leachable calcium (or strontium) fluoro-alumino-silicate glass and a liquid that is a 40-55% solution of a 2:1 acrylic/itaconic acid copolymer (or a maleic/acrylic copolymer), with tartaric acid 5-15% [SRC 24] [SRC 25] [SRC 15]. A typical glass (weight %): SiO2 29.0, Al2O3 16.6, CaF2 34.2, Na3AlF6 5.0, AlF3 5.3, AlPO4 9.9 [SRC 26]. The glass is fused (1000-1500 deg C), quenched in water so it cracks, then ground [SRC 27]; particle size runs from 4 to 50 micrometres, finer for luting and lining and coarser for restoratives [SRC 28] [SRC 29]. Fluoride is in the glass as a flux and oxide scavenger, not as part of the matrix [SRC 30]. The glass must be reactive enough: the alumina:silica ratio has to exceed 1:2 by mass for the acid to attack it [SRC 31].

**Setting reaction (three overlapping stages).**
1. Dissolution: the polyacid attacks the glass surface; roughly 20-30% of the glass decomposes, releasing calcium/strontium, aluminium and fluoride ions [SRC 32].
2. Precipitation and gelation: calcium/strontium ions cross-link the polyacid chains first, giving a clinically hard surface within about 4-10 minutes, then aluminium ions cross-link later and form the strong, insoluble matrix [SRC 33] [SRC 34].
3. Maturation: progressive hydration of the matrix salts sharply improves properties over the next day and beyond [SRC 35].
The set cement is unreacted glass cores in a siliceous gel inside a cross-linked polysalt matrix [SRC 36]. About 24% of it is water, some loosely bound (lost easily early on) and some tightly bound (part of the hydration shell) [SRC 37].

**Why water balance matters.** Calcium cross-links form first, are fragile and wash out if the cement takes up water early; aluminium cross-links form later [SRC 34]. Early water uptake dissolves the matrix, early dehydration cracks it. The cement is most vulnerable during Stage 2 and stays vulnerable to water loss for some time afterwards [SRC 38]. Modern auto-cure cements, made with calcium removed from the glass surface, tolerate early water better, but waterproof sealing immediately after placement is still recommended [SRC 39]. This one principle explains almost every handling rule below.

**Adhesion.** The polyacid's carboxyl groups displace phosphate ions from the apatite, each phosphate taking a calcium ion with it, and an ion-enriched interface layer forms between cement and tooth [SRC 40] [SRC 41]. Brief conditioning with 10% polyacrylic acid (10-15 s) removes the smear layer, wets the surface and pre-activates calcium and phosphate ions [SRC 42] [SRC 43]; left longer than about 20 s it begins to demineralise [SRC 42]. The tooth must never be acid-etched before GIC [SRC 44]. **A real disagreement about bond strength:** Mount says the bond strength has not been measured, because the failure is cohesive in the cement, so bond tests actually measure the cement's tensile strength [SRC 45]. Craig's 11th edition gives a dentine bond of 2-3 MPa [SRC 46]. Both statements are in textbooks; the Mount reading is the more careful one. [Likely]

**Fluoride.** Release is high in the first days, falls over the first weeks and settles by 2-3 months at a low steady level that continues for years [SRC 47]; the cement takes fluoride back up from toothpaste or topical agents and so acts as a reservoir [SRC 48] [SRC 49]. Summitt's key point is that the ability to be recharged matters more than the size of the initial release [SRC 50], and that GICs and RMGICs recharge best [SRC 51]. Release is higher at lower pH [SRC 52]. Acidic topical fluorides (APF) can roughen and degrade the cement [SRC 53].

**Properties, in numbers.** Seven-day strengths (MPa) in Mount & Hume's Table 9.2, with large variation between products: Type II.1 compressive 70-220, diametral tensile 12-20; Type II.2 compressive 140-220; Type I luting compressive 70-150 [SRC 54]. ISO 9917 minimums: compressive strength 130 MPa for restoratives and 70 MPa for luting and lining; film thickness 25 micrometres for luting; setting time 2-6 min for restoratives; acid erosion 0.05 mm/h [SRC 55]. Flexural strength is only 15-20 MPa against more than 70 MPa for composites [SRC 56]. Thermal expansion is close to tooth (10-11 against 11.4), which is why the cement stays bonded through temperature change [SRC 57]. In one reported comparison, one-year wear was 73 micrometres for GIC against under 10 micrometres per year for composites [SRC 58]. In practice GIC is not for marginal ridges, incisal corners or sole support of a crown [SRC 59].

**Biocompatibility.** Fresh cement is very acidic (pH about 0.9-1.6) but dentine buffers it, and the pulp response is mild and resolves in 10-20 days as the pH rises within the first hour [SRC 60]. Poly(alkenoic) acid chains are large and do not readily enter the tubules [SRC 61]. Pulpal inflammation is driven by bacteria rather than by the cement, so a complete seal is what protects the pulp [SRC 62]. Direct contact with an exposed pulp is different: without intervening dentine GIC is quite toxic to exposed pulp, so calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material goes first [SRC 63]. RMGIC tends to be more cytotoxic than conventional GIC [SRC 64], consistent with HEMA release in laboratory studies [LIT 65] [Likely]. Post-cementation sensitivity with GIC luting is no worse than with zinc phosphate [SRC 66].

### S4. PRESENT: classification and which type for which job [clinical decision-making]

| Type (Wilson & McLean 1988, Mount) | Use | Powder:liquid | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| I luting | crowns, bridges, inlays, orthodontic bands | about 1.5:1 | film thickness 25 micrometres or less, fast set [SRC 67] |
| II.1 restorative, aesthetic | any aesthetic restoration without undue load | 2.8:1 to 6.8:1 | auto-cure or dual-cure [SRC 68] |
| II.2 restorative, reinforced | non-aesthetic use needing fast set and strength | 3:1 to 4:1 | metal-modified or high-strength [SRC 69] |
| III lining/base | lining 1.5:1; base or dentine substitute 3:1 or more | | only difference between lining and base is the P/L ratio [SRC 70] [SRC 71] |

Phillips uses the same idea with different labels (Type I luting; IIa aesthetic restorative; IIb reinforced restorative; III lining/base) [SRC 72], and Bhat adds fissure sealant, orthodontic cement and core build-up types [SRC 73].

**Decision logic** used in Mount's system: GIC is the material of choice where the lesion is not under heavy occlusal load [SRC 74]; for cervical (Site 3) lesions a Type II.1 cement, auto-cure or resin-modified depending on light access [SRC 75]; for proximal tunnels, a radiopaque high-strength auto-cure cement [SRC 74]. Where load is high, use a composite or amalgam laminate over a GIC base [SRC 76].

### S5. PRESENT: modified materials and what is, and is not, a glass ionomer
- **Metal-reinforced:** silver cermet (about 40% by weight micro-fine silver sintered to glass, with up to 5% titanium dioxide to restore colour) and amalgam-alloy admixes. They improve abrasion resistance but not other properties and may reduce the ion-exchange adhesion [SRC 77] [SRC 78]. They are no longer popular [SRC 79].
- **Resin-modified GIC:** liquid with 15-25% resin (HEMA and other methacrylates) [SRC 80]; sets partly by acid-base reaction and partly by light and chemical polymerisation, and still sets in the dark [SRC 81] [SRC 82]. Gains: immediate set on light, higher strength. In one manufacturer comparison, setting time fell from 4 min to 20 s and diametral tensile strength rose from 16 to 37 MPa [SRC 83]. Costs: residual HEMA of 4.5% to possibly 15% [SRC 84], water uptake and swelling [SRC 85], and (some sources) polymerisation shrinkage of 3-4% [SRC 86]. Mount's counter-point: acid-base shrinkage is small and the water uptake offsets it [SRC 87].
- **High-viscosity GIC:** finer glass (about 3 micrometres) and polyacid added to the powder give amalgam-like handling; strength changes little but abrasion resistance seems better [SRC 88]. Used for ART [SRC 21]; not for load-bearing areas of permanent teeth [SRC 89].
- **Nano-ionomer:** nanoscale fillers in an RMGIC to improve polish and optics; data are sparse and the benefits are laboratory claims [SRC 90].
- **Not glass ionomers:** compomers (will not set without light; no acid-base dark set) [SRC 22] [SRC 91]; giomers (pre-reacted glass-ionomer particles in a resin matrix, so no ion exchange) [SRC 92]. A simple clinical test: a true GIC reaches a reasonable set within about 7-10 minutes under a light-proof cover, while imitation materials stay soft or rubbery [SRC 93] [SRC 94]. (Sources disagree on 5-7 versus 10 minutes: [SRC 95].) [Likely]

### S6. PRESENT: technique, because it decides outcome [treatment]
1. Isolate, clean the surface (pumice and water), condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 s, wash, dry lightly without dehydrating [SRC 96].
2. Use a capsule if possible: standardised P/L ratio and mixing; hand mixing is possible but powder content varies [SRC 97]. Mix for about 10 seconds; placement must be in the working window, judged by loss of gloss [SRC 98] [SRC 99].
3. Place under a matrix with pressure [SRC 100].
4. **Seal at once** with a single-component, very low-viscosity light-cured unfilled resin; a varnish is less ideal because the solvent leaves porosity [SRC 101] [SRC 102].
5. Delay final finishing, ideally 24 hours (a week for best translucency) [SRC 103]; fast-set Type II.2 can be finished from about 6 minutes [SRC 104].
**Cavity design.** Because the bond is chemical, the preparation can be minimal: no mechanical undercuts [SRC 105]. For Class V, a butt joint (cavosurface margin near 90 degrees) with about 1 mm minimum depth, no bevel, because GIC is brittle and needs bulk [SRC 106]. Minor undercuts left by caries removal can be filled with GIC rather than cutting more tooth [SRC 107].
**Sandwich (bi-layered) technique.** GIC replaces dentine, composite replaces enamel. Mount's logic: composite shrinks and its dentine bond is uncertain, whereas GIC bonds dentine reliably and, being water-based, does not hydrolyse [SRC 108] [SRC 109]. Closed sandwich covers the GIC with composite; open sandwich leaves GIC exposed at the gingival margin to release fluoride [SRC 110] [SRC 111]. Cement must be at least 1 mm thick at the highest P/L available [SRC 110].

### S7. PRESENT: outcome and prognosis
Failures are mostly handling failures: ditching from a low-powder mix or early water contact, cracks from dehydration, bulk fracture from overload [SRC 112]. Reported figures vary with the study: in Summitt's summary, Class I and Class II GIC restorations showed 8% and 40% failure at 24 months [SRC 58]; a Class V study showed 80% retention at 10 years with no mechanical retention [SRC 113]; GIC survives better than amalgam in irradiated xerostomic patients without topical fluoride, but not with it [SRC 114]. Randomised-trial evidence is set out in the synthesis below.

### S8. FUTURE: where the field is going
- **Aim of research:** higher fracture resistance without losing the ion-exchange adhesion and fluoride release, which are the whole point of the material [SRC 115] [SRC 116].
- **Antibacterial additives** (chlorhexidine, chitosan) and remineralising agents (CPP-ACP): results are equivocal [SRC 117] [SRC 118]; metallic nano-additives (silver, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) improve antibacterial action in laboratory work, but clinical benefit is unproven [LIT 119] [LIT 120]; hydroxyapatite-modified GIC is promising in the laboratory [LIT 121].
- **Ion-releasing resin restoratives (alkasite, bioactive resins)** are the new competitors, not true GICs [LIT 122] [LIT 123].
- **Glass hybrid (EQUIA-type)** systems pair high-viscosity GIC with a resin coat, with 5-year survival comparable to composite in Class II cavities [LIT 124].
- **Predicted but unverified:** the intended direction is bioactivity with composite-level strength. [Guessing]


## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
Four claims where textbook statements and published evidence do not line up. Study types are named because laboratory data cannot be read as clinical proof.

### Claim 1. Does the fluoride a GIC releases prevent recurrent (secondary) caries?
- **Textbook position.** Mount's Atlas says chemical union plus fluoride gives "almost total prevention of recurrent caries" [SRC 125]. More cautious texts call it "a matter of some debate" [SRC 126] or "disputed", citing retrospective and heterogeneous studies [SRC 127]. Summitt describes the evidence as "modest but growing" and notes that in two retrospective reports GIC developed recurrent caries within 5 years and almost half of GIC restorations were replaced [SRC 128] [SRC 129].
- **Retrieved evidence.** A meta-analysis of 64 RCTs (8310 GIC against 5857 amalgam or composite restorations, 1-10 years) found secondary caries was lower with GIC than amalgam (primary teeth RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.41-0.72; permanent teeth RR 0.20, 0.11-0.38) but not lower than composite (primary RR 0.92, 0.77-1.10; permanent RR 0.77, 0.39-1.51) [LIT 130]. A second meta-analysis of 40 RCTs pooled bioactive materials against composite and amalgam and found RR 0.55 (0.46-0.65) with moderate certainty, GICs alone RR 0.36 and giomers no benefit (RR 1.09) [LIT 131]. The ADA evidence review summarised by Summitt reached 65% lower chance of recurrent caries against amalgam in permanent teeth [SRC 132]. In the laboratory, GICs still released the most fluoride of all direct materials [LIT 133], and GI sealants showed a burst in the first 24-48 hours then slower release and better recharge than resin sealants [LIT 134].
- **Interpretation.** The advantage over amalgam is consistent across sources. The Atlas's "almost total prevention" is not supported, because there is no advantage over composite in the RCT meta-analysis. The bioactive-material benefit depends on the comparator: the pooled control group mixes amalgam and composite, so the amalgam contrast can drive it (this is a reviewer's inference, not stated in the abstract). [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Abstract-level reading only. The fluoride data are in vitro; no study isolated fluoride release as the mechanism. Certainty for Ge 2023 is not stated in the abstract.
- **Conclusion and implication.** What can be concluded: GIC restorations have fewer secondary caries than amalgam; fluoride release is highest in GICs in the laboratory. What cannot: that fluoride release itself is the reason, or that GIC beats composite. Clinical use: pick GIC for high-caries-risk or moisture-compromised situations, and do not promise caries-free margins.

### Claim 2. How long do GIC and high-viscosity (ART) restorations last?
- **Textbook position.** Mount, Summitt and van Noort all restrict GIC to areas without heavy occlusal load; high-viscosity GICs are "still inferior to composite" in wear and not for load-bearing permanent areas [SRC 89]; van Noort does not recommend GIC for adult Class II [SRC 88]. A DCNA review notes high-viscosity GI Class II restorations were satisfactory at 3 years despite many failures, and cites a retrospective failure rate of 60% at 72 months [SRC 135].
- **Retrieved evidence.** A systematic review of 14 RCTs (only 2 at low risk of bias) found no significant difference in survival between ART/high-viscosity GIC and traditional restorations for single- and multiple-surface cavities in primary molars and single-surface cavities in permanent posterior teeth at 1, 2, 3 and 5 years; at 4.3 and 6.3 years the difference favoured ART/HVGIC; no significant difference for multiple-surface permanent restorations [LIT 136]. A second review (67 articles, non-randomised designs included) found the conventional technique better than ART for single-surface occlusal and multi-surface GIC restorations, with higher annual failure rates for multi-surface restorations [LIT 137]. An umbrella review of 13 reviews graded the evidence "conflicting" and the medium- and long-term performance "still questionable" [LIT 138]. In a 5-year randomised trial of two-surface Class II molar restorations, glass hybrid and nano-hybrid composite had the same survival (94.5% against 94.4%) although the success rate was numerically lower for glass hybrid (81.9% against 90.7%) [LIT 124]; in molar-incisor hypomineralisation molars, 5-year survival was 59.2% for glass hybrid against 75.5% for fibre-reinforced composite [LIT 139].
- **Interpretation.** The textbooks' caution is partly refined, not overturned: modern high-viscosity and glass-hybrid materials do acceptably in single-surface cavities and primary teeth, and in one good trial in moderate Class II cavities. The reviews disagree on ART versus conventional technique, and the textbooks' blanket exclusion of Class II is too strong for the newer materials. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Most trials are small or at high risk of bias; review abstracts do not report certainty ratings; the MIH result is population-specific.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Can conclude: no proven survival penalty for ART/HVGIC in single-surface and primary-tooth cavities up to 5 years. Cannot conclude equivalence for multi-surface permanent-tooth restorations. Clinical use: reserve GIC for single-surface, low-load or high-risk situations; use composite where load is high.

### Claim 3. Does the sandwich (bi-layered) technique improve restoration success?
- **Textbook position.** Mount strongly recommends a GIC base under composite where a margin lies on dentine, citing composite shrinkage and an uncertain dentine bond [SRC 108] [SRC 140], while admitting early sandwich studies showed more bulk fractures [SRC 141]. A DCNA article states routine GI liners under composite are not evidence-supported [SRC 142]. Summitt reports an open sandwich with RMGI successful at 5 years in moderate-to-high caries-risk patients, but a 9-year compomer trial found no difference in secondary caries [SRC 143] [SRC 144].
- **Retrieved evidence.** A meta-analysis of RCTs in non-carious cervical lesions found only loss of retention differed: lower with the sandwich technique at 3 years (RR 7.5, 95% CI 2.1-27.2), but the review contained only 4 studies, all at unclear risk of bias [LIT 145]. An umbrella review also states that the sandwich technique with RMGIC bases improved retention in non-carious cervical lesions [LIT 146]. Laboratory reviews show composite-to-GIC bonding depends on surface treatment [LIT 147].
- **Interpretation.** Mount's rationale is supported only for cervical lesions and only for retention, on thin evidence. For Class I and II, and for open versus closed sandwich, no trial evidence was found in this retrieval. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Wide confidence interval; the exact figure should not be quoted as a precise effect.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Use the sandwich technique where dentine margins or caries risk make composite bonding unreliable, and say that the evidence is limited.

### Claim 4. Are the new ion-releasing and bioactive materials better than GIC?
- **Textbook position.** Mount judges antibacterial and remineralising additives "equivocal" in value [SRC 117] and nano-ionomer claims are laboratory-only [SRC 90].
- **Retrieved evidence.** In a meta-analysis of clinical studies, alkasite materials performed comparably to resin composite and "superior" to GIC, with no pooled numbers in the abstract and moderate risk of bias in several studies [LIT 122]. The laboratory network meta-analysis found alkasite properties similar to composite and stronger than GIC, while GICs still released the most fluoride [LIT 133]. One small non-randomised pilot (36 restorations, 6 months) reported dislodgement in 1 of 28 alkasite against 6 of 8 Fuji IX restorations [LIT 148]. ACTIVA BioACTIVE in primary molars performed comparably to composite and compomer at 12-24 months [LIT 123].
- **Interpretation.** Resin-based ion-releasing materials win on strength; GIC wins on fluoride release. Bioactivity has not been shown to give a clinical caries-preventive advantage. [Likely]
- **Limitation.** Short follow-up, small trials; the pilot is hypothesis-generating only.
- **Conclusion and implication.** Alkasite and bioactive resins are credible alternatives where strength matters; none replaces GIC's chemical adhesion to dentine.

### 1. Introduction
- **Definition.** A water-based cement formed by acid-base reaction between a polyalkenoic acid and an ion-leachable, fluoride-containing calcium or strontium aluminosilicate glass; official ISO name "glass polyalkenoate cement" [SRC 24] [SRC 149].
- **Credit.** Wilson and Kent, early 1970s (1972 in most textbooks; some give 1969) [SRC 6] [SRC 9] [Likely].
- **Parentage.** Glass from silicate cement (fluoride, translucency) plus polyacid from zinc polycarboxylate (adhesion) [SRC 5].
- **Key claims to fame.** Chemical bond to enamel and dentine, fluoride release and uptake, biocompatibility; weakness is brittleness and water sensitivity [SRC 150] [SRC 151].

### 2. PAST: historical evolution
| Period | Event |
|---|---|
| Before 1970 | Silicate cement: fluoride and translucency, but soluble [SRC 1]. Polycarboxylate (1960s): first adhesive cement, but weak [SRC 2]. |
| 1969-1972 | Wilson and Kent: ASPA cement (aluminosilicate polyacrylate) [SRC 6] |
| 1975 | First clinical use reported by McLean and Wilson [SRC 6] |
| 1970s | Liquid gelled; copolymers (itaconic, maleic) and tartaric acid solved storage and set [SRC 12] [SRC 13] [SRC 14] |
| 1981 | Water-hardening ("anhydrous") cements marketed [SRC 16] |
| 1988 | Wilson and McLean classification: Types I, II, III [SRC 18] |
| 1980s-early 1990s | Resin-modified GIC [SRC 19] [SRC 8] |
| 1990s | High-viscosity GIC (Fuji VIII, IX) for ART [SRC 21]; compomers (about 1995), not true GIC [SRC 8] [SRC 22] |
| 2007 | Nano-ionomer [SRC 23] |
| Today | Glass hybrid systems; ion-releasing resins (alkasite, bioactive) [LIT 122] |
- **Lesson of the early years:** most early failures were unsealed restorations that took up water or dried out and cracked [SRC 17].
- **A useful observation (Mount and Hume):** while dentine bonding agents went through eight "generations", glass ionomer chemical adhesion stayed at one generation and was reliable throughout [SRC 152]. GIC derivatives have also been used as bone replacement in surgery [SRC 153].

### 3. PRESENT: composition and manufacture
**Powder:** ion-leachable fluoro-alumino-silicate glass; typical weight %: SiO2 29.0, Al2O3 16.6, CaF2 34.2, Na3AlF6 5.0, AlF3 5.3, AlPO4 9.9 [SRC 26].
- Silica gives translucency and hardness; alumina forms the polyacrylate matrix; fluorides act as flux and are released [SRC 154].
- Strontium or lanthanum add radiopacity [SRC 155].
**Liquid:** 40-55% aqueous 2:1 acrylic/itaconic acid copolymer (or maleic/acrylic) [SRC 25]; tartaric acid 5-15% speeds the set and sharpens it [SRC 15].
**Manufacture:** ingredients fused at 1000-1500 deg C, plunged into water, ground [SRC 27]; particle size 4-50 micrometres: finer for luting and lining, coarser for restoratives [SRC 28] [SRC 29]; glass reactivity is controlled by fusion temperature and heat history [SRC 156].
**Why each component is there.** Tartaric acid does two things: it first binds released calcium (extending working time) and then speeds aluminium cross-linking, so the set is sharper [SRC 157]. The alumina:silica ratio of the glass is higher than in silicate cement because polyacrylic acid is a much weaker acid than phosphoric acid; the cost is a shorter working time [SRC 157]. Polyacid molecular weight and concentration raise strength but also viscosity, which is why dehydrated polyacid in the powder became popular [SRC 158].
**Presentations:** powder and liquid; water-hardening (polyacid dried into powder, water or dilute tartaric acid as liquid); capsules; paste-paste [SRC 159] [SRC 160].

### 4. Setting reaction
1. **Dissolution:** acid attacks the glass; about 20-30% decomposes, releasing Ca/Sr, Al and F ions [SRC 32].
2. **Gelation:** Ca/Sr ions cross-link polyacid chains (early set, fragile, water-soluble); initial hard set at about 4-10 min [SRC 33] [SRC 34].
3. **Hardening/maturation:** Al ions cross-link slowly (strong, insoluble); hydration of the matrix continues for 24 hours and longer [SRC 35] [SRC 33].
- **Set structure:** unreacted glass cores, siliceous gel, polysalt matrix; about 24% water, loosely and tightly bound [SRC 36] [SRC 37].
- **Water balance:** early water uptake dissolves the matrix; early drying cracks it [SRC 38] [SRC 161].
- **Resin-modified GIC:** acid-base plus light/chemical polymerisation; the material hardens on light but full set takes days [SRC 162] [SRC 163].
**Factors that alter the rate of set:** glass composition and fusion temperature, particle size, tartaric acid content, powder:liquid ratio and mixing temperature; a chilled slab and powder lengthen the working time by up to 25%, but the liquid must never be refrigerated because it can gel [SRC 164] [SRC 165]. High ambient temperature speeds the reaction [SRC 166].

### 5. Properties
**(a) Adhesion.** Ionic exchange with apatite: carboxyl groups displace phosphate; an ion-enriched layer forms [SRC 40] [SRC 41]. Condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 s; do not acid-etch [SRC 42] [SRC 44]. Failure is cohesive, within the cement, so the stronger the cement the better the "bond" [SRC 45].
- **Why polyacrylic acid is the conditioner:** it is the cement's own acid, so residue does not interfere with setting; it lowers the surface energy of tooth and improves wetting; it is mild and does not unduly open tubules, because plugs remain after 10 seconds [SRC 43] [SRC 167].

**(b) Fluoride.** High release for the first weeks, falling to a low steady level over 2-3 months and continuing for years [SRC 47]; recharged from toothpaste and topical fluoride, so it is a reservoir [SRC 48] [SRC 51]. Clinical value is debated (see section 10). For remineralisation to start, salivary fluoride should reach at least 10 ppm, a level GICs can provide [SRC 168]; Summitt recommends long-term release of at least 2-3 micrograms per mL per day, achievable only with supplemental fluoride recharge [SRC 169]. Fluoride also suppresses *Streptococcus mutans* growth on the cement surface [SRC 170].
**(c) Biological.** Fresh pH 0.9-1.6, buffered by dentine; mild pulp response resolving in 10-20 days [SRC 60]. On an exposed pulp, cover with calcium hydroxide or a calcium silicate material first [SRC 63]. Resists plaque; gingival tolerance high [SRC 171]. The aim of a lining is to seal the dentine against bacteria, not to deliver a therapeutic effect through sound dentine [SRC 172].
**(d) Mechanical.** Compressive strength 70-220 MPa by type; flexural 15-20 MPa (composite over 70) [SRC 54] [SRC 56]. ISO 9917 minimum compressive strength 130 MPa (restorative), 70 MPa (luting, lining) [SRC 55]. Brittle; poor shear resistance; low wear resistance [SRC 59] [SRC 173].
**(e) Thermal.** Expansion 10-11 against tooth 11.4, diffusivity close to dentine: good insulation and little stress at the interface [SRC 57].
**(f) Optical.** Translucent; translucency develops over days; dehydration or early water ruins it [SRC 174].
**(g) Solubility and erosion.** Low in lactic acid; vulnerable to acidic fluorides (APF) and fresh cement in acid [SRC 175] [SRC 176]. ISO limit 0.05 mm/h [SRC 55].
**(h) Radiopacity.** Most restorative GICs are more radiopaque than dentine, but some auto-cure Type II.1 cements are radiolucent [SRC 177].

### 7. Modifications and newer materials
1. **Metal-reinforced:** silver cermet (about 40% silver by weight sintered to glass, up to 5% TiO2) and amalgam-alloy admix; better abrasion resistance, no other gain, less ion-exchange [SRC 77] [SRC 78].
2. **Resin-modified:** 15-25% resin (HEMA); sets on light and in the dark; higher strength and immediate finishing; drawbacks are residual HEMA (4.5% to 15%), water uptake, some shrinkage [SRC 80] [SRC 84] [SRC 85] [SRC 86].
3. **Tri-cure:** adds redox chemical initiator [SRC 81].
4. **High-viscosity:** finer glass (about 3 micrometres), higher P/L; packable; ART; not for load-bearing permanent areas [SRC 88] [SRC 89].
5. **Nano-ionomer:** nanomers and nanoclusters in RMGIC for polish and optics; clinical data sparse [SRC 23] [SRC 90].
6. **Glass hybrid:** high-viscosity GIC plus resin coat; 5-year survival in Class II comparable to composite in one RCT [LIT 124].
7. **Not true GIC:** compomer, giomer, alkasite (resin-based) [SRC 22] [SRC 92].
- **Dark-set test** to tell a true GIC from an imitation: sets within about 7-10 min under light-proof cover [SRC 93] [SRC 94].

### 7A. Resin-modified, high-viscosity and delivery variants in more detail
- **RMGIC gains:** set on light, more strength and toughness, early resistance to water, good translucency from the start [SRC 182] [SRC 83].
- **RMGIC losses:** HEMA is hydrophilic, so the cement takes up water and swells in the first 5-7 days and may stain [SRC 85]; residual HEMA is released into dentine [SRC 84]; some powders are sensitive to ambient light and should be dispensed only at the moment of mixing [SRC 183]; it is more cytotoxic than conventional GIC [SRC 64].
- **Auto-cure RMGIC** (resin catalyst, no light needed) is useful where light cannot reach, as in minimal cavities [SRC 184].
- **High-viscosity advantages:** packable, non-sticky, reduced early moisture sensitivity, quicker finishing, better wear resistance, low solubility [SRC 185].
- **Delivery:** capsules remove porosity and standardise the ratio; automixed RMGIC avoids air entrapment; granulated powder mixes in under half the time [SRC 186] [SRC 187]; paste-paste systems (film thickness about 5 micrometres, set in 3 min) are used for luting and orthodontic work but lose some fluoride release and properties [SRC 160].

### 8. Clinical uses and technique
**Uses:**
- Luting: crowns, bridges, bands; reasons are low solubility, fluoride, thixotropic flow; retention comes from the preparation, since cement tensile strength is only 2-4 MPa [SRC 188] [SRC 181].
- Restorative: Class III, Class V (including erosion and abrasion), root caries, deciduous teeth, small posterior lesions not under load [SRC 189] [SRC 75] [SRC 190].
- Lining and base: best long-term seal to dentine; under amalgam and composite [SRC 191] [SRC 192].
- Sealants: partially erupted teeth, caries-active children [SRC 193].
- ART and minimal intervention: sealing the lesion can arrest caries; high-viscosity GIC [SRC 194] [SRC 21].
- Core: only where at least half the crown and a 3 mm sound collar remain [SRC 195].
- Indirect pulp capping and transitional restoration [SRC 196].
**Technique:**
1. Isolate; clean with pumice; condition 10% PAA 10 s; wash; dry lightly [SRC 96].
2. Capsule mix about 10 s; place in the working window [SRC 97] [SRC 98].
3. Matrix and pressure [SRC 100].
4. Seal at once with unfilled light-cured resin (varnish second best) [SRC 101] [SRC 102].
5. Finish at 24 hours or later [SRC 103].
**Cavity design.** Adhesion removes the need for undercuts; minimal tunnels and slots possible [SRC 197]. Class V: butt joint, no bevel, about 1 mm depth [SRC 106]. Leave affected dentine if the margin is sealed [SRC 198].
**Sandwich technique (bi-layered):**
- GIC replaces dentine, composite replaces enamel.
- Rationale: composite shrinks and its dentine bond is uncertain; GIC is water-based and bonds reliably [SRC 108] [SRC 109].
- Steps: place high-P/L GIC (at least 1 mm), cut back to expose enamel margins, etch enamel (and cement 15 s for auto-cure), bond, add composite incrementally [SRC 110] [SRC 199].
- Open sandwich leaves GIC at the gingival margin for fluoride release [SRC 110] [SRC 111].

### 8A. Site-wise cavity design and placement (Mount and Hume system)
- **Occlusal and proximal lesions (Sites 1 and 2):** GIC is the best initial restoration; use the strongest, preferably auto-cure, high-P/L cement and laminate with composite or amalgam if the load is heavy; no sub-lining [SRC 76].
- **Tunnel (occlusal approach):** enter through the fossa medial to the marginal ridge, remove infected dentine only, leave affected axial dentine [SRC 200]; Type II.2 radiopaque capsulated cement in two increments tamped with a plastic sponge under a mylar strip, trimmed at about 5-6 minutes [SRC 201] [SRC 202] [SRC 203].
- **Cervical (Site 3) lesions:** Size 2 gets a Type II.1 cement, auto-cure or resin-modified [SRC 75]; Size 3 (approximal root-surface or recurrent caries) gets a radiopaque, high-strength auto-cure cement, conditioned 10 s, placed in two increments and sealed with low-viscosity resin [SRC 204].
- **Large cavities:** a high-strength GIC base as a thermal barrier with ion exchange; calcium hydroxide sub-lining unnecessary [SRC 205]; if the floor is very near the pulp, a thin high-fluoride auto-cure sub-lining may be added [SRC 206].
- **Undercuts left by caries removal** are filled with GIC rather than cut away [SRC 107].

### 8B. Special uses
- **Luting.** Vital tooth: do not condition dentine with polyacrylic acid because of hydraulic pressure; use a mineralising solution or tannic acid if needed and vent the crown. Non-vital tooth: condition with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10-15 s [SRC 207]. Post cementation: condition the posthole, dry with alcohol, wind the cement in with a Lentulo spiral [SRC 208].
- **Fissure sealant.** Pumice, condition with 10-20% polyacrylic acid for 10 s, dry field, protect with unfilled resin [SRC 209]; best in partially erupted teeth and caries-active individuals [SRC 193]. Resin sealants remain the first choice in many texts because of retention [SRC 210] [Likely].
- **Paediatric.** Many deciduous teeth can be restored with GIC alone [SRC 190]; hypoplastic permanent molars are held with GIC while they erupt [SRC 211]; stainless steel crowns on primary molars are cemented with GIC [SRC 211].
- **ART and high-viscosity GIC:** minimum-preparation restorations with hand instruments [SRC 21].
- **Root caries and high-risk patients:** root lesions need more fluoride than enamel to remineralise [SRC 212]; for xerostomic and high-risk patients, RMGIC is an aesthetic choice for Class V [SRC 213].
- **Indirect pulp capping and transitional restoration:** GIC (or RMGIC over calcium hydroxide) seals residual caries; it is relatively insoluble and releases ions [SRC 214] [SRC 196].
- **Orthodontics:** bands cemented with GIC show better retention and less demineralisation than zinc phosphate [SRC 215].
- **Cervical resorption:** after removal of resorptive tissue the defect is restored with GIC protected by unfilled composite resin [SRC 216].
- **Elderly patients:** repair rather than replace; GIC preferred for its anti-caries value [SRC 217].
- **Therapeutic coating:** a long-lasting protective coat on susceptible surfaces where hygiene is poor [SRC 218].

### 9. Advantages, disadvantages, failure
**Advantages:** chemical adhesion; fluoride release and recharge; biocompatibility; thermal expansion close to tooth; conservative cavity; useful where isolation is difficult [SRC 150] [SRC 105] [SRC 224].
**Disadvantages:** brittle, low tensile and shear strength; low wear resistance; water sensitivity; staining and loss of gloss; slow maturation; not for marginal ridge, incisal corner or heavy load [SRC 59] [SRC 128] [SRC 58].
**Failure causes:** low-powder mix or early water contact (ditching), dehydration (cracks), overload (bulk fracture), contaminated surface (loss in erosion lesions) [SRC 112] [SRC 225].
**Reported clinical performance (textbook-quoted):** Class V GIC without mechanical retention, 80% retention at 10 years [SRC 113]; Class I and Class II failure 8% and 40% at 24 months [SRC 58]; ADA review: 65% lower chance of recurrent caries than amalgam in permanent teeth [SRC 132]; Class V resin-modified GIC retained in 96.4% at 5 years against 51.5% for bonded composite in one study [SRC 226]; in retrospective reports almost half of GIC restorations were replaced for recurrent caries, which shows fluoride release does not remove caries risk [SRC 129]. These figures come from different study designs and should not be merged into one success rate [Likely].
**Special cautions:** xerostomia (Sjogren): auto-cure may disintegrate; acidic topical fluoride degrades GIC [SRC 176] [SRC 53]; irradiated xerostomic patients lose the GIC survival advantage when topical acidic fluoride is used [SRC 114].

### 9A. Repair and maintenance
- A failed GIC is normally replaced after removing all the old material, because the union between old and new GIC is not strong; partial removal with a composite laminate is the alternative [SRC 227]. (One chapter says the opposite for sealants [SRC 228].)
- Minor marginal ditching can be reopened with a fine diamond and repaired with GIC [SRC 229].
- Beneath indirect restorations, a low-solubility luting cement is chosen because dissolution of the lute opens the margin [SRC 230].

### 10. Present evidence (critical appraisal)
1. **Fluoride and secondary caries.** RCT meta-analysis (64 trials): GIC has fewer secondary caries than amalgam (permanent teeth RR 0.20, primary RR 0.55) but not fewer than composite (RR 0.77, 0.92) [LIT 130]. So the benefit is real against amalgam; "almost total prevention" is not supported [Likely].
2. **Longevity and ART.** Review of 14 RCTs: ART with high-viscosity GIC did not differ significantly from traditional restorations in primary teeth and single-surface permanent cavities; only 2 of 14 trials had low risk of bias [LIT 136]. Evidence overall is "conflicting" [LIT 138]. Multi-surface permanent restorations remain unproven [Likely].
3. **Sandwich technique.** Meta-analysis in cervical lesions: lower loss of retention at 3 years, wide CI, 4 small trials [LIT 145]. No trial evidence found here for Class I/II [Likely].
4. **Fluoride release is not the same as clinical benefit.** Laboratory release is highest for GIC [LIT 133] but this has not been shown to be the mechanism of any clinical advantage [Likely].

5. **Conventional GIC against RMGIC.** In the same meta-analysis, secondary caries did not differ significantly between conventional GIC and RMGIC (primary teeth RR 1.12, 0.67-1.87; permanent teeth RR 1.63, 0.34-7.84) [LIT 130]. Neither type is proven better for caries prevention [Likely].

### 11. FUTURE
- **Research aim:** more fracture resistance without losing ion exchange and fluoride release [SRC 115] [SRC 116].
- **Antibacterial and remineralising additives:** chlorhexidine, chitosan, CPP-ACP, silver, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide; promising in vitro, not proven clinically [SRC 117] [LIT 119] [LIT 120].
- **Hydroxyapatite and nano-fillers:** laboratory improvement in strength and fluoride release [LIT 121].
- **Ion-releasing resins (alkasite, bioactive):** composite-like strength, short-term clinical performance comparable to composite and better than GIC [LIT 122] [LIT 123].
- **Glass hybrid and high-viscosity systems:** moving GIC into moderate Class II [LIT 124].
- **Other directions mentioned in the DCNA literature:** amino-acid-modified, non-HEMA GICs and ultrasound-assisted hardening [SRC 231]; bioactive orthodontic RMGIC needing further study [SRC 232]; and self-etch and self-adhesive materials that borrowed from the GIC interface [SRC 233].
- **Likely direction:** bioactivity with composite-level strength [Guessing].

### 11A. Role of GIC in modern (biological, minimal-intervention) restorative dentistry
- **Sealing the lesion rather than removing it all.** Infected dentine is removed, affected dentine may be left if the margin is fully sealed, and the lesion is expected to arrest and remineralise [SRC 198] [SRC 194].
- **Transitional restoration.** A high-P/L GIC over affected dentine releases fluoride, strontium and phosphate ions; in a high-caries-risk mouth it may be kept as the permanent restoration and laminated later [SRC 234].
- **Dentine sealing under other materials.** GIC gives the most effective long-term seal to dentine, so it is used as the base under composite and amalgam [SRC 191].
- **High-risk patients.** Fluoride-releasing materials are described as essential for the high-caries-risk patient [SRC 235].
- **Moisture and technique tolerance.** Useful where isolation is difficult, though durable restorations still need moisture control, with dentine moist but not wet [SRC 224] [SRC 236].
- **Limit of the role.** Minimal-cavity designs apply to new lesions with early dentine involvement; they are a first stage that may later be replaced by a conventional design [SRC 237].

### 12. Conclusion
GIC is the only restorative with a reliable chemical bond to tooth and a fluoride reservoir. Its past was a story of fixing handling (copolymers, tartaric acid, sealing); its present is a family of materials for luting, lining, sealing and low-load restoration; its future is stronger, bioactive materials that must not lose the ion exchange that defines the group [SRC 151] [SRC 115] [Likely]. For an examiner, the three points to leave on the page are the chemistry (acid-base, three-stage set, water balance), the clinical rules that follow from it (condition, seal, delay finishing, choose by load) and the honest limit of the evidence (benefit against amalgam, not proven against composite) [Likely].


## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Wilson and Kent, early 1970s (1972 in most books); ISO name "glass polyalkenoate cement"; first product ASPA.
- Powder = fluoro-alumino-silicate glass; liquid = 40-55% polyacrylic/itaconic copolymer + tartaric acid 5-15% [SRC 25] [SRC 15].
- Setting = dissolution, gelation (Ca/Sr, hard set 4-10 min), maturation (Al, 24 h and beyond); about 24% water [SRC 32] [SRC 33] [SRC 37].

- Fluoride = early burst, low steady level, rechargeable reservoir; benefit shown against amalgam, not against composite [LIT 130].
- Types I (luting, 1.5:1), II.1 aesthetic, II.2 reinforced (3-4:1), III lining/base; only P/L separates lining from base [SRC 70] [SRC 71].
- Seal immediately, finish at 24 h or later [SRC 101] [SRC 103].
- RMGIC has 15-25% resin; compomer, giomer and alkasite are not glass ionomers [SRC 80] [SRC 22].

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Calling compomers, giomers or alkasite materials "glass ionomers"; they are resin-based.
2. Writing "acid-etch the tooth before GIC"; the conditioner is 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds.
3. Leaving the restoration unsealed or finishing it at once, then wondering why it cracked.
4. Writing "fluoride release prevents recurrent caries" without qualification; the RCT evidence shows benefit against amalgam only.
5. Quoting one date for the invention, or a precise "bond strength" figure, without noting that sources disagree on both.

## 1. Definition

**Explanation.** See Q1 section 1. The only point to add here is that "success" in a single-visit question means two things that must be kept apart: short-term comfort (pain, flare-up) and long-term healing or survival.

#### ✎ Exam core
Single visit endodontics is completion of root canal treatment, that is cleaning, shaping, disinfection and obturation of the canal system (and removal of an old filling in retreatment), in one appointment (Baumann and Beer, 2010). It is generally accepted for vital teeth, where only the coronal pulp is superficially infected; its use in necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis is debated (Baumann and Beer, 2010).

---

## 2. Criteria of success

**Explanation.** Outcome can be judged in four dimensions: the physical (health or disease, pain, function), tooth survival, the economic, and the patient's quality of life (Berman et al, 2021) [SRC]. The definitive measure, with absence of signs and symptoms, is periapical healing, because treatment aims to resolve periapical disease (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019) [SRC]. Friedman and Mor regarded "success" and "failure" as ambiguous and proposed healed, healing and disease; the AAE terms are healed, healing and non-healed, and "functional retention" means a symptom-free tooth kept in function regardless of a radiolucency (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019) [SRC]. Most lesions heal within a year, but healing can continue for up to 4 years or longer (Berman et al, 2021) [SRC], which is why a single-visit outcome cannot be judged at the next appointment.

#### ✎ Exam core
**Clinical:** no pain; no swelling, mobility or sinus tract (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
**Radiographic:** normal periodontal ligament width, continuous lamina dura, recovered cancellous bone, no root resorption (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

**AAE outcome categories** (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019):
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Healed | Functional, asymptomatic, minimal or no periradicular pathosis |
| Healing | Asymptomatic and functional with a reducing lesion, or symptomatic with unaffected function |
| Non-healed | Nonfunctional, symptomatic, with or without a lesion |
| Functional retention | Symptom-free tooth in function regardless of a radiolucency |

Outcome has four dimensions: health or disease with pain and function, tooth survival, economics, and quality of life (Berman et al, 2021). Lesions mostly heal within 1 year but may take 4 years or more; success is established only by long-term follow-up (Berman et al, 2021; Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

## 3. Success of single visit endodontics

**Explanation.** The figures come in two kinds. Single-arm series show what one visit achieves alone: Pekruhn's 925 teeth reached 95% overall success at one year, with failure higher in retreatments and teeth with apical periodontitis; Field et al reported 89.2% in 223 teeth, anterior teeth doing better than posterior (Sikri, 2019) [SRC]. Comparative evidence answers whether one visit does worse than two. The 2022 Cochrane review found no difference in radiological failure or extraction, but more patients reporting pain within the first week after one visit (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT]. For orientation, nonsurgical treatment and retreatment overall succeed in about 85% of cases, rising above 90% when judged by functional retention (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
| Study | Teeth | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Pekruhn (1986) | 925, one operator, 1 year | 95% success; failures higher in retreatment and apical periodontitis |
| Field et al (2004) | 223 | 89.2% success; anteriors better than posteriors |
| Trope (1991) | 226 | No flare-ups without preoperative symptoms |
| Peters and Wesselink (2002); Molander et al (2007); Paredes-Vieyra and Enriquez (2012) | Randomised comparisons | Healing equal to two visits |

*(Sikri, 2019)*

- Systematic reviews: no significant difference in healing between one and two visits (Baumann and Beer, 2010); Cochrane 2022, radiological failure RR 0.93, moderate certainty (Mergoni et al, 2022).
- Vital pulpectomy, often done in one visit, succeeds in more than 94% (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

## 4. Factors affecting success

**Explanation.** Ingle's groups the factors into treatment, patient and practitioner factors: correct diagnosis and plan, anatomical complexity, severity of disease, host response and the operator's experience (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019) [SRC]. For single visit the two that dominate are preoperative pulp-periapical status and case selection (Q1 section 4).

#### ✎ Exam core
1. **Pulp and periapical status:** vital teeth do best; failure is higher with apical periodontitis and in retreatment (Sikri, 2019).
2. **Tooth type and anatomy:** anterior and single-rooted teeth do better (Sikri, 2019; Weine, 2004).
3. **Operator experience:** the most important single factor (Gopikrishna, 2021).
4. **Quality of disinfection:** bacteria remaining at obturation lower healing, 68% versus 94% (Sjögren et al 1997, in Baumann and Beer, 2010).
5. **Apical control:** no overinstrumentation or extrusion (Weine, 2004).
6. **Coronal seal:** immediate core build-up prevents reinfection (Gopikrishna, 2021).
7. **Host response** (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

## 5. Failures of single visit endodontics

**Explanation.** Failures split into early (flare-up, postoperative pain) and late (non-healing lesion). The early failures are the ones peculiar to one visit, because the canal is already sealed. Grossman defines a flare-up as an acute exacerbation after starting or continuing treatment, gives an incidence of 1.4-19%, and lists preoperative pain, abscess, overinstrumentation, step-back shaping and hypochlorite extrusion as causes (Gopikrishna, 2021) [SRC]. Late failure reflects residual infection: above a threshold, obturation cannot entomb surviving bacteria (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
**A. Early failure: flare-up and pain.**
- Flare-up: acute exacerbation of an asymptomatic pulp or periapical pathosis after treatment starts; incidence 1.4-19% (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Causes: overinstrumentation, extrusion of debris or irrigant; preoperative pain, swelling or abscess; retreatment (Gopikrishna, 2021; Sikri, 2019).
- Single visit: more patients with pain within one week (RR 1.55), but no difference in flare-up (Mergoni et al, 2022).

**B. Late failure: persistent or new periapical lesion.**
- Residual bacteria not entombed by the filling (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022).
- Missed canals and complex anatomy: one visit gives no chance to re-evaluate additional canals (Baumann and Beer, 2010).
- Coronal leakage before the final restoration (Gopikrishna, 2021).
- Extruded filling material can delay healing (Berman et al, 2021).

**C. Failures of case selection.** Weeping canals, symptomatic teeth or retreatments forced into one visit (Baumann and Beer, 2010; Gopikrishna, 2021).

---

## 6. Management of failures

**Explanation.** A flare-up is managed symptomatically (Q1 section 6A). A non-healed tooth with a persisting or enlarging lesion and symptoms needs nonsurgical or surgical retreatment or extraction, chosen on local and general factors (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019) [SRC].

#### ✎ Exam core
- **Flare-up:** NSAIDs with acetaminophen, long-acting local anaesthetic, occlusal relief, anxiety reduction (Gopikrishna, 2021); antibiotics only with systemic signs (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019); do not leave the tooth open (Berman et al, 2021).
- **Overinstrumentation found during the visit:** do not complete; place a corticosteroid-antibiotic dressing and finish later (Weine, 2004).
- **Non-healed tooth:** nonsurgical or surgical retreatment, or extraction (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).
- **Persisting asymptomatic radiolucency:** continue observation (Rotstein and Ingle, 2019).

**Success and failure compared with multiple visits.**
| Outcome | Single visit vs multiple visits |
|---|---|
| Radiographic healing | No difference (RR 0.93) (Mergoni et al, 2022) |
| Tooth extraction | No difference; very low certainty (Mergoni et al, 2022) |
| Pain within one week | More patients after single visit (RR 1.55) (Mergoni et al, 2022) |
| Flare-up | No difference in pooled trials (Mergoni et al, 2022) |
| Necrotic teeth with lesions | Some studies 10-20% better with two visits (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) |
| Managing a flare-up | Harder after one visit: canal already filled (Baumann and Beer, 2010) |

**Conclusion.** With good case selection, single visit gives success equal to multiple visits; its typical failure is short-term pain, not poorer healing (Mergoni et al, 2022; Sikri, 2019).

## CRITICAL EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS
**Claim: "Single visit fails more often than multiple visits."** Textbook series report 89-95% single-visit success (Sikri, 2019) [SRC], and the 2022 Cochrane review finds no difference in radiological failure (RR 0.93, moderate certainty) but more first-week pain after one visit (Mergoni et al, 2022) [LIT]. Siqueira and Rôças counter that the trials are small and of low-to-medium quality, and that some studies show 10-20% better outcomes with two visits (Siqueira and Rôças, 2022) [SRC]. The single-arm series lack a control group and the trials lack power, so the evidence shows no detected difference rather than proven equivalence [Certain]. Clinically, one visit is not a higher-failure option for well-selected cases; failure risk is driven by preoperative periapical status and retreatment [Likely].

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
1. Single-visit success: 95% (Pekruhn), 89.2% (Field).
2. Cochrane 2022: radiological failure RR 0.93; first-week pain RR 1.55.
3. AAE outcomes: healed, healing, non-healed; plus functional retention.
4. Healing may take up to 4 years.
5. Flare-up incidence 1.4-19%.
6. Non-healed: retreat (nonsurgical or surgical) or extract.

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Judging success at one week: postoperative pain is a short-term outcome, not failure of healing.
2. Quoting single-arm success rates as proof that one visit beats two.
3. Using only "success/failure" without the AAE healed/healing/non-healed terms.
4. Listing failures without separating early (flare-up) from late (persistent lesion).

## STUDY MODE

### Marginal integrity, restated
Marginal integrity is the continuity of the seal at the margin. A cast or bonded restoration seats against the tooth with a residual gap, and any gap that bacteria or fluid can cross is a leakage route (Sikri, 2020, p.435; see Q2). Margin design is the part of cavity and crown preparation that the operator controls most directly, so it is where the seal is built.

### Circumferential tie
The circumferential tie is the peripheral marginal anatomy of the preparation, created by bevels along the cavosurface margins. Its job is to reduce the error between the cast and the tooth at the margin. An accurate wax pattern and casting do not guarantee a well-adapted margin, so the margin is shaped to support the casting (Sikri, 2020, p.287). The enamel must be supported by sound dentin, and enamel rods at the margin should be continuous with sound dentin (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- A **bevel** reduces the marginal error compared with the internal dimensions, depending on its angle. A bevel also indirectly helps retention, because it reduces the frictional component between tooth and casting (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- Types: **partial bevel** (part of the enamel wall, for direct gold); **short bevel** (whole enamel wall, preferred for cast gold); **inverted bevel** (on the labial shoulder of metal-ceramic crowns, for aesthetics); **reverse bevel** (at the dentinal cervical wall, resisting rotation of a casting with flat gingival walls during cementation) (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- A **secondary flare** is a flat plane added to the primary flare. In gold inlays it works as an occlusal-type bevel, using the malleability of gold to adapt the margin to the proximal wall (Sikri, 2020, p.288).

### Finish lines (margin designs)
Three margin designs are commonly used in cast restoration: bevel, chamfer and shoulder. A chamfer is less than 0.5 mm wide and rounded; a shoulder is more than 0.5 mm wide and sharp. A chamfer suits malleable metal and a shoulder suits metal that is not malleable (Sikri, 2020, p.286).

**Table 16.2 summary** (Sikri, 2020, p.287):
- *Bevel:* removes unsupported enamel and allows finishing of the metal margin; can extend into the sulcus if used apically. Indicated for the facial margin of metal-ceramic crowns and intracoronal cast restorations.
- *Chamfer:* a distinct margin, easy to control, but needs care to avoid an unsupported enamel lip. Indicated for lingual margins of cast metal and metal-ceramic crowns.
- *Shoulder:* gives bulk of restorative material; less conservative and difficult in mandibular incisors. Indicated for labial margins of metal-ceramic crowns.

### Marginal integrity and the bevel
Occlusal bevelling of an inlay reduces the microgap: a non-beveled inlay shows a significant gap, a beveled one virtually none (Sikri, 2020, p.286–287, Fig 16.11). The gingival-wall cervical bevel is placed with a flame-shaped bur or gingival marginal trimmer, and for direct wax patterns it is made wider across the cervical floor (Sikri, 2020, p.286).

### Restorations: what changes by type
- **Cast gold inlay:** short bevel on the enamel wall; secondary flare for the proximal wall; burnish the margin. Gaps over 0.1 mm mean remaking (Sikri, 2020, p.287–288; Sikri, 2020, p.444 for burnishing).
- **Metal-ceramic crown:** inverted bevel or shoulder on the labial margin for aesthetics; chamfer lingually (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- **Direct composite Class II:** bevels only on accessible facial and lingual proximal margins; occlusal bevel not required; cervical bevel disadvantage in extensive cavities because it removes remaining enamel (Sikri, 2020, p.441). Finish after 24 hours (Sikri, 2020, p.442).
- **Amalgam and porcelain:** not yet read for this answer [pending].

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** Marginal integrity depends on the seal at the margin, which the operator builds through margin design. Three linked ideas are the circumferential tie, the bevel and the finish line.

**1. Marginal integrity.** The margin always has some gap. What counts clinically is whether it admits bacteria and fluid. Margin design controls how big that gap is, how well the restoration seats, and how well it can be finished (Sikri, 2020, p.435, 287).

**2. Circumferential tie.** This is the peripheral marginal anatomy created by bevels along cavosurface margins. It reduces the gap between casting and tooth by a factor set by the bevel angle, and it supports the casting (Sikri, 2020, p.287). Enamel must be backed by sound dentin.

**3. Bevel types and roles.** Partial bevel for direct gold; short bevel for cast gold; inverted bevel on the labial metal-ceramic shoulder for aesthetics; reverse bevel at the cervical dentin wall to resist rotation during cementation (Sikri, 2020, p.287). The secondary flare in gold inlays is an occlusal-type bevel that uses gold's malleability (Sikri, 2020, p.288).

**4. Finish lines.**
- *Bevel:* removes unsupported enamel and allows finishing. Indicated for facial metal-ceramic margins and intracoronal cast restorations.
- *Chamfer:* under 0.5 mm, rounded, easy to control, needs care to avoid an enamel lip. Indicated for lingual margins of cast metal and metal-ceramic crowns.
- *Shoulder:* over 0.5 mm, sharp, gives bulk; less conservative and hard in mandibular incisors. Indicated for labial metal-ceramic margins (Sikri, 2020, p.286–287).
- Metal malleability decides the choice: chamfer for malleable metal, shoulder for non-malleable metal (Sikri, 2020, p.286).

**5. Restorations by type.**
- *Cast gold inlay:* short bevel; secondary flare proximally; burnish the margin. Gaps over 0.1 mm mean remaking (Sikri, 2020, p.287–288, 444).
- *Metal-ceramic crown:* chamfer lingually, inverted bevel or shoulder labially (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- *Direct composite Class II:* bevels on accessible facial and lingual proximal margins, not occlusal. Extensive cervical bevelling can remove remaining enamel (Sikri, 2020, p.441). Finish after 24 hours (Sikri, 2020, p.442).
- *Amalgam and porcelain:* to be completed after reading the outstanding sources.

**6. Interpretation.** Margin design is the operator's main tool for marginal integrity. The bevel is clearest where it supports cast metal and enamel; for bonded composite its effect is contested. The finish line should suit the material and be finished so that the gap stays closed.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Circumferential tie = peripheral marginal anatomy from bevels; reduces casting–tooth error (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- Bevel types: partial (direct gold), short (cast gold), inverted (metal-ceramic labial), reverse (cervical dentin) (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- Chamfer < 0.5 mm, rounded; shoulder > 0.5 mm, sharp (Sikri, 2020, p.286).
- Chamfer for malleable metal; shoulder for non-malleable (Sikri, 2020, p.286).
- Table 16.2: bevel, chamfer, shoulder — advantages, disadvantages, indications (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- Occlusal bevel removes microgap on inlays (Sikri, 2020, p.286–287).
- Cast gap > 0.1 mm: remake (Sikri, 2020, p.444).
- Composite bevel role controversial (Sikri, 2020, p.441).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Defining chamfer and shoulder by appearance alone. Give the width threshold (0.5 mm) and the metal reason.
2. Saying bevels always improve the seal. The composite bevel role is controversial (Sikri, 2020, p.441).
3. Forgetting the reverse bevel and its function against rotation.
4. Mixing up the secondary flare (proximal, gold) with the primary flare (axial).
5. Writing about finish lines without linking them to the material (malleable vs non-malleable).

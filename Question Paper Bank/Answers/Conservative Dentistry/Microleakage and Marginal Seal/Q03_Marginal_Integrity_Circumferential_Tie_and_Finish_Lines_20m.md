## STUDY MODE

### Marginal integrity, restated
Marginal integrity is the continuity of the seal at the margin. Summitt's gives a useful clinical frame: a completely closed margin is unattainable. Bacteria pass under every margin, so patient resistance matters as much as the margin opening. The practical criterion is the smallest detectable ledge, about 100 µm (Summitt's, PDF p.956).

### Circumferential tie
The circumferential tie is the peripheral marginal anatomy of the preparation, created by bevels along the cavosurface margins. Its job is to reduce the error between the casting and the tooth at the margin. An accurate wax pattern and casting do not guarantee a well-adapted margin, so the margin is shaped to support the casting (Sikri, 2020, p.287). The enamel must be backed by sound dentin, and enamel rods at the margin should be continuous with sound dentin (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- A bevel reduces the marginal error compared with the internal dimensions, depending on its angle. A bevel also helps retention indirectly, by reducing the frictional component between tooth and casting (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- Types: partial bevel (part of the enamel wall, for direct gold); short bevel (the whole enamel wall, preferred for cast gold); inverted bevel (labial shoulder of metal-ceramic crowns, for aesthetics); reverse bevel (dentinal cervical wall, resisting rotation of a casting with flat gingival walls during cementation) (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- A secondary flare is a flat plane added to the primary flare. In gold inlays it works as an occlusal-type bevel, using gold's malleability (Sikri, 2020, p.288).

### Finish lines
Sikri names three margins for cast restoration: bevel, chamfer and shoulder. A chamfer is under 0.5 mm wide and rounded; a shoulder is over 0.5 mm and sharp. Chamfer suits malleable metal and shoulder suits non-malleable metal (Sikri, 2020, p.286). Table 16.2 gives advantages, disadvantages and indications (Sikri, 2020, p.287):
- *Bevel:* removes unsupported enamel and allows finishing; can extend into the sulcus if placed apically. Indicated for facial metal-ceramic margins and intracoronal cast restorations.
- *Chamfer:* a distinct margin and easy to control, but needs care to avoid an unsupported enamel lip. Indicated for lingual margins of cast and metal-ceramic restorations.
- *Shoulder:* gives bulk; less conservative; difficult in mandibular incisors. Indicated for labial metal-ceramic margins.

For ceramic crowns, Summitt's gives the most detailed finish-line data. In the metal-ceramic context, margin design traditionally has little effect on seal, and shoulder, shoulder-bevel and chamfer all give acceptable fit when fully seated. Two studies found the shoulder had the least discrepancy after cementation, better than a shoulder-bevel or chamfer. Stress analysis found chamfer and internally rounded shoulder had the lowest concentration under vertical load. A feather-edge produced crowns with more than twice the marginal opening of a chamfer or rounded shoulder (Summitt's, PDF pp.956–957).

### Bevels in direct restorations
- **Class V:** Summitt's says cavosurface bevels are contraindicated in amalgam and GIC preparations because of their low edge strength, and recommends a 90° butt joint about 1 mm deep for GIC (Summitt's, PDF p.801).
- **Class II composite:** Summitt's places 0.5 mm bevels at about 45° on facial and lingual proximal margins that meet the tooth at a right angle, and cites research that bevels significantly reduce leakage (Summitt's, PDF p.594). Gingival bevels only where enamel is well above the CEJ. Within about 1 mm of the CEJ, bonding is no better than bonding to dentin (Summitt's, PDF p.594). An inverse (internal) bevel at the gingival margin significantly reduces microleakage against a butt margin, and is preferred to a gingival margin on or near the CEJ (Summitt's, PDF p.595). Occlusal bevels are not indicated (Summitt's, PDF p.595; Sturdevant's, PDF p.591).
- **Sturdevant's** agrees: no occlusal bevels, bevels on proximal facial and lingual margins only when extra retention is needed, and usually none on the gingival margin (Sturdevant's, PDF pp.591–592). Sturdevant's also says that extending the enamel cavosurface bevel improves the peripheral enamel seal (PDF p.259).
- **Sikri** calls the bevel's role in composite controversial (Sikri, 2020, p.441).

### Restorations by type
- **Cast gold inlay:** short bevel on the enamel wall; secondary flare proximally; burnish the margin. Gaps over 0.1 mm mean remaking (Sikri, 2020, pp.287–288, 444).
- **Metal-ceramic crown:** inverted bevel or shoulder labially for aesthetics; chamfer lingually (Sikri, 2020, p.287). Summitt's: shoulder 90°, 1.0–1.5 mm deep (PDF p.957).
- **Ceramic crown:** rounded shoulder or chamfer; avoid the feather-edge (Summitt's, PDF pp.956–957).
- **Direct Class V:** 90° butt joint for amalgam and GIC; bevels only for resin (Summitt's, PDF p.801).
- **Direct Class II composite:** proximal bevels as above; no occlusal bevel; inverse bevel at the gingival margin where enamel allows (Summitt's, PDF pp.594–595).

### Literature on bevels
- **Ameri et al (2010, abstract):** an enamel occlusal bevel gave no benefit on the gingival margin of a loaded Class V composite. Non-beveled cavities leaked significantly less (P = 0.001) [LIT].
- **Bagheri & Ghavamnasiri (2008, abstract):** enamel bevel had no effect on leakage for hybrid or microfilled composite in Class V [LIT].
- **Peixoto et al (2002, abstract):** for indirect composite inlays, bevel and chamfer gave less leakage than a butt joint at the mandibular buccal margin. Chamfer performed worst in maxillary premolars. Leakage occurred mainly at the tooth–cement interface [LIT].
- **Uno et al (1997, abstract):** with RMGI and composite Class V restorations, a bevelled enamel margin and a V-shaped cavity leaked less than a semicircular 90° cavity with no bevel [LIT].

### Critical evidence synthesis
**Claim A: "Bevels reduce marginal leakage."** Textbook and Summitt's say yes, Sikri says controversial for composite, and Sturdevant's says a bevel helps the enamel seal. Primary data are mixed. Ameri (2010) and Bagheri (2008) find no benefit in Class V composite. Peixoto (2002) and Uno (1997) find benefit in other designs or materials.
- EVIDENCE: null in two Class V composite studies, positive in an inlay study and an RMGI Class V study.
- INTERPRETATION: a bevel's effect depends on material, margin position and loading. It is not a general rule for composite.
- LIMITATION: abstracts only; small samples; different dyes and cycling protocols.

**Claim B: "Finish-line design decides ceramic crown fit."** Summitt's presents a traditional view that metal-ceramic margin design has little effect on seal, while the feather-edge result and stress data favour a rounded shoulder or chamfer (PDF pp.956–957). No primary study on this topic was read in this session.
- LIMITATION: the ceramic-crown comparison is textbook-only, so it is labelled [Likely], not settled.

**Disagreement preserved:** Summitt's says bevels reduce composite leakage, Sikri says the role is controversial, and two Class V primary studies find no effect. Summitt's also contraindicates bevels for GIC and amalgam, which is a different claim from the composite one.

## EXAM MODE

**Introduction.** Marginal integrity depends on the seal at the margin, which the operator builds through margin design. The circumferential tie, the bevel and the finish line are the linked concepts.

**1. Marginal integrity.** A closed margin is unattainable, and bacteria pass every margin. The practical measure is whether the gap is small enough for the patient to floss and maintain the restoration. A 100 µm ledge is the smallest detectable (Summitt's, PDF p.956; Sikri, 2020, p.435).

**2. Circumferential tie.** The peripheral marginal anatomy created by cavosurface bevels. It reduces the gap between casting and tooth by an amount set by the bevel angle, and it supports the casting (Sikri, 2020, p.287). Enamel must be backed by sound dentin.

**3. Bevel types and roles.** Partial bevel (direct gold); short bevel (cast gold); inverted bevel on the labial metal-ceramic shoulder; reverse bevel at the cervical dentin wall, resisting rotation during cementation (Sikri, 2020, p.287). The secondary flare in gold inlays is an occlusal-type bevel (Sikri, 2020, p.288).

**4. Finish lines.**
- *Bevel:* removes unsupported enamel and allows finishing. Indicated for facial metal-ceramic margins and intracoronal cast restorations.
- *Chamfer:* under 0.5 mm, rounded; easy to control; needs care to avoid an enamel lip. Lingual metal and metal-ceramic margins.
- *Shoulder:* over 0.5 mm, sharp; more bulk; less conservative. Labial metal-ceramic margins. A 90° shoulder 1.0–1.5 mm deep gives a precise margin for metal-ceramic and most all-ceramic systems (Sikri, 2020, pp.286–287; Summitt's, PDF p.957).
- Metal malleability decides the choice: chamfer for malleable metal, shoulder for non-malleable (Sikri, 2020, p.286).
- Ceramic: a feather-edge gives more than twice the marginal opening of a chamfer or rounded shoulder (Summitt's, PDF p.957).

**5. Bevels by restoration.**
- *Cast gold inlay:* short bevel; secondary flare proximally; burnish the margin. Gaps over 0.1 mm mean remaking (Sikri, 2020, pp.287–288, 444).
- *Metal-ceramic crown:* inverted bevel or shoulder labially; chamfer lingually (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- *Class V:* bevels contraindicated for amalgam and GIC, which need a 90° butt joint about 1 mm deep (Summitt's, PDF p.801).
- *Class II composite:* 0.5 mm proximal facial and lingual bevels on right-angle margins; inverse bevel at the gingival margin where enamel allows; no occlusal bevel (Summitt's, PDF pp.594–595; Sturdevant's, PDF pp.591–592).
- *Ceramic inlay:* bevel or chamfer; butt-joint mandibular premolars leaked more than bevel in one study (Peixoto, 2002 [LIT]).

**6. Interpretation.** Margin design is the operator's main tool for marginal integrity. Bevels help where they support metal or expose enamel rods, and they are contraindicated where the material is brittle. For bonded composite the bevel's benefit is contested in the primary data.

## HIGH-YIELD POINTS
- Circumferential tie = peripheral marginal anatomy from bevels; reduces casting–tooth error (Sikri, 2020, p.287).
- Chamfer < 0.5 mm, rounded; shoulder > 0.5 mm, sharp (Sikri, 2020, p.286).
- Feather-edge: twice the marginal opening (Summitt's, PDF p.957).
- Bevels contraindicated for amalgam and GIC; 0.5 mm proximal bevels for composite (Summitt's, PDF pp.801, 594).
- No occlusal bevels for composite (Summitt's, PDF p.595; Sturdevant's, PDF p.591).
- Composite bevel benefit: contested (Ameri 2010; Bagheri 2008 [LIT]).
- Cast gap over 0.1 mm: remake (Sikri, 2020, p.444).

## COMMON EXAM MISTAKES
1. Defining chamfer and shoulder by appearance alone. Give the 0.5 mm threshold and the metal reason.
2. Saying bevels always improve the seal. The composite benefit is contested.
3. Recommending bevels for amalgam or GIC. Summitt's contraindicates them.
4. Mixing up the secondary flare (proximal, gold) with the primary flare (axial).
5. Writing finish-line rules without the material.
